Lecture 2: What the model assumes and how residuals reveal it

By the end of Lecture 2, you can

  • explain what the main assumptions of a regression are needed for,
  • check the main assumptions of a regression with residual plots,
  • say which assumptions need knowledge of the data rather than plots,
  • find outliers and influential observations, and check with a sensitivity analysis how much they change the results.

Driving question

R will always give you a regression line, also when the line is misleading. One unusual deal, a curved pattern, or a hidden factor can change your conclusion. Before you base a decision on a fitted line, you check what the model assumes and whether these assumptions are plausible.

The fitted line from Lecture 1 looks convincing. Under what conditions should we trust it, and what do its residuals reveal?

Lecture material

This section states what the regression model assumes and what each assumption is needed for. It then goes through the assumptions one at a time. For three of them you learn to read a plot of the residuals, and for the other two you learn which questions about the data to ask instead. The last part looks at single deals that move the line, and at how to check how much the results depend on them.

Start with a founder’s question

Shark Tank data Lecture 1 gave you a number: each extra dollar of claimed valuation goes with about 65 cents more agreed valuation, on average. Before you plan a negotiation around this number, ask whether you can trust the line behind it. R draws a line through any data, also through data that a straight line describes badly, and it gives no warning.

In QM Basic Lecture 1 you saw that a few very large offers pull the mean up, while the median stays put. A fitted line is a line of averages, so a few very large deals can pull it in the same way. This lecture first lists what the model assumes. It then checks each assumption in turn, mostly with plots of the residuals, and ends with a search for the few deals that move the line most. Lecture 3 then builds on this check when it measures how precise the slope is.

Definition

Residual plot (residual-versus-fitted plot). A scatterplot with the fitted values \(\widehat Y_i\) on the horizontal axis and the residuals \(e_i\) on the vertical axis. It shows how the residuals change from the left to the right of the plot.

In Figure 2.1, both axes are in the units of the outcome, $1 million. A point above zero is a deal that was agreed at a higher valuation than its fitted value. A point below zero is a deal that was agreed at a lower valuation than its fitted value. To read patterns in this plot, you first need to know what the model assumes.

What each assumption supports

The model of Lecture 1 is

\[ Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+\varepsilon_i. \]

Least squares gives a line for any data. Whether the numbers that come with the line are reliable depends on a few assumptions. A founder who plans with the 65 cents wants to know two things. Is the number right on average? And how far from the truth could it be? The two answers need different assumptions, so it helps to know which assumption supports which answer.

Definition

Model assumptions. Conditions on the data and on the error terms \(\varepsilon_i\) under which the estimates, standard errors, tests, and intervals of least squares are reliable.

The assumptions are about the error terms \(\varepsilon_i\) of the model. We do not have the error terms in the data, because they depend on the population line. The plots in this lecture therefore use the residuals \(e_i\), which are our estimates of the error terms (Lecture 1). The table lists each assumption and says what goes wrong if it fails. Its third column says how to check the assumption and what the check looks like when the assumption holds and when it fails. The first three rows can be checked with plots of the residuals. The last two follow from how the data were collected and produced.

Assumption What it says in plain words How to check it What goes wrong if it fails
Linearity The average of \(Y\) at each value of \(X\) lies on a straight line: \(E[Y\mid X=x]=\beta_0+\beta_1x\) Residual plot. Holds: the residuals scatter around zero in every part of the range of fitted values. Fails: the residuals follow a curve, for example below zero at both ends and above zero in the middle One slope misses part of the pattern, and the line is wrong in some parts of the range
Homoskedasticity (constant variance) The errors have the same variance at every value of \(X\): \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i\mid X_i)=\sigma^2\) Residual plot. Holds: the vertical spread of the residuals is about the same from left to right, an even band. Fails: the vertical spread grows or shrinks along the fitted values, like a funnel The slope stays unbiased, but the usual standard errors, tests, and intervals are wrong
Normality of the errors At each value of \(X\), the errors follow a normal distribution Normal Q-Q plot of the standardised residuals. Holds: the points lie close to the straight reference line. Fails: the points bend away from the line, usually at one or both ends In small samples the t and F distributions of Lecture 3 are no longer exact. In large samples this matters little
Independence (random sampling) The observations are drawn independently: the error of one row says nothing about the error of another row No plot. Check how the data were collected. Holds: each row was drawn separately, for example customers drawn at random from a long list. Fails: rows share something that affects the outcome, such as the same shop, week, or episode The usual standard errors are often too small, so intervals are too narrow. A sample that is not random can also bias the line
Zero conditional mean (exogeneity): no omitted confounder The error averages zero at every value of \(X\): \(E[\varepsilon_i\mid X_i]=0\). For a causal reading: no other cause of \(Y\) that is missing from the model is related to \(X\) No plot. Check how the values of \(X\) came about. Holds: \(X\) is unrelated to other causes of \(Y\), for example because \(X\) was set at random. Fails: another cause of \(Y\) that is missing from the model is related to \(X\) The slope is not the effect of changing \(X\). It still describes how average outcomes differ across values of \(X\)

The rows support different conclusions. Linearity, independent observations, and zero conditional mean together make the estimated slope \(\hat\beta_1\) unbiased: across many samples, it is correct on average. Homoskedasticity and independence are needed for the usual standard errors, and so for the t-tests and intervals of Lecture 3. Normality of the errors makes these tests and intervals exact in small samples. In large samples they hold approximately without it, because of the central limit theorem.

No row of the table says that the data are normal. This answers the prediction question from before class: the outcome does not need to be normally distributed, and neither does the predictor. The agreed valuations are strongly right-skewed, and this skew alone does not break any assumption. The assumptions are about the average of \(Y\) at each \(X\), about the errors, and about how the data were produced. They are not about the histogram of \(Y\) or the histogram of \(X\).

Checking the assumptions one at a time

The table gives a short version of each check. This section goes through the five assumptions in the order of the table. For each of the first three you first see a clear invented example in which the assumption holds and one in which it fails. You then read the same kind of plot for the Shark Tank model. For independence and zero conditional mean you see why the residuals cannot show them, and which questions about the data you ask instead.

Linearity

Linearity does not say that individual outcomes lie on a line. It says that the average outcome at each value of the predictor follows a line. Think of a founder who regresses weekly orders on weekly advertising spending. If each extra €100 of advertising brings about the same number of extra orders at low and at high spending, the average follows a line. If extra advertising brings fewer and fewer extra orders, the average follows a curve that flattens out, and one straight line cannot describe it. Figure 2.2 shows both situations with invented data for 60 weeks. We chose the true average in each column, so we know whether linearity holds.

See the R code for this figure 
library(ggplot2)
set.seed(802)
n_w <- 60
ad <- runif(n_w, 0, 10)                               # advertising, in €100
orders_lin <- 40 + 3 * ad + rnorm(n_w, 0, 3)          # straight-line average
orders_cur <- 40 + 14 * log1p(ad) + rnorm(n_w, 0, 1.5)  # average flattens
m_lin <- lm(orders_lin ~ ad)
m_cur <- lm(orders_cur ~ ad)

col_names <- c("Linearity holds", "Linearity fails")
data_df <- data.frame(
  case = factor(rep(col_names, each = n_w), levels = col_names),
  x = c(ad, ad), y = c(orders_lin, orders_cur),
  fit = c(fitted(m_lin), fitted(m_cur)),
  res = c(residuals(m_lin), residuals(m_cur))
)
plot_df <- data.frame(
  case = rep(data_df$case, 2),
  row = rep(c("Orders against advertising",
              "Residuals against fitted values"), each = 2 * n_w),
  x = c(data_df$x, data_df$fit),
  y = c(data_df$y, data_df$res),
  line = c(data_df$fit, rep(NA, 2 * n_w))
)
plot_df$panel <- factor(
  paste(plot_df$case, ifelse(plot_df$row == "Orders against advertising",
                             "(data and line)", "(residuals)")),
  levels = c("Linearity holds (data and line)", "Linearity fails (data and line)",
             "Linearity holds (residuals)", "Linearity fails (residuals)"))
zero_df <- data.frame(
  panel = factor(c("Linearity holds (residuals)", "Linearity fails (residuals)"),
                 levels = levels(plot_df$panel)),
  yintercept = 0
)

ggplot(plot_df, aes(x = x, y = y)) +
  geom_hline(data = zero_df, aes(yintercept = yintercept),
             colour = "#C98B2E", linetype = "dashed", linewidth = 0.8) +
  geom_line(data = subset(plot_df, !is.na(line)), aes(y = line),
            colour = "#D55E00", linewidth = 0.9) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.55, size = 1.8, colour = "#009E73") +
  facet_wrap(~ panel, scales = "free") +
  labs(x = "Advertising in €100 (top) or fitted orders (bottom)",
       y = "Orders (top) or residual (bottom)") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 13) +
  theme(
    panel.grid.minor = element_blank(),
    panel.grid.major.x = element_blank(),
    axis.title = element_text(colour = "#17324D"),
    strip.text = element_text(colour = "#17324D", face = "bold")
  )

Read the figure column by column. In the left column the orders scatter evenly around the straight line. Its residuals form a band around zero, with points above and below zero in every part of the plot. This is what a residual plot looks like when linearity holds. In the right column the gain from advertising flattens out at high spending. The straight line lies above the data at low and at high spending, and below the data in between. So the residuals are negative at both ends and positive in the middle, an arch. A curve like this in a residual plot, an arch or a U, shows that linearity fails. Here one constant slope understates the gain from advertising at low spending and overstates it at high spending. Lecture 6 shows how a curved term in the model captures such a pattern.

Shark Tank data Now read Figure 2.1 for linearity. Ignore the spread for a moment and look at where the middle of the cloud lies. In every part of the range there are residuals above and below zero, and the cloud shows no arch or U. A short calculation makes this reading precise. The code sorts the deals by the claimed valuation into five groups, each with about a fifth of the deals. It then prints the average residual in each group:

fifth <- cut(vals$claimed_m,
             breaks = quantile(vals$claimed_m, probs = seq(0, 1, 0.2)),
             include.lowest = TRUE)
round(tapply(residuals(val_model), fifth, mean), 2)   # average residual
[0.04,0.625] (0.625,1.33]   (1.33,2.5]      (2.5,5]       (5,50] 
        0.10         0.06         0.12        -0.26        -0.08

The labels above the numbers are the ranges of claimed valuations, in $ millions. The groups hold between 146 and 206 deals, not exactly 175, because many founders claim the same round amounts, such as $1 million. The output gives the average residuals, also in $ millions: 0.10, 0.06, 0.12, \(-0.26\), and \(-0.08\). They do not form an arch or a U, and they are small next to the misses for single deals, which reach several million dollars. So the plot agrees with linearity.

Homoskedasticity (constant variance)

Homoskedasticity is about the vertical spread of the errors. It says that the errors scatter equally widely at every value of the predictor. It does not say that all residuals have the same size. When the spread changes with the predictor, this is called heteroskedasticity. Business data often show it. Take shops of different sizes. The monthly revenue of a small shop may differ from its fitted value by a few thousand euros, and that of a large shop by tens of thousands. Figure 2.3 shows both situations with invented data for 90 shops, with monthly revenue regressed on floor space.

See the R code for this figure 
library(ggplot2)
set.seed(803)
n_s <- 90
space <- runif(n_s, 20, 500)                         # floor space, in m2
rev_const <- 10 + 0.12 * space + rnorm(n_s, 0, 8)    # same spread everywhere
rev_funnel <- 10 + 0.12 * space +
  rnorm(n_s, 0, 0.04 * space)                        # spread grows with size
m_const <- lm(rev_const ~ space)
m_funnel <- lm(rev_funnel ~ space)

case_names <- c("Homoskedasticity holds", "Homoskedasticity fails")
spread_df <- data.frame(
  case = factor(rep(case_names, each = n_s), levels = case_names),
  fitted = c(fitted(m_const), fitted(m_funnel)),
  residual = c(residuals(m_const), residuals(m_funnel))
)

ggplot(spread_df, aes(x = fitted, y = residual)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, colour = "#C98B2E",
             linetype = "dashed", linewidth = 0.8) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.55, size = 1.8, colour = "#009E73") +
  facet_wrap(~ case) +
  labs(x = "Fitted monthly revenue (€1,000)",
       y = "Residual (€1,000)") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 13) +
  theme(
    panel.grid.minor = element_blank(),
    panel.grid.major.x = element_blank(),
    axis.title = element_text(colour = "#17324D"),
    strip.text = element_text(colour = "#17324D", face = "bold")
  )

In the left panel the residuals form an even horizontal band: the vertical spread is about the same at small and at large fitted values. This is what a residual plot looks like when homoskedasticity holds. In the right panel the band is narrow on the left and wide on the right, like a funnel lying on its side. The vertical spread of the residuals grows along the fitted values, so homoskedasticity fails. A band that narrows from left to right is heteroskedasticity too. Both panels have the same average pattern, so the problem is not the line itself but how far the shops scatter around it.

Shark Tank data Now look for heteroskedasticity in Figure 2.1. At small fitted values the residuals stay close to the zero line. At larger fitted values they spread over several million dollars. The same five groups as in the linearity check confirm what the plot shows. The code gives the standard deviation of the residuals in each group:

round(tapply(residuals(val_model), fifth, sd), 2)   # spread of the residuals
[0.04,0.625] (0.625,1.33]   (1.33,2.5]      (2.5,5]       (5,50] 
        0.14         0.36         1.19         1.08         3.61 
round(tapply(vals$agreed_m, fifth, mean), 2)         # average agreed valuation
[0.04,0.625] (0.625,1.33]   (1.33,2.5]      (2.5,5]       (5,50] 
        0.28         0.62         1.34         2.22         7.83

The first output gives the standard deviations of the residuals, in $ millions. They are $0.14 million in the fifth with the smallest claims, then $0.36, $1.19, and $1.08 million, and $3.61 million in the fifth with the largest claims. The last is about 25 times the first. So the spread broadly grows with the size of the claim, like a funnel. The second output shows that the deals also get larger: the average agreed valuation rises from $0.28 million in the first fifth to $7.83 million in the last. Relative to the size of the deals the spread is more stable: in every fifth the standard deviation lies between 0.46 and 0.89 times the average agreed valuation of that fifth. This makes economic sense: founders and sharks can disagree by much more money about a $20 million company than about a $200,000 company. This pattern is heteroskedasticity. The usual standard errors assume one common variance \(\sigma^2\), so here they depend on an assumption that probably does not hold. The slope itself stays unbiased, because heteroskedasticity is not part of the conditions for an unbiased slope. Lecture 3 returns to this point.

Normality of the errors

The third row of the table is normality of the errors. It matters most when you have few observations, for example a young venture with 20 weeks of sales data. The tests and intervals of Lecture 3 then rely on it. A histogram of the residuals gives a first impression, but it is hard to judge the ends of a distribution from a histogram. A normal Q-Q plot (quantile-quantile plot) shows the ends more clearly. It puts the standardised residuals in order and compares them with the values that a normal distribution would give. A standardised residual is a residual measured in standard deviations, and the section on outliers below defines it precisely. The horizontal axis shows where a residual of this rank would lie if the errors were normal. The vertical axis shows where the residual actually lies. If the residuals are close to normal, the points lie along the reference line.

Shark Tank data Figure 2.4 compares an invented case with normal errors and the Shark Tank model. The invented case has the same number of observations, 874, so that the two panels are easy to compare.

See the R code for this figure 
library(ggplot2)
sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
vals <- subset(sharks, deal_on_show == 1 & deal_equity_pct > 0)
vals$claimed_m <- vals$requested_valuation_usd / 1e6
vals$agreed_m <- vals$deal_valuation_usd / 1e6
val_model <- lm(agreed_m ~ claimed_m, data = vals)

set.seed(805)
x_inv <- runif(874, 0, 10)
y_inv <- 1 + 0.6 * x_inv + rnorm(874, 0, 1)          # normal errors
m_inv <- lm(y_inv ~ x_inv)

qq_names <- c("Invented: normal errors", "Shark Tank valuation model")
qq_df <- data.frame(
  case = factor(rep(qq_names, each = 874), levels = qq_names),
  rs = c(rstandard(m_inv), rstandard(val_model))
)

ggplot(qq_df, aes(sample = rs)) +
  geom_qq_line(colour = "#D55E00", linewidth = 0.9) +
  geom_qq(colour = "#009E73", alpha = 0.4, size = 1.6) +
  facet_wrap(~ case) +
  labs(x = "Normal theoretical quantile",
       y = "Standardised residual") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 13) +
  theme(
    panel.grid.minor = element_blank(),
    panel.grid.major.x = element_blank(),
    axis.title = element_text(colour = "#17324D"),
    strip.text = element_text(colour = "#17324D", face = "bold")
  )

In the left panel the points follow the reference line from one end to the other. Small departures at the very ends are normal in any sample. This is what a Q-Q plot looks like when normality holds. In the right panel the middle of the distribution is close to the line, but both ends bend away from it, the upper end more strongly. The Shark Tank residuals have more extreme values on both sides than a normal distribution has. We say that such a distribution has heavy tails. As a benchmark, under a normal distribution about 3 in 1,000 values lie beyond \(\pm 3\) standard deviations, so about 2 or 3 of the 874 deals. Here 25 deals do, and the most extreme standardised residual is about 8. So normality fails for this model.

Two base R commands draw the Shark Tank panel without the styling of the figure:

qqnorm(rstandard(val_model))
qqline(rstandard(val_model))

How much does this failure matter? Normal errors are needed for exact t and F distributions in small samples. With 874 observations the tests and intervals of Lecture 3 still hold approximately without normal errors, because of the central limit theorem: averages over many observations are close to normal, and the estimated slope is such an average. The approximation is weaker when a few extreme deals decide where the line lies. Very extreme outliers distort both the line and its standard errors, in samples of any size. The section on outliers below therefore looks at these deals one by one.

Independence

Independence says that the error of one row tells you nothing about the error of another row. It is a property of how the data were collected, not of the numbers themselves. It holds when the rows are drawn separately from each other, as in a random sample of customers from a long customer list. It fails when rows share something that affects the outcome. Three business examples show how this happens.

  • Weekly sales of one shop. A busy week, for example during a local event, is often followed by another busy week. The errors of neighbouring weeks then move together.
  • Customers of the same shop. In a survey of customer satisfaction across 20 shops, the customers of one shop meet the same staff in the same location. Their errors move together.
  • Shark Tank episodes. Pitches in the same episode face the same panel of sharks on the same day, and the same few sharks appear in hundreds of deals. Their rows share negotiators, moods, and editing decisions.

When rows are related but you treat them as independent, the data seem to contain more information than they really do. The 874 deals contain somewhat less information than 874 unrelated deals would, and the usual standard errors are too small. A residual-versus-fitted plot cannot show this problem, because it does not show which rows belong together. Only knowledge of how the data were collected tells you which rows might be related. So before you trust the standard errors, ask: were the rows drawn separately, or do some of them share a person, a firm, a place, or a time?

Zero conditional mean (exogeneity)

The last row of the table matters for how you read the slope. It uses two new terms, defined together here.

Definition

Confounder. A variable that is not in the model and that affects both \(X\) and \(Y\).

Omitted variable bias. The distortion of the slope of \(X\) that arises when a confounder is left out of the model: the slope also picks up the relationship between the confounder and \(Y\).

If you read the slope as a description of how average outcomes differ across values of \(X\), the error is just the distance from the line of averages. Linearity then already makes the errors average zero at every value of \(X\). If you read the slope as the effect of changing \(X\), the error term contains all other causes of \(Y\). Zero conditional mean then says that these other causes are unrelated to \(X\). An omitted confounder breaks this assumption, and the slope is then too large or too small as a measure of the effect of \(X\). This is omitted variable bias.

No residual plot can show a confounder. Least squares always gives residuals with a mean of zero and no straight-line relation to \(X\), even when the error terms are related to \(X\). So the residual plot looks clean even when a confounder exists. Instead, ask how the values of \(X\) came about, and which common causes of \(X\) and \(Y\) are plausible but missing from the data. Three business examples show the kind of reasoning this takes.

  • Price and demand. A café cuts its prices in weeks when demand is already weak. Weak demand lowers the number of orders and also leads to the price cut, so a regression of orders on price mixes the two.
  • Advertising and season. A webshop advertises most in December, when people buy more anyway. The advertising slope then also picks up the December rush.
  • Network ties on Shark Tank. Suppose founders with strong investor networks both claim higher valuations, because they know the market, and get better deals, because the sharks know them. Network strength is not recorded in the data, so it is an omitted confounder for a causal reading of the valuation slope.

In each example the slope still describes how average outcomes differ across values of \(X\), which is useful for comparison and prediction. To read it as the effect of changing \(X\), you need a design in which \(X\) is unrelated to the other causes. The A/B tests of Lecture 5 set \(X\) at random for exactly this reason. Lecture 4 shows omitted variable bias in an example, and shows how adding a confounder to the model as a control variable removes it.

Analysing residuals: outliers and influential observations

Shark Tank data The residual plots showed that a few deals lie far from the line. A founder with an ordinary company wants to know whether the 65 cents describe the typical deal or rest mainly on a handful of very large deals. You would not want one huge number to drive the slope. This section first finds the unusual deals. It then checks how much the results change without them, which is called a sensitivity analysis.

A first check needs no plot. The function summary() gives the smallest residual, the largest residual, the mean, the median, and the two quartiles. Basic Lecture 1 defines the median and the quartiles: at least a quarter of the values lie at or below the first quartile, half at or below the median, and at least three quarters at or below the third quartile.

Example 2.1 (Worked example: reading the summary of the residuals) Shark Tank data Read the numbers one by one, all in $ millions:

  • The median residual is about \(-0.01\): the typical deal lies almost exactly on the fitted line.
  • The middle half of all residuals, from the first to the third quartile, runs from \(-0.40\) to \(0.17\): a band about $570,000 wide.
  • The minimum is about \(-7.8\) and the maximum about \(13.4\): one deal was agreed $7.8 million below its fitted valuation and another $13.4 million above. As a benchmark, the average agreed valuation is $2.2 million, so each of these misses is several times the size of an average deal.

The mean is zero, as it always is when the model has an intercept. If no deal were extreme, the largest residuals would be a few times the width of the middle half. Here they are 14 to 24 times as large. A few deals miss the line by far more than the typical deal does, and such deals can strongly change the fitted line. The same numbers, without the mean, appear at the top of summary(val_model). Lecture 3 explains this output.

Two standard tools make this check precise. A standardised residual, rstandard(), is a residual divided by its standard deviation, so it measures the residual in standard deviations. Values beyond about \(\pm 3\) are large by any common standard. Cook’s distance, cooks.distance(), measures how much the whole fitted line would move if one observation were removed. A common threshold is \(4/n\): you look more closely at observations above it.

Definition

Outlier. An observation with an unusually large residual: its outcome lies far from the fitted line. A common rule is a standardised residual beyond \(\pm 3\).

Influential observation. An observation that changes the fitted line noticeably when it is removed. A common rule is a Cook’s distance above \(4/n\).

Sensitivity analysis. Fitting the model again under a different, stated choice, for example without the influential observations, and comparing the results with the original fit.

Without the deal claimed at $50 million, the slope rises a little, from 0.649 to 0.666: about 67 cents instead of 65 cents per claimed dollar, a change of 3%. Without all 64 flagged deals, the slope falls to 0.567: about 57 cents per claimed dollar, 13% less than with all deals. So no single deal decides the slope, but the 7% of flagged deals together move it noticeably. The intercepts stay close to zero in all three fits, between \(-\$0.12\) million and $0.05 million. The flagged deals are real pitches, so the fit without them does not replace the fit with all deals. It shows how much the result depends on a few large deals, and a reader of your analysis needs both numbers: 65 cents with all deals and 57 cents without the flagged ones.

What should you do with a flagged observation? Follow three steps, in this order:

  1. Investigate. Is it a data error, or a real pitch? Here the valuations of the flagged deals match their recorded amounts and shares, so we treat these deals as real.
  2. Check and report. Refit the model without the flagged observations, as in the sensitivity analysis above. Say which observations you flagged and with which rule, and report both fits if the results change.
  3. Decide openly. Keep or drop an observation only for a stated reason: a data error, a rule that you fixed before looking at the results, or knowledge that the observation comes from a different situation. State the decision and the reason. Never drop rows silently or because they do not fit your story.

What a residual check can and cannot show

  • A residual plot without a pattern agrees with linearity and homoskedasticity. An omitted confounder and dependent errors do not show up in a plot, so they need knowledge of how the data were produced.
  • Heteroskedasticity or an influential deal is a finding to report. Report it, and check with a sensitivity analysis how much it changes the results.
  • Keeping or dropping an unusual observation is a decision that you make from knowledge of the data or a stated rule, and that you state openly.

What you can do with it: before you trust a fitted line, look at its residual plot and at the few points that move it most. If the conclusion changes without them, say so.

Answering the driving question

Under what conditions should we trust the Lecture 1 line? The slope is right on average if the average of \(Y\) lies on a straight line, the deals are independent, and no omitted confounder is related to the claim. The usual standard errors also need a constant spread of the errors (homoskedasticity) and independence. Normal errors matter only in small samples.

What do its residuals reveal? The average residuals stay close to zero across the range of claims, so the plots agree with linearity. But the vertical spread grows with the size of the claim, from a standard deviation of $0.14 million in the fifth with the smallest claims to $3.61 million in the fifth with the largest. This heteroskedasticity does not bias the slope, but it makes the usual standard errors doubtful. And 64 deals, 7% of all deals, have a Cook’s distance above \(4/n\). Without them the slope falls from 0.65 to 0.57. Independence and zero conditional mean cannot be seen in any plot. They depend on how the show works, for example on shared episodes and on founders’ networks.

As a founder you might expect about 57 to 65 cents of agreed valuation per extra dollar of claimed valuation, under the assumption that your company is like the companies on the show. The 65 cents describe all deals, and the 57 cents describe the deals without the 64 that move the line most. Lecture 3 calculates standard errors, intervals, and tests for this model. The spread that grows with the claim and the influential deals limit how much you can trust those results.

A founder’s decision (invented example)

You regress weekly orders on weekly advertising spending. One week in your data includes a catering order of 200 meal boxes, in a week with high advertising. With that week the advertising slope is 0.15 orders per euro. Without that week it is 0.09. You earn 4 euros of margin per order. Which slope do you use to plan next term’s advertising budget?

Try it yourself first.

  1. What do the two slopes say about 100 euros of advertising, in orders and in margin?
  2. Why does one week change the slope so much?
  3. Decide which slope to plan with, and what to report.
See the solution
  1. With the slope of 0.15: 15 more orders and 60 euros of margin. With 0.09: 9 more orders and 36 euros of margin.
  2. The catering week is an influential observation: an unusually large outcome at a high value of the predictor. It pulls the line up at high spending, so the slope gets steeper. One week out of many changes the slope by 40%, from 0.15 to 0.09.
  3. Do not base the budget on a fit that depends on one single week. Catering is a different business from selling single meal boxes every day. Report both fits, plan the daily business with the slope of 0.09, and look at catering separately. Do not simply delete the week.
