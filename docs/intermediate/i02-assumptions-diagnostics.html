Lecture 2: What the model assumes and how residuals betray it

By the end of Lecture 2, you can

  • state the assumptions behind ordinary least-squares estimation and inference, and say what each one supports;
  • explain why the outcome itself does not need to be normally distributed;
  • inspect residual plots for non-linearity and unequal variance;
  • explain which assumptions no plot can establish, and what design knowledge assesses them;
  • compute standardised residuals and Cook’s distance, and identify influential observations;
  • read a normal Q–Q plot and say when normality matters;
  • respond to an influential observation properly: investigate, report, never silently delete.

Driving question

Every fitted line looks authoritative — the software draws it with equal confidence through good data and bad. Whether the line deserves trust depends on assumptions no software checks for you.

The fitted line from Lecture 1 looks convincing. Under what conditions should we trust it, and what do its residuals confess?

In Figure 18.1, fitted outcomes run horizontally and observed-minus-fitted residuals vertically, both in the outcome’s units of $1,000. A point above zero is a pitch that agreed more than its fitted value. Before reading patterns out of this cloud, we need to know what the model actually assumes.

What each assumption supports

For

\[ Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+\varepsilon_i, \]

separate the conditions below rather than memorising one vague statement that “the data are normal.” Each condition supports a different part of the analysis — take the table one row at a time.

Condition Meaning What it supports
Linear conditional mean \(E[Y\mid X=x]=\beta_0+\beta_1x\) over the modelled range One constant slope summarises the mean relationship
Zero conditional mean \(E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0\) The slope is not systematically absorbing omitted components related to \(X\)
Independent observations Errors from different rows do not supply duplicate information The usual standard-error calculation and effective sample size
Positive variation in \(X\) Not all observations have the same predictor value A slope can be estimated
Constant conditional variance \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2\) Classical OLS standard errors have the stated form
Conditional normality \(\varepsilon\mid X\) follows a normal distribution Exact t and F reference distributions in small samples

Say it early and loudly: the outcome does not need to be normally distributed, and neither does the predictor. Agreed deal amounts are strongly right-skewed, and that alone breaks nothing. The assumptions concern the conditional mean, the errors, and the sampling process — not the marginal histograms of \(Y\) and \(X\).

Linearity is about the average, not every point

Individual outcomes need not lie on a line. Linearity says that the average outcome at each predictor value follows a line. A systematic curve in residuals means one constant slope misses part of that average pattern.

Constant variance is the vertical counterpart: homoskedasticity says the conditional variance is the same at each predictor value. It does not say all residuals have the same size. A fan-shaped residual plot — in which the vertical band widens or narrows — suggests changing conditional variance. Figure 18.2 shows both stories.

Check: match each residual pattern to the assumption it challenges

A U-shaped (curved) residual pattern challenges the linear conditional mean: the average residual is not zero everywhere, so a straight line misses part of the mean relationship. A fan challenges constant conditional variance: the vertical spread of residuals changes with the fitted value. A real plot can show both problems at once.

Reading the deal model’s vertical spread

Now apply the fan test to Figure 18.1. At small fitted values the residuals hug the zero line; at larger fitted values they range over hundreds of thousands of dollars. The conditional variance of agreed amounts grows with the size of the deal — an economically unsurprising fan. Classical standard errors, which assume one common \(\sigma^2\), are therefore built on a doubtful assumption here. Lecture 3 returns to exactly this point.

What plots cannot show

Two rows of the assumptions table never appear in any residual plot.

Zero conditional mean. Fitted OLS residuals are mechanically centred around the fitted line — the least-squares algebra forces it — so a residual plot cannot reveal whether \(E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0\) holds in the population. Unobserved confounders are, by definition, not on the plot. Ask instead how the observations came about: what plausible common causes were omitted, whether measurement error or reverse direction is credible.

Independence. Whether rows share information is a fact about the data collection, not about the residuals. If rows are related, treating them as independent makes the effective sample size look larger than it is and standard errors too small.

Two short Shark Tank stories, classified:

  • Repeat pitchers. Some ventures appear in more than one season, and some founders pitch multiple products. Their rows share a founder, a negotiation style, a network of sharks. This is an independence problem: the 882 deal rows carry less than 882 rows’ worth of information.
  • Network ties. Suppose founders with strong investor networks both ask for more (they know the market) and get systematically better deals (the sharks know them). Network strength is unrecorded, related to the predictor, and moves the outcome: this is a zero conditional mean problem — the slope partly absorbs the omitted network effect.

Neither story is visible in Figure 18.1. Both come from knowing how the show works.

Influence: when a few rows steer the line

A diagnosis can start before any plot, in one line of printed output. The five-number summary of the residuals is the first thing summary() reports for any model; here it is on its own:

summary(residuals(deal_model))
   Min. 1st Qu.  Median    Mean 3rd Qu.    Max. 
-434.38  -30.12  -28.85    0.00  -28.21 2821.15

Example 18.1 (Worked example: reading the residual line as a red flag) Same five numbers, read slowly, all in $1,000s:

  • The median residual is about \(-28.8\): the typical deal sits $28,800 below the fitted line.
  • The middle half of all residuals — first to third quartile — spans only \(-30.1\) to \(-28.2\): a band about $2,000 wide. Most deals sit in a tight bundle just under the line.
  • The maximum residual is about \(2821\): one deal came in $2.82 million above its fitted value.

A symmetric, well-behaved residual distribution would have a median near zero and a maximum and minimum of comparable size. Here the mean is zero (it always is, with an intercept), but the distribution is wildly asymmetric: a dense mass slightly below the line, balanced by a few enormous positive misses. Before computing anything else, this one line says the fit is being shaped by a handful of extreme pitches. The same five numbers sit at the top of summary(deal_model), which Lecture 3 reads in full.

Two standard tools quantify the suspicion. A standardised residual, rstandard(), re-expresses each residual in standard-deviation units, so values beyond about \(\pm 3\) are large by any convention. Cook’s distance, cooks.distance(), measures how much the entire fitted line would move if one observation were removed; a common screening threshold is \(4/n\).

rs <- rstandard(deal_model)
cd <- cooks.distance(deal_model)

sum(abs(rs) > 3)          # unusually large standardised residuals
[1] 12
sum(cd > 4 / nrow(deals)) # influential by the 4/n screen
[1] 21
deals[order(cd, decreasing = TRUE)[1:3],
      c("ask_1k", "deal_1k")]
    ask_1k deal_1k
836    150    3000
671    350    2350
364   1000    1750

Twelve pitches have standardised residuals beyond \(\pm 3\), and 21 exceed the \(4/n\) Cook’s screen. The single most influential record asked for $150,000 and agreed $3,000,000 — twenty times its request. Figure 18.3 places the flagged pitches on the scatter.

What to do with a flagged observation — the standing rule, in order:

  1. Investigate. Is it a data error, or a real pitch? (These are real: some sharks buy whole companies.)
  2. Report. State that results depend on a few identifiable observations; show the fit with and without them if it matters.
  3. Never silently delete. Dropping inconvenient rows without saying so is not cleaning, it is misrepresentation.

Q–Q plots: how to read them, when normality matters

The last assumption in the table is conditional normality of the errors. A normal Q–Q plot compares the ordered standardised residuals with the values expected under a normal distribution; agreement appears as points along the reference line.

See the R code for this figure 
library(ggplot2)
sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
deals <- subset(sharks, deal_on_show == 1)
deals$ask_1k <- deals$ask_amount_usd / 1000
deals$deal_1k <- deals$deal_amount_usd / 1000
deal_model <- lm(deal_1k ~ ask_1k, data = deals)
qq_df <- data.frame(rs = rstandard(deal_model))

ggplot(qq_df, aes(sample = rs)) +
  geom_qq_line(colour = "#D55E00", linewidth = 0.9) +
  geom_qq(colour = "#009E73", alpha = 0.4, size = 1.6) +
  labs(x = "Normal theoretical quantile",
       y = "Standardised residual") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 13) +
  theme(
    panel.grid.minor = element_blank(),
    panel.grid.major.x = element_blank(),
    plot.caption = element_text(colour = "#5B6976"),
    axis.title = element_text(colour = "#17324D")
  )

Read the horizontal axis as “where a residual of this rank should sit if errors were normal” and the vertical axis as where it actually sits. In Figure 18.4 the middle of the distribution is close to the line while the upper tail shoots far above it: the residual distribution has a much heavier right tail than a normal.

When does this matter? Normality is used to derive exact t and F distributions in small samples. With 882 reasonably independent observations, central-limit arguments make the inference of Lecture 3 approximately valid even without normal errors — but “approximately” is doing real work when the same few pitches that break normality also dominate the fit. Severe outliers distort both the line and its uncertainty, whatever the sample size.

What a residual check can and cannot certify

  • No visible pattern is not proof. A clean residual plot is consistent with the assumptions; it does not establish them. Omitted confounders and dependent rows leave no trace on any plot.
  • Diagnostics start the conversation, they do not end it. A fan or an influential pitch is a finding to investigate and report, not an automatic verdict on the model — and not a licence to delete rows until the pictures look better.
  • Keeping or dropping an influential observation is a substantive decision, made from knowledge of the data, stated openly in the report — never made silently by the software or the analyst.

Answering the driving question

Trust in the Lecture 1 line is conditional. Its residuals confess two things: the vertical spread grows with the size of the deal (the fan), and a handful of extreme pitches — flagged by standardised residuals beyond \(\pm3\) and Cook’s distances above \(4/n\) — steer both the line and its apparent precision. Nothing in the plots contradicts a linear conditional mean, but zero conditional mean and independence rest on how the show works, not on any picture. Lecture 3 computes standard errors, intervals, and tests for this model — and must respect exactly these two confessions.

Next: We now know when a fitted line deserves provisional trust. What we still lack is precision and worth: the slope is an estimate from one sample, and a model can pass every diagnostic while explaining almost nothing. Lecture 3 brings the inference machinery from QM Basic to the coefficient — standard error, interval, t-test — and adds the fit toolkit: the SST = SSR + SSE decomposition, \(R^2\), and the overall F-test for whether the model is useful at all.
