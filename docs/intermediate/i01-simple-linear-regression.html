Lecture 1: Simple linear regression

By the end of Lecture 1, you can

  • state a simple linear regression model and identify its components;
  • read the slope as a difference of conditional means, with units;
  • convert a coefficient between measurement units (dollars, $1,000s, $100,000s);
  • distinguish observations, fitted values, errors, and residuals;
  • explain what least squares minimises and why;
  • compute a slope and intercept by hand on a tiny dataset;
  • fit a regression with lm() in R and interpret both coefficients within the observed range.

Driving question

The first question of deal-making: does asking for more get you more? A line fitted through the data turns that vague hunch into one number a founder can actually use.

Among pitches that reached a deal, does asking for more money go together with getting more money — and by how much per dollar asked?

Units first: money in thousands

Every number this lecture produces carries the units of these two columns, so settle the units before any model. We divide raw dollar amounts by 1,000: a request of $300,000 becomes ask_1k = 300. Scaling changes nothing about the relationship; it only makes axes and coefficients readable (ask_amount_usd = 300000 and ask_1k = 300 are the same fact).

Example 17.1 (Worked example: the same coefficient in three unit systems) Later in this lecture, R will print an intercept of about 27.571 with both variables in $1,000s. Two lines of arithmetic read it:

\[ 27.571 \text{ (in \$1,000s)} = 27.571 \times \$1{,}000 = \$27{,}571. \]

Suppose a published paper measures both variables in $100,000s instead. Dividing both \(Y\) and \(X\) by a further 100 shrinks the intercept by that factor but leaves the slope untouched, because the slope is a ratio of the two:

\[ \text{intercept: } 27.571 / 100 = 0.276 \text{ (in \$100,000s)}, \qquad \text{slope: } 1.0085 \text{ (unchanged)}. \]

And in raw dollars? Multiplying both variables by 1,000 gives an intercept of \(27.571 \times 1000 = 27{,}571\) dollars — and again the same slope, 1.0085 dollars per dollar. The general rule: rescaling the outcome and the predictor by the same factor moves the intercept and leaves the slope alone; rescaling only one of them rescales the slope by that factor. When two sources disagree about a coefficient, check the units before suspecting the analysis.

The population model and the conditional mean

The simple linear regression model is

\[ Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+\varepsilon_i. \]

Equivalently, the conditional mean is

\[ E[Y_i\mid X_i=x]=\beta_0+\beta_1x. \]

  • \(Y_i\) is the outcome for observation \(i\).
  • \(X_i\) is the predictor.
  • \(\beta_0\) is the population intercept.
  • \(\beta_1\) is the population slope.
  • \(\varepsilon_i\) is the part of \(Y_i\) not represented by the line.

The slope is a difference of conditional means:

\[ \beta_1 =E[Y\mid X=x+1]-E[Y\mid X=x]. \]

It is the expected difference in \(Y\) associated with a one-unit difference in \(X\) — here, the expected difference in agreed amount between pitches whose requests differ by $1,000. “Associated with” is deliberate, and this is the one place the phrase is flagged: a regression line alone compares averages across observed groups; it does not identify what would happen if a given pitch changed its request. The caveat box at the end of the lecture collects the full scope statement.

Errors versus residuals

The population line is a model statement; nobody has seen it. What we compute from data is the fitted sample line

\[ \widehat Y_i=b_0+b_1X_i, \]

and, for each observation, the residual — the vertical difference

\[ e_i=Y_i-\widehat Y_i. \]

The error \(\varepsilon_i\) and the residual \(e_i\) play parallel roles in the two equations but are different objects, as Figure 17.2 shows.

  • \(\varepsilon_i = Y_i - (\beta_0+\beta_1X_i)\) measures the distance to the population line. Because \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) are unknown, \(\varepsilon_i\) is never observed.
  • \(e_i = Y_i - (b_0+b_1X_i)\) measures the distance to the fitted line. It is computed from data, one number per observation.

Residuals are our window on the errors, but they are estimates, not the thing itself — Lecture 2 lives entirely in that window.

Check: which object is observable?

Only the residual \(e_i\). The observed data give \(Y_i\) and \(X_i\); the fitted line gives \(\widehat Y_i\), so \(e_i = Y_i - \widehat Y_i\) is a computable number. The error \(\varepsilon_i\) depends on the unknown population coefficients \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\), so it can be reasoned about but never calculated.

Least squares: why vertical, why squared

For observation \(i\), the model predicts \(\widehat Y_i\) at its observed \(X_i\). The residual \(e_i=Y_i-\widehat Y_i\) is therefore a difference in the outcome’s units — thousands of dollars of agreed amount, not distance on the page. A positive residual lies above the line; a negative residual lies below it.

Adding raw residuals is not a useful score: positive and negative misses can cancel. Adding absolute residuals avoids cancellation, but the resulting objective has sharp corners and is harder to optimise algebraically. Squared residuals are non-negative, penalise large misses more strongly, and give a smooth objective with a tractable solution. This is a modelling choice, not a claim that squared error is the only possible loss function.

Ordinary least squares chooses \(b_0\) and \(b_1\) to minimise

\[ \operatorname{SSE}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_i^2 =\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i-b_0-b_1X_i)^2. \]

The initials OLS stand for ordinary least squares: the rule that fits the linear model by minimising this squared-error criterion.

Try the criterion yourself

Drag either square handle. The vertical segments are residuals; the displayed SSE updates after every move. In class the widget stays open over the break with a target SSE to beat; then reveal the line with the smallest SSE.

Least-squares line lab

Squares make every contribution to SSE non-negative. Longer residuals contribute disproportionately because their lengths are squared.

The line that minimises SSE has closed-form coefficients. For simple regression,

\[ b_1=\frac{s_{XY}}{s_X^2}, \qquad b_0=\bar y-b_1\bar x, \]

where \(s_{XY}\) is the sample covariance of \(X\) and \(Y\) and \(s_X^2\) is the sample variance of \(X\). The second formula rearranges to \(\bar y = b_0 + b_1\bar x\): the fitted line always passes through the point of means \((\bar x,\bar y)\).

Example 17.2 (Worked example: least squares by hand on three points) Three observations: \((1,2)\), \((2,5)\), \((3,5)\). Every sum written out.

Means. \(\bar x = (1+2+3)/3 = 2\) and \(\bar y = (2+5+5)/3 = 4\).

Deviations and their products.

\(i\) \(x_i-\bar x\) \(y_i-\bar y\) \((x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)\) \((x_i-\bar x)^2\)
1 \(-1\) \(-2\) \(2\) \(1\)
2 \(0\) \(1\) \(0\) \(0\)
3 \(1\) \(1\) \(1\) \(1\)
Sum \(3\) \(2\)

Covariance and variance. Dividing both sums by \(n-1=2\): \(s_{XY} = 3/2 = 1.5\) and \(s_X^2 = 2/2 = 1\).

Slope and intercept.

\[ b_1 = \frac{s_{XY}}{s_X^2} = \frac{1.5}{1} = 1.5, \qquad b_0 = \bar y - b_1\bar x = 4 - 1.5\times 2 = 1. \]

The fitted line is \(\widehat y = 1 + 1.5x\). Figure 17.3 confirms the third fact: the line passes exactly through \((\bar x,\bar y)=(2,4)\). The residuals are \(-0.5\), \(+1\), \(-0.5\) — and they sum to zero, as least-squares residuals always do when the model has an intercept.

Fit and read the Shark Tank model in R

Now the real data. The construction is repeated so this block runs on its own — it is the same subset and the same $1,000-unit columns as the warm-up:

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
deals <- subset(sharks, deal_on_show == 1)
deals$ask_1k <- deals$ask_amount_usd / 1000
deals$deal_1k <- deals$deal_amount_usd / 1000

deal_model <- lm(deal_1k ~ ask_1k, data = deals)
coef(deal_model)
(Intercept)      ask_1k 
   27.57097     1.00851

The fitted equation is approximately

\[ \widehat{\text{deal amount}} =27.571+1.0085\times\text{amount requested}, \]

with both monetary variables measured in $1,000s.

The slope, with units. One additional $1,000 requested is associated with about $1,008.50 more in the fitted agreed amount among pitches that reached an on-air deal — close to dollar-for-dollar. It does not mean that asking for more causes investors to offer more.

The intercept, with units. The intercept predicts about $27,571 at a request of zero. A zero request lies outside the observed range (the smallest request in the data is $10,000) and is not substantively meaningful; an intercept can be mathematically necessary without having a useful standalone interpretation.

Observed, fitted, and residual for the first rows:

head(data.frame(
  observed = deals$deal_1k,
  fitted = fitted(deal_model),
  residual = residuals(deal_model)
))
   observed    fitted  residual

For every row, observed = fitted + residual. Check it on the first row: the pitch asked $50,000 and agreed $50,000; the model fits \(27.571 + 1.0085 \times 50 = 77.996\), and indeed \(50 = 77.996 + (-27.996)\).

Prediction inside the evidence boundary

predict(deal_model, newdata = data.frame(ask_1k = 300))
       1 
330.1239

For a $300,000 request (ask_1k = 300), the fitted mean agreed amount is about 330.12, that is, $330,124. This is a model-based average, not a guarantee for a new pitch. If such a pitch actually agreed $250,000, its residual would be

\[ e = 250 - 330.12 = -80.12, \]

about $80,124 below the fitted conditional mean. Predictions are defensible only inside the range of requests the data contain; extrapolating far beyond it would rely almost entirely on the assumed line.

What this fitted line can and cannot say

The slope 1.0085 describes an association among the 882 televised pitches that reached an on-air deal. Three limits apply.

  • Association, not causation. Requests were not randomly assigned. Pitches asking for more differ in many other ways — stage, industry, revenue — that also move agreed amounts.
  • Selection into deals. The analysis conditions on reaching a deal. Pitches without a deal are structurally excluded, so the line describes deal records, not all pitches, and certainly not all start-ups.
  • No advice about changing an ask. The model does not say that a founder who raises their request by $1,000 will walk away with $1,008.50 more. That is a claim about an intervention, which this observational comparison cannot support.

Answering the driving question

Among pitches that reached a deal: yes, asking for more goes together with getting more, almost one-for-one. The fitted slope is 1.0085, so each extra $1,000 requested is associated with about $1,008.50 more agreed on air — about $1.01 per dollar asked — with the caveat box’s limits attached.

Next: We now have a fitted line and can read its slope in money terms. But least squares will draw an equally confident line through terrible data — the software never warns you. Lecture 2 lays out the assumptions under which the line means what we claimed (linearity of the average, equal spread, independence, and design conditions no plot can certify) and teaches the diagnostics: reading residual plots, and spotting the single influential pitch that can bend an entire fit.

Worked exam interpretation (5 minutes)

Question (mock exam Question 1 pattern). A model of venture sales growth (percentage points) on marketing spending (€10,000s) reports \(\widehat{\text{growth}} = 6.60 + 1.38\,\text{spending}\). Check a model answer

Each additional €10,000 of marketing spending is associated with 1.38 percentage points higher fitted sales growth among sampled ventures. €30,000 is spending = 3, so the fitted value is \(6.60+1.38\times3=10.74\) percentage points; the residual is \(15-10.74=4.26\) percentage points — observed growth exceeds the fitted value.

Utility: two sentences

Think of your EiA3 venture — or any business you know well. Name one numerical outcome and one numerical input you suspect drives it, and write two sentences: first state the slope you would expect (sign and units), then say what decision would change if the fitted slope turned out to be near zero. There is no wrong answer; the point is connecting today’s tool to something you care about.

See an example, then write your own

Take a student-run campus meal-prep box venture. “Outcome: weekly orders; input: euros of social-media spend that week — I expect a positive slope of a few orders per extra €100. If the fitted slope came out near zero, I would move the whole budget to campus flyers.”
