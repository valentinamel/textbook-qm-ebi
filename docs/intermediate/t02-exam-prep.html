In the tutorial

Try each task in R before you open the answer. More practice for home is in the final section of this page, Extra practice for home.

Tasks 1 to 4 use the Shark Tank file, and money is measured in thousands of dollars. Tutorial 1 asked how a single predictor relates to the outcome. This tutorial asks what changes when several predictors are held fixed at the same time, when categories enter the model, and when a slope depends on a group or on the season. First build the data for the analysis. The code converts the requested amount to thousands of dollars (ask_1k). Task 2 uses all 1,441 pitches (sharks). Task 1 uses the 874 deals for a share of the company (deals). Tasks 3 and 4 use the 869 of these deals with a known team classification (known_deals). The 5 deals with an unknown team are left out, because the team dummy cannot be defined for them, and Tasks 3 and 4 then use the same observations. For these deals the code builds the dummy women (1 for all-women and mixed teams), the team variable gender with all-men teams as the reference category, and the season centred at its mean (season_c).

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
sharks$ask_1k <- sharks$ask_amount_usd / 1000
deals <- subset(sharks, deal_on_show == 1 & deal_equity_pct > 0)
known_deals <- subset(deals, pitcher_gender_group != "unknown")
known_deals$women <- as.integer(
  known_deals$pitcher_gender_group %in% c("all_women", "mixed")
)
known_deals$gender <- relevel(factor(known_deals$pitcher_gender_group),
                              ref = "all_men")
known_deals$season_c <- known_deals$season - mean(known_deals$season)
c(pitches = nrow(sharks), deals = nrow(deals), known_deals = nrow(known_deals))

Filled-in structure:

  • R code: see the code above.
  • Number of dummy coefficients, and why: 15, one for each of the 16 industries except the reference category Food and Beverage.
  • Interpretation of the Technology/Software coefficient: at the same season and requested amount, Technology/Software pitches are fitted to offer about 2.93 percentage points less equity than Food and Beverage pitches (p \(\approx\) 0.004).
  • Size, as a percent of the mean equity offered: 2.93 points is about 22% of the mean equity offered of 13.2%, a sizeable difference for a founder.
  • Null hypothesis H0: all 15 industry coefficients are zero. Alternative hypothesis H1: at least one of them is not zero.
  • Test statistic formula: \(F=\dfrac{(\text{SSE}_R-\text{SSE}_C)/q}{\text{SSE}_C/(n-k_C-1)}\).
  • Values in the formula: \(\text{SSE}_R=83{,}493\), \(\text{SSE}_C=82{,}114\), \(q=15\), and \(n-k_C-1=1{,}441-17-1=1{,}423\).
  • Value of the test statistic: \(F=\dfrac{1{,}378.4/15}{82{,}114/1{,}423}=\dfrac{91.89}{57.70}\approx1.59\).
  • P-value: about 0.069.
  • Conclusion: since \(0.069>0.05\), do not reject \(H_0\) at the 5% level. The data do not show that the industry terms together improve the model. This does not prove that all industries offer the same share.
  • Why the t-test and the F-test can disagree: the F-test asks whether the 15 industry terms together improve the model, while each t-test asks about one comparison with Food and Beverage. With 15 separate t-tests at the 5% level, one p-value below 0.05 can easily occur by chance even if no industry differs, because each test rejects a true null hypothesis in about 1 of 20 cases.

  • Value of the test statistic: \(F\approx40.7\), on 1 and 865 degrees of freedom.

A note on the late rise. The season means of the agreed equity in the output fall from 50.1% in Season 1 to about 21% in Season 8 and then stay between about 17% and 23%, with no clear rise after Season 11. The quadratic curve describes the steep early decline well, but its late rise comes mainly from its U shape. A plot of the season means next to the fitted curve is the simplest check of such a turn.

  • Ratio of the two standard errors: \(1.654/0.641\approx2.6\), so the standard error is about 2.6 times as large.
  • Why the standard error grows: with machines in the model, the floor area slope compares clubs with the same number of machines. Such clubs differ only a little in floor area, because less than 15% of the variation in floor area is not shared with the machines. With so little separate variation, the data say little about the floor area slope, and its standard error grows.
  • Overall F-test and its conclusion: \(F\approx28.2\) on 2 and 37 degrees of freedom, far above the critical value qf(0.95, 2, 37) = 3.25, so reject the null hypothesis that both slopes are zero. Together, the two predictors are clearly related to new members, and they explain about 60% of the variation (\(R^2=0.604\)).
  • How both results can be true: the F-test asks whether club size, in floor area and machines together, is related to new members, and the answer is clearly yes. Each t-test asks about one predictor with the other held fixed, and because the two move so closely together, the data cannot tell which of the two matters, so the non-significant t-tests do not show that size is unimportant.
