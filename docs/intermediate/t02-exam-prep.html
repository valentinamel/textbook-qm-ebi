Tutorial 2: Multiple regression and extensions

By the end of Tutorial 2, you can

  • fit and interpret multiple regression in R;
  • test individual and grouped coefficients against zero;
  • interpret dummy, interaction, and polynomial terms correctly;
  • compare nested models with a partial F-test;
  • write a complete TestVision-ready analysis with design limits.

Tutorials are exam preparation sessions: every task below follows the format of the final exam. The standing routine for each task is the same — the TA states the task, pairs work on it first for a few minutes with AI use allowed, and the solution is then discussed in plenary, starting from the pair attempts.

One note on the order. The tasks run in course order: Task 1 rests on Lecture 4, Task 2 on Lecture 5, and Tasks 3–4 on yesterday’s Lecture 6. This is deliberate — the tutorial meets the day after Lecture 6, so you warm up on settled material before testing what you learned yesterday.

In class

Attempt each task in R before opening the answer. All tasks use the Shark Tank teaching file and the running money unit of thousands of dollars. Build the analysis data once — the same constructions as Lectures 4–6:

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
sharks$ask_1k <- sharks$ask_amount_usd / 1000
known_gender <- subset(sharks, pitcher_gender_group != "unknown")
known_gender$gender <- relevel(factor(known_gender$pitcher_gender_group),
                               ref = "all_men")
known_gender$ask_1k <- known_gender$ask_amount_usd / 1000
known_gender$women <- as.integer(
  known_gender$pitcher_gender_group %in% c("all_women", "mixed")
)
known_gender$season_c <- known_gender$season - mean(known_gender$season)

At fixed values of the other included predictors, among the 1,441 recorded pitches: one later season is associated with about 0.836 percentage points less initial equity; an extra $1,000 requested with about 0.0029 percentage points less equity (about 0.29 points per $100,000); and one additional description word with about 0.128 percentage points less equity. Selection into the show, unmeasured venture quality, reverse relationships, and measurement choices are all possible causal threats.

Check your answer

The reference is all-men. At the same season and requested amount, all-women presenting teams are fitted to offer about 1.28 equity percentage points more, and mixed teams about 0.12 points more, than all-men teams — percentage points, not percent. The partial null is that both dummy coefficients equal zero; \(F\approx3.76\), p \(\approx\) 0.023, so reject at 5%: the terms are jointly useful even though the mixed dummy’s own t-test (p \(\approx\) 0.82) is far from significant. The F-test asks whether the pair improves the model together; each t-test asks about one reference-group contrast. The broad source coding and observational design prohibit a causal gender claim.

For all-men teams the season slope is about \(-0.799\) percentage points per season; for teams with women represented it is \(-0.799-0.061=-0.861\). The estimated slope difference is \(-0.061\) percentage points per season with p \(\approx\) 0.520: this model provides no evidence of different season trends between the two source-defined groups — and no proof of equal trends, since the estimate is imprecise. Centering makes the lower-order group coefficient compare the groups at the average season (about 8.8) instead of at the unobserved Season 0; it changes neither the fitted values nor the interaction estimate.

  1. Why must I(season^2) be used in the R formula?
  2. Decide whether the quadratic term adds fit under the classical test.
  3. Calculate the fitted change from Season 10 to Season 11.
  4. State why the curve should not be extrapolated beyond Season 16.
Check your answer

I() tells R to square arithmetically rather than read ^ as formula syntax. The partial F-test gives \(F\approx20.1\) with p \(<\) 0.001, so the quadratic term adds detectable fit. With \(b_1\approx-1.713\) and \(b_2\approx0.051\), the change from Season 10 to 11 is \(b_1+b_2(2\times10+1)=b_1+21b_2\approx-0.64\) percentage points — never read \(b_1\) alone while the squared term is in the model. Beyond Season 16 the fitted parabola turns upward with no data support; polynomials can behave sharply outside the observed range.

After class

Timed integrated task — 30 minutes

Develop one model of equity_offered_pct using season, the requested amount in $1,000s, the three known source gender groups, and one justified extension.

Your submission must include:

  1. a precise research question;
  2. reproducible data preparation;
  3. one pre-model visual;
  4. the R formula and model summary;
  5. coefficient interpretations with units and reference groups;
  6. an individual t-test and an overall or partial F-test;
  7. \(R^2\), adjusted \(R^2\), and residual diagnostics;
  8. one graph of fitted conditional predictions;
  9. a 150-word conclusion separating association, uncertainty, and causal limits.
Check a model structure

A defensible solution could fit

m <- lm(
  equity_offered_pct ~ season * gender + ask_1k,
  data = known_gender
)

or a quadratic-season model with group dummies. Keep lower-order terms, name the reference group, interpret combined slopes or fitted contrasts, compare a nested reduced model with anova(reduced, complete), inspect residuals (including rstandard() and Cook’s distance), and plot predictions within Seasons 1–16. The conclusion must state that the source’s broad presenter classification and the selected televised records do not identify a causal effect of gender or season.

Make it yours (5 minutes)

Utility: two sentences

Think of your EiA3 venture — or any business you know well. Sketch, in two sentences, the integrated analysis you would actually run on its data: first name the outcome, two predictors, and the one extension (a dummy set, an interaction, or a quadratic) that a real question motivates, then say which single caveat — selection, measurement, or omitted variables — would headline your 150-word conclusion. There is no wrong answer; the point is noticing that you now own the entire pipeline.

See an example, then write your own

Take a student-run campus meal-prep box venture. “Outcome: weekly orders; predictors: ad spend and price, plus an exam-week dummy interacted with ad spend, because I believe ads work mainly when students are on campus. The headlining caveat is omitted variables: weather drives both my flyer effort and campus foot traffic, and nothing in my data measures it.”

You can discuss this task — and any other task in this tutorial — with Tilly, the course AI assistant on Canvas: ask it to referee your two-sentence design before you invest a weekend in the data.
