Lecture 3: Coefficient inference, fit, and the F-test

By the end of Lecture 3, you can

  • read a coefficient’s standard error and build its confidence interval by hand, using the correct degrees of freedom;
  • test a regression coefficient against zero and say what the p-value does and does not establish;
  • decompose outcome variation into explained and residual components;
  • calculate and interpret \(R^2\) — and correct four common misreadings;
  • state and interpret the overall F-test for model usefulness;
  • connect the simple-regression F-test to the slope t-test;
  • distinguish fit, statistical evidence, prediction, and causality.

Driving question

A slope is an estimate, not a fact. Before a number goes into a pitch deck or a report, we need to know how precise it is and whether the model behind it earns its keep.

The ask–deal slope is about 1.01. How precisely do we know that number, how much of the outcome does the line explain, and is the model useful at all?

Check your preparation

Interval: \(\text{estimate} \pm (\text{multiplier}) \times \text{standard error}\), with the multiplier close to 1.96 for large samples. Test: \(t = (\text{estimate} - \text{benchmark})/\text{standard error}\), compared with a t distribution; small p-values are evidence against the benchmark.

Check your preparation

How precisely do we know the slope?

The warm-up construction once more, so every object on this page is built in the open:

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
deals <- subset(sharks, deal_on_show == 1)
deals$ask_1k <- deals$ask_amount_usd / 1000
deals$deal_1k <- deals$deal_amount_usd / 1000
deal_model <- lm(deal_1k ~ ask_1k, data = deals)
summary(deal_model)$coefficients

(Intercept) 27.57097 7.24074969  3.80775  1.499641e-04
ask_1k       1.00851 0.01820567 55.39538 4.161677e-289

Lecture 1 read only the first column. The other three columns — and the interval R computes from them — are the pentad from QM Basic, returning unchanged. Nothing in this table is new; only the quantity being estimated is:

Pentad ingredient Where it sits For the ask–deal slope
estimate Estimate column \(b_1 = 1.0085\)
standard error Std. Error column \(\operatorname{SE}(b_1) = 0.0182\)
t value t value column \(t = 55.4\)
p-value Pr(>|t|) column \(< 0.001\)
confidence interval confint() computed below

The standard error answers the pentad’s standing question, now about a slope: if we could redraw the sample, how much would \(b_1\) wobble? The estimated slope 1.0085 came from these 882 deals; a different set of deals would give a slightly different line. The SE of 0.0182 — in the slope’s own units, $1,000 of deal per $1,000 of ask — estimates the size of that sampling wobble under the Lecture 2 model conditions.

Degrees of freedom. The interval multiplier comes from a t distribution with \(n - 2 = 882 - 2 = 880\) degrees of freedom. The subtraction is the same bookkeeping as in QM Basic: estimating the intercept and the slope uses two pieces of information before any is left over to estimate the residual spread. (With \(k\) slopes plus an intercept, the residual degrees of freedom become \(n - k - 1\); that general form matters from Lecture 4 onwards.) At 880 degrees of freedom, the multiplier \(t_{0.975,\,880} = 1.963\) is indistinguishable in practice from the familiar 1.96.

Example 19.1 (Worked example: the slope interval by hand) Everything comes from the coefficient table: \(b_1 = 1.0085\), \(\operatorname{SE}(b_1) = 0.0182\), multiplier \(\approx 1.96\).

\[ b_1 \pm t_{0.975,\,880}\operatorname{SE}(b_1) = 1.0085 \pm 1.96 \times 0.0182 = 1.0085 \pm 0.0357 = (0.973,\; 1.044). \]

In words: under the model conditions, the data are consistent with a population slope between about $973 and $1,044 of agreed amount per $1,000 requested. The interval’s endpoints carry the slope’s units — that is the pentad rule that the estimate, SE, and interval share units, while t and the p-value are unit-free.

R agrees:

confint(deal_model)
                 2.5 %    97.5 %
(Intercept) 13.3598133 41.782122
ask_1k       0.9727782  1.044241

The interval describes sampling uncertainty under the model conditions. It does not include uncertainty from selection into televised pitches, the decision to analyse only on-air deals, or measurement choices — no formula widens an interval for a flawed design.

The t-test against zero

The standard significance test asks whether the data are consistent with no linear association:

\[ H_0:\beta_1=0 \qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_1:\beta_1\ne0, \qquad t=\frac{b_1-0}{\operatorname{SE}(b_1)}. \]

By hand, once: \(t = 1.0085 / 0.0182 \approx 55.4\). An estimate 55 standard errors from zero is astronomically far into the tail of a t distribution. Live verification, computing the p-value exactly as QM Basic did:

coef_table <- summary(deal_model)$coefficients
b1 <- coef_table["ask_1k", "Estimate"]
se_b1 <- coef_table["ask_1k", "Std. Error"]
t_value <- b1 / se_b1
p_value <- 2 * pt(-abs(t_value), df = df.residual(deal_model))
c(estimate = b1, standard_error = se_b1,
  t_statistic = t_value, p_value = p_value)
      estimate standard_error    t_statistic        p_value 
  1.008510e+00   1.820567e-02   5.539538e+01  4.161677e-289

What the tiny p-value does say: if the population slope were zero and the model conditions held, a sample slope this far from zero would essentially never occur — strong evidence against a zero linear association in this selected sample.

What it does not say, one line each:

  • it does not say the model is correctly specified — Lecture 2’s fan and influential pitches stand as the permanent caveat under this table;
  • it does not say the association is large or important — with 882 observations, even tiny slopes can earn tiny p-values;
  • it does not say that requesting more causes a larger deal — no test statistic repairs an observational design.
Check: the p-value is 4×10-289. Is this slope therefore trustworthy?

No. The p-value measures distance from zero in standard-error units, and the standard error was computed under the classical conditions — constant error variance and independent rows among them. Lecture 2 showed the residual fan and the handful of pitches that steer the fit, so this particular SE rests on doubtful ground. “Significant” means the data are inconsistent with a zero slope, not that 0.0182 is the right measure of wobble.

Decomposing variation

The second part of the driving question — how much does the line explain? — needs a benchmark. Without a predictor, the best single prediction for every deal is the mean agreed amount \(\bar y\). Regression is useful to the extent that the fitted values \(\widehat y_i\) improve on that flat prediction.

For each observation, the total deviation from the mean splits exactly into an explained part and a residual part, as Figure 19.1 shows for one point:

\[ \underbrace{y_i-\bar y}_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{(\widehat y_i-\bar y)}_{\text{explained by the line}} + \underbrace{(y_i-\widehat y_i)}_{\text{residual}}. \]

Square each piece and sum over all observations. For ordinary least squares with an intercept, the cross-terms cancel and the identity holds exactly:

\[ \underbrace{\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}_{\text{SST: total}} = \underbrace{\sum_i(\widehat y_i-\bar y)^2}_{\text{SSR: explained}} + \underbrace{\sum_i(y_i-\widehat y_i)^2}_{\text{SSE: residual}}. \]

Live, on the deal model, with the identity checked:

y <- deals$deal_1k
yhat <- fitted(deal_model)

SST <- sum((y - mean(y))^2)
SSR <- sum((yhat - mean(y))^2)
SSE <- sum((y - yhat)^2)

c(SST = SST, SSR = SSR, SSE = SSE, check = SSR + SSE)
      SST       SSR       SSE     check 
103509324  80441122  23068202 103509324

The units are squared thousands of dollars, so the raw magnitudes (around \(10^8\)) mean little on their own — the sums of squares matter only relative to each other, which is exactly what the next section computes.

Example 19.2 (Worked example: from SST and SSE to SSR and R²) A regression output reports \(\text{SST} = 500\) and \(\text{SSE} = 125\). Find SSR and \(R^2\).

By the identity, \(\text{SSR} = \text{SST} - \text{SSE} = 500 - 125 = 375\). Then

\[ R^2 = \frac{\text{SSR}}{\text{SST}} = \frac{375}{500} = 0.75 \qquad\text{or equivalently}\qquad R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}} = 1 - \frac{125}{500} = 0.75. \]

The fitted values account for 75% of the observed sample variation in the outcome; 25% remains as residual variation around the line. In class, the one-click Mentimeter check on this calculation is posed right before the break.

R²: what 0.777 means — and four things it is not

The general definition, applied to the live numbers:

\[ R^2=\frac{\text{SSR}}{\text{SST}} =1-\frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}}. \]

c(
  manual = 1 - SSE / SST,
  from_R = summary(deal_model)$r.squared,
  correlation_squared = cor(deals$ask_1k, deals$deal_1k)^2
)
             manual              from_R correlation_squared 
          0.7771389           0.7771389           0.7771389

Here \(R^2 \approx 0.777\): about 77.7% of the observed sample variation in agreed on-air deal amounts, among deal records, is explained by the linear association with the amount requested. The third number above shows a simple-regression bonus fact: with an intercept, \(R^2 = r_{XY}^2\), the squared correlation. Squaring discards the sign, so inspect the slope or correlation for direction.

\(R^2\) is one of the most misquoted numbers in applied work. Four things it is not, each with a wrong sentence corrected. The first two are worked here; the last two are yours.

Not the percentage of correct predictions. Wrong: “The model predicts 77.7% of deal amounts correctly.” Corrected: the model explains 77.7% of the variation in deal amounts; individual predictions can still miss by hundreds of thousands of dollars (the residual standard error is about $162,000).

Not proof that the assumptions hold. Wrong: “With \(R^2\) this high, the model is clearly well specified.” Corrected: \(R^2\) measures fit, not validity — Lecture 2 found a residual fan and influential pitches in this very model, and 0.777 is silent about both.

Sentence 3 (you). “Asking explains 78% of the deal, so raising your ask will raise your deal.”

Sentence 4 (you). “The model will explain 77.7% of deal-amount variation in next year’s pitches too.”

Check your answers

Sentence 3 claims causation: \(R^2\) describes an association among observed deals; requests were not randomly assigned, and nothing in a fit statistic says what would happen if a founder changed their ask. Sentence 4 claims out-of-sample performance: \(R^2\) is computed on this sample; a new season, a different selection of pitches, or a changed negotiation culture can all lower it. In-sample fit is evidence about these 882 records, not a guarantee for the next 882.

The overall F-test

The last part of the driving question: is the model useful at all, compared with predicting \(\bar y\) for everyone? For a regression with \(k\) predictors, the overall usefulness test is

\[ H_0:\beta_1=\cdots=\beta_k=0 \]

against the alternative that at least one slope is non-zero. The statistic is

\[ F=\frac{\text{SSR}/k}{\text{SSE}/(n-k-1)}. \]

Read the ratio the way it is built: the numerator is explained variation per predictor; the denominator is residual variation per residual degree of freedom. F asks how many times more explanation the model buys, per coefficient spent, than leftover noise would suggest. Values near 1 are what pure noise delivers; large values are evidence that the predictors jointly matter.

The output reports \(F \approx 3069\) on 1 and 880 degrees of freedom, with a p-value too small to print meaningfully. Rejecting the null says the model with requested amount fits better than an intercept-only model, under the inferential conditions.

In simple regression, F and t answer the same question. With one predictor and a two-sided alternative, \(H_0:\beta_1 = 0\) is the entire joint null, and

\[ F = t^2. \]

t_slope <- summary(deal_model)$coefficients["ask_1k", "t value"]
f_model <- unname(summary(deal_model)$fstatistic["value"])
c(t_squared = t_slope^2, F = f_model)
t_squared         F 
 3068.648  3068.648

Both equal about 3069. This equivalence dies in multiple regression, where the F-test evaluates several slopes jointly while each t-test evaluates one — Lecture 5 builds a genuine joint test on exactly that difference.

Usefulness has more than one meaning

“Is the model useful?” has at least four distinct readings, and a model can pass one while failing another:

  • Statistically non-zero slope, low \(R^2\). A loyalty-programme analysis on 400,000 customers finds a reliably positive effect of mailings on spending — real, precisely estimated, and explaining 1% of spending variation. Useful for a mass decision, useless for predicting any one customer.
  • High \(R^2\), poor causal interpretation. Store revenue regressed on staff count fits beautifully — because both track store size. Hiring more staff at a small store will not produce a big store’s revenue.
  • Good in-sample fit, fragile out-of-sample prediction. A travel-demand model fitted on 2015–2019 data explained bookings superbly and collapsed in 2020. In-sample \(R^2\) never promised stability.
  • Accurate predictions, no structural story. A black-box model may forecast weekly orders extremely well while its coefficients support no explanation of why — fine for stocking decisions, useless for designing a price change.

Always align the evaluation with the goal: explanation, prediction, or decision support.

Optional: robust standard errors
Heteroskedasticity-consistent, or robust, standard errors relax the constant-variance assumption when computing uncertainty. The fitted coefficients remain the ordinary least-squares values; only the estimated SEs (and hence t values, p-values, and intervals) change. Given Lecture 2’s residual fan, they are a natural upgrade for this model:

# Run this if these packages are available in the course R environment
library(lmtest)
library(sandwich)

coeftest(deal_model, vcov. = vcovHC(deal_model, type = "HC1"))

They fix exactly one thing: a misspecified conditional error variance. They do not repair non-linearity, omitted-variable bias, dependence across rows, influential data errors, or non-random selection. A robust SE on a biased slope is a precise measurement of the wrong quantity.

What a precise slope and a high R² can and cannot say

Every number this lecture produced — the SE of 0.0182, the interval \((0.973, 1.044)\), the p-value, \(R^2 = 0.777\), \(F \approx 3069\) — is computed under the model conditions of Lecture 2, and the diagnostics there flagged two of them: the residual spread grows with deal size, and a handful of extreme pitches steer the fit. So the classical SE (and everything built from it) rests on a doubtful variance assumption, and both the slope and \(R^2\) are partly the work of a few identifiable records. Beyond that, the sample is on-air deals only — selection travels with every estimate — and none of today’s statistics moves the association any closer to a causal claim. Precision and fit are internal report cards, not external validation.

Answering the driving question

Precisely, substantially, and yes — with conditions. The slope 1.0085 has standard error 0.0182, so the 95% interval \((0.973, 1.044)\) pins the association down to within about seven cents per dollar asked; the t-test rejects a zero slope beyond any doubt (\(t \approx 55.4\)). The line explains 77.7% of the variation in agreed amounts, and the overall F-test (\(F \approx 3069\)) confirms the model beats a flat mean. Every one of those statements is conditional on the Lecture 2 diagnostics — the fan and the influential pitches — and none of them is causal.

Check your answer

A complete conclusion reports: one later season is associated with about 0.824 percentage points less initial equity offered (SE 0.048); the 95% interval \((-0.917, -0.731)\) excludes zero and the t-test rejects a zero slope (\(t\approx-17.3\), p \(<\) 0.001); season explains about 17.3% of observed equity-offer variation, and the overall F-test (\(F\approx300.7 = t^2\)) rejects the intercept-only model. Scope: an observational time trend among recorded pitches — not a causal effect of season, and the classical SEs assume the Lecture 2 conditions.

The residual-versus-fitted plot of the deal model fans out to the right. Which of today’s printed numbers does that pattern undermine most directly, and why?

Check your answer

The classical standard error 0.0182 — and with it the t statistic, p-value, and confidence interval built from it. Worked exam interpretation (5 minutes)

Question (mock exam Question 3 pattern). A model of venture sales growth (percentage points) on marketing spending (€10,000s), fitted on \(n = 102\) ventures, reports a slope of 1.38 with standard error 0.46, \(R^2 = 0.083\), and \(F = 9.0\). Build the 95% interval for the slope, decide the 5% test against zero, verify the F statistic from the t statistic, and state in one sentence what the low \(R^2\) does and does not imply.

Check a model answer

\(t = 1.38/0.46 = 3.0\); with \(df = 100\) the multiplier is about 1.98, so the interval is \(1.38 \pm 1.98\times0.46 \approx (0.47,\; 2.29)\) percentage points per €10,000. The interval excludes zero, so the two-sided 5% test rejects \(H_0:\beta_1=0\); consistently, \(F = t^2 = 9.0\). The low \(R^2\) says marketing spending explains about 8% of growth variation — predictions for individual ventures will be poor — but it does not undo the evidence that the association is non-zero, and neither number makes the association causal.
