Tutorial 1: Simple regression, diagnostics, and model usefulness

By the end of Tutorial 1, you can

  • fit a simple regression in R and interpret its results with units,
  • check the assumptions of a model with residual plots and influence measures,
  • judge a model with its coefficient table, \(R^2\), and the F-test,
  • check an AI reading of a regression output and correct its errors.

Tutorials are exam preparation sessions. The tasks below are exam-style questions. You take the exam in TestVision, and R is available. Try each task yourself before you look at the solution. You may use AI for this, following Attempt, Ask, Verify, Explain.

In the tutorial

A complete written answer does five things. It names the sample, shows the calculation, and reports values with units. It interprets the values in context. And it states one important limit of the model or of the data. More practice for home is in the final section of this page, Extra practice for home.

All six tasks follow one founder question through the steps of Lectures 1 to 3: when founders offer more equity, how much equity do they end up giving in the deal? Before a pitch, every founder decides which share of the company to offer. On Shark Tank the sharks often accept the amount of money but ask for a larger share in return. A founder who knows how the final share is related to the opening offer can choose the offer with the likely final share in mind, and can decide in advance how far to go in the negotiation.

All tasks use the Shark Tank teaching file. The code below loads the data and keeps the 882 pitches that ended in a deal on air (deals). Two variables answer the question, both in percent of the company: the equity that the founder offered at the start of the pitch (equity_offered_pct) and the equity that the founder gave in the deal (deal_equity_pct). A difference between two shares is measured in percentage points. Review percent and percentage points if you need to. In eight of the deals the sharks invested without taking a share of the company, so the file records a deal share of 0%. These deals stay in the sample, because 0% is the equity that these founders gave. The last line prints the number of pitches and the number of deals.

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
deals <- subset(sharks, deal_on_show == 1)   # pitches with a deal on air
c(pitches = nrow(sharks), deals = nrow(deals))
pitches   deals 
   1441     882

Filled-in structure:

  • R code: see the code above.
  • First problem, and what in the plots shows it: the residuals lie in a narrow band at the smallest fitted values and spread out more at larger fitted values. Their standard deviation grows from about 3 percentage points for offers below 5% to about 11 for offers of 5% to 20% and 18 for offers above 20%. This widening band shows heteroskedasticity, so it challenges homoskedasticity.
  • Second problem, and what in the plots shows it: the Q-Q plot leaves the line at the right end, with standardised residuals up to about 7.4. Some deals lie far above the line, so the right tail is much heavier than a normal tail, which challenges normality.
  • Number with a standardised residual beyond ±3: 15 deals, about 1.7% of the 882.
  • Number with a Cook’s distance above 4/n: 34 deals, about 3.9% of the 882.
  • What most deals with a standardised residual above 3 have in common: in 14 of the 15 the founder gave far more than the fitted share, and in 8 of them the sharks bought the whole company (a deal share of 100%).
  • Offers and deal shares at a multiple of 5%: 745 offers (84%) and 686 deal shares (78%) are a multiple of 5%. Another 49 deal shares are about one third of the company, recorded as 33% or 33.3%.
  • How the data were generated, and why the bunching is not a violation: founders and sharks name these shares on the spot during a televised negotiation, so they choose round numbers such as 10%, 20%, 25%, a third, or a half. A share calculated with a valuation method would not bunch at these values. The bunching only shows that the offers and the deal shares take few distinct values, and it does not by itself challenge linearity, homoskedasticity, or normality.
  • Assumption the plots cannot assess, and what would assess it: the plots cannot assess independence. To assess it, you need knowledge of how the data were collected, for example whether deals in the same episode were negotiated by the same sharks on the same day.

You may also notice that the 42 deals with an offer below 5% lie mostly below the line. Their average residual is about \(-5.6\) percentage points, so the straight line overstates the deal share for the smallest offers.

Filled-in structure:

  • R code: see the code above.
  • Test, hypotheses: \(H_0:\beta_1=0\), \(H_1:\beta_1\ne0\). The significance level is 5%.
  • Test, test statistic: \(T=\hat\beta_1/\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\), with \(882-2=880\) degrees of freedom.
  • Test, critical value and rejection region: \(t_{0.975,\,880}\) = qt(0.975, 880) = 1.963. Reject \(H_0\) if \(|t_{obs}|\ge1.963\).
  • Test, observed value: the slope is \(1.207\) with standard error \(0.0511\), so \(t_{obs} \approx 23.6\).
  • Test, conclusion: since \(23.6\ge1.963\), reject a zero slope at the 5% level. Founders who offered a larger share gave a larger share in the deal, on average. The p-value, far below 0.001, gives the same decision.
  • Interval, formula: \(\hat\beta_1\pm t_{0.975,\,880}\,\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\).
  • Interval, given values: \(\hat\beta_1=1.2073\), \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=0.05114\), \(t_{0.975,\,880}=1.963\). One more digit than usual avoids rounding error.
  • Interval, calculations: \(1.2073 \pm 1.963 \times 0.05114 = 1.2073 \pm 0.1004\).
  • Interval, lower and upper bound: \((1.107,\; 1.308)\), in percentage points given per percentage point offered.
  • Interval, comparison with the output: the interval by hand matches the interval in the output.
  • Interval interpretation: under the model assumptions, the data are consistent with a population slope between about 1.11 and 1.31 percentage points of equity given per extra percentage point offered. For an offer one standard deviation (7.67 points) higher, this is about 8.5 to 10.0 points more equity given, a narrow range of large values.
  • Slope of 1, inside the interval or not: 1 lies below the lower bound of 1.107, so it is outside the interval.
  • Slope of 1, observed test statistic: \(t_{obs}=(1.2073-1)/0.05114\approx4.05\). Since \(4.05\ge1.963\), reject \(H_0:\beta_1=1\) at the 5% level, the same decision as the interval gives.
  • Slope of 1, conclusion in business terms: founders who offered more gave more than one extra point per point offered, so the extra share that the sharks take on top of the offer grows with the offer, from about \(8.32+0.207\times10\approx10.4\) points at an offer of 10% to about 12.5 points at an offer of 20%.
  • Robust standard errors, yes or no, and why: no. Robust standard errors address the heteroskedasticity only. They do not change the estimated line and do not address the heavy right tail of the residuals. With 882 deals the non-normality matters little for the tests, but the deals far above the line can still move the fit, which Task 5 checks.

The code computes the three sums of squares from the observed and the fitted values, and then prints \(R^2\) by hand, \(R^2\) from the output, the overall F statistic, and the squared t statistic of the slope.

y <- deals$deal_equity_pct
yhat <- fitted(equity_model)

SSE <- sum((y - yhat)^2)
SSR <- sum((yhat - mean(y))^2)

Filled-in structure:
