Tutorial 1: Simple regression, diagnostics, and model usefulness

By the end of Tutorial 1, you can

  • fit a simple regression in R and interpret its results with units
  • check the assumptions of a model with residual plots and influence measures
  • judge a model with its coefficient table, \(R^2\), and the F-test

Tutorials are exam preparation sessions. The tasks below are exam-style questions. You take the exam in TestVision, and R is available. You first try each task in pairs. You may use AI for this, following Attempt, Ask, Verify, Explain. Then the class discusses the solution together.

The tasks follow the order of the course. Tasks 1 and 2 use Lecture 1, Task 3 uses Lecture 2, and Tasks 4 and 5 use Lecture 3. Task 6 applies all steps to a new question.

Before class

Complete the after-class practice from Lectures 1 and 2. Lecture 3 is on the day before this tutorial: read its lecture material, and do its practice after the tutorial. Keep the Lectures 1–3 sections of the QM Intermediate Code Sheet open while you work. If the notation slows you down, review Mathematics Refresher: straight lines, slopes, and intercepts and powers, roots, and squared deviations.

Write from memory:

  1. the simple linear regression model
  2. the fitted line
  3. the residual
  4. the formula for \(R^2\)
  5. the null hypothesis of the overall model F-test.
Check your preparation
  1. \(Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+\varepsilon_i\).
  2. \(\widehat Y_i=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1X_i\).
  3. \(e_i=Y_i-\widehat Y_i\).
  4. \(R^2=1-\text{SSE}/\text{SST}=\text{SSR}/\text{SST}\).
  5. \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) in simple regression (all slopes zero in general).

In the tutorial

Try each task in R before you open the answer. A complete written answer does five things. It names the sample, shows the calculation, and reports values with units. It interprets the values in context. And it states one important limit of the model or of the data. Tasks 1 to 6 belong to the tutorial.

All tasks use the Shark Tank teaching file and the valuations of Lectures 1 to 3, in millions of dollars: the valuation agreed on air (agreed_m) and the valuation claimed by the founder (claimed_m). Build the data once, in the same way as in Lectures 1–3:

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
vals <- subset(sharks, deal_on_show == 1 & deal_equity_pct > 0)
vals$claimed_m <- vals$requested_valuation_usd / 1e6
vals$agreed_m <- vals$deal_valuation_usd / 1e6
nrow(vals)   # deals for a share of the company
[1] 874

Task 1: Fit and interpret (Lecture 1)

Shark Tank data Fit agreed_m ~ claimed_m among the deals for a share of the company. Report the sample, the fitted equation, the interpretation of the slope with units, and the limit of the intercept’s meaning.

Check your answer 
val_model <- lm(agreed_m ~ claimed_m, data = vals)
coef(val_model)
(Intercept)   claimed_m 
-0.06798986  0.64874084

Among the 874 deals for a share of the company, the fitted equation is \(\widehat y = -0.068 + 0.649x\), with both valuations in $ millions. A claimed valuation that is $1 million higher is associated with an agreed valuation about $0.65 million higher. A claimed valuation of zero is outside the observed range, so the intercept (about \(-\$68{,}000\)) has no useful business meaning of its own.

Task 2: Fitted value and residual (Lecture 1)

Shark Tank data A founder claimed a valuation of $5 million and the deal closed at a valuation of $2.5 million.

  1. Calculate the fitted value of this deal.
  2. Calculate the residual of this deal.
  3. Interpret the sign of the residual.
Check your answer

With \(x=5\) (in $ millions), \(\widehat y = -0.068 + 0.649 \times 5 \approx 3.18\), or about $3.18 million. The observed \(y = 2.5\), so \(e = 2.5 - 3.18 = -0.68\), about \(-\$680{,}000\). The negative sign means this deal closed below the model’s fitted conditional mean for a claimed valuation of $5 million.

val_model <- lm(agreed_m ~ claimed_m, data = vals)
unname(2.5 - predict(val_model, newdata = data.frame(claimed_m = 5)))
[1] -0.6757143

Task 3: Diagnostics and influence (Lecture 2)

Shark Tank data Produce the residual-versus-fitted plot and the normal Q–Q plot for the Task 1 model.

  1. Name two problems with the assumptions that you can see in the plots.
  2. Count the standardised residuals beyond \(\pm 3\) and the observations with a Cook’s distance above \(4/n\).
  3. State the three-step rule for handling a flagged observation.
  4. Name one assumption that these plots cannot assess, and say what information would assess it.
Check your answer 
val_model <- lm(agreed_m ~ claimed_m, data = vals)
old_par <- par(no.readonly = TRUE)
par(mfrow = c(1, 2), mar = c(4.5, 4.4, 1.3, 0.7), las = 1)
plot(fitted(val_model), residuals(val_model),
     pch = 16, col = grDevices::adjustcolor("#0072B2", alpha.f = 0.35),
     bty = "n", xlab = "Fitted agreed valuation ($ millions)",
     ylab = "Residual ($ millions)")
abline(h = 0, lty = 2, lwd = 2, col = "#D55E00")
qqnorm(rstandard(val_model), pch = 16,
       col = grDevices::adjustcolor("#009E73", alpha.f = 0.40),
       bty = "n", main = "", xlab = "Normal theoretical quantile",
       ylab = "Standardised residual")
qqline(rstandard(val_model), col = "#D55E00", lwd = 2)
par(old_par)
c(big_rstandard = sum(abs(rstandard(val_model)) > 3),
  cooks_flagged = sum(cooks.distance(val_model) > 4 / nobs(val_model)))
big_rstandard cooks_flagged 
           25            64

The spread of the residuals widens with the fitted value. This fan challenges constant conditional variance. The Q–Q plot leaves the line at both ends, with several extreme residuals on both sides. This pattern challenges normality and suggests that some deals may be influential. Twenty-five standardised residuals lie beyond \(\pm3\), and 64 observations have a Cook’s distance above \(4/n\). The rule for flagged observations: investigate, report, never silently delete. The plots cannot assess independence or the absence of confounding. To assess them, you need knowledge of how the data were collected, for example whether the same sharks or founders appear in several rows.

Task 4: Coefficient inference (Lecture 3)

Shark Tank data Use summary() and confint() on the Task 1 model.

  1. Test \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) against the two-sided alternative.
  2. Report the 95% confidence interval for \(\beta_1\), and verify it by hand from the estimate and the standard error.
  3. Would robust standard errors (Lecture 3) fix both problems from Task 3?
Check your answer 
val_model <- lm(agreed_m ~ claimed_m, data = vals)
summary(val_model)$coefficients
               Estimate Std. Error    t value      Pr(>|t|)
(Intercept) -0.06798986 0.06847892 -0.9928582  3.210545e-01
claimed_m    0.64874084 0.01106883 58.6096854 1.099793e-304
confint(val_model)
                 2.5 %     97.5 %
(Intercept) -0.2023926 0.06641292
claimed_m    0.6270162 0.67046551

The slope is \(0.649\) with standard error \(0.0111\), so \(t_{obs} \approx 58.6\) on \(872\) degrees of freedom. The critical value is \(t_{0.975,\,872}\) = qt(0.975, 872) = 1.963. Since \(58.6\ge1.963\), reject a zero slope at the 5% level. The p-value, far below 0.001, gives the same decision. By hand, with one more digit to avoid rounding error, \(0.6487 \pm 1.963 \times 0.01107 = 0.6487 \pm 0.0217 = (0.627,\; 0.670)\). This matches confint(), in $ million agreed per $ million claimed. Robust standard errors address the unequal variance only. They do not help with influential observations, with the selection of pitches that ended in a deal, or with a relationship that is not linear.

Task 5: R² and F (Lecture 3)

  1. Shark Tank data Calculate SST, SSE, SSR, and \(R^2\) manually.
  2. Verify them against summary().
  3. Verify \(F = t^2\).
  4. Write one concluding sentence that does not claim more than the data show.
Check your answer 
val_model <- lm(agreed_m ~ claimed_m, data = vals)
y <- vals$agreed_m
yhat <- fitted(val_model)
SST <- sum((y - mean(y))^2)
SSE <- sum((y - yhat)^2)
SSR <- sum((yhat - mean(y))^2)
c(R2_manual = 1 - SSE / SST,
  R2_summary = summary(val_model)$r.squared,
  F_summary = unname(summary(val_model)$fstatistic["value"]),
  t_squared = summary(val_model)$coefficients["claimed_m", "t value"]^2)
   R2_manual   R2_summary    F_summary    t_squared 
   0.7975434    0.7975434 3435.0952236 3435.0952236

The sums (in squared $ millions) satisfy \(\text{SST}=\text{SSR}+\text{SSE}\), and \(R^2 \approx 0.798\). The overall F statistic is about 3435, equal to the squared slope t statistic, as it must be in simple regression. A sentence that does not claim more than the data show: among these deals, the claimed valuation explains about 80% of the variation in the agreed valuations, but this is an association in a selected set of deals, partly driven by a few influential deals, and not the effect of changing a claim.

Task 6: A new question: how much more equity do the sharks take?

Shark Tank data Lecture 1 showed that the sharks usually pay the amount that the founder asked for and take a larger share of the company. Model this pattern directly. Use the 874 deals for a share of the company. Regress the equity agreed in the deal on the equity that the founder first offered, both in percent of the company:

all_deals <- subset(sharks, deal_on_show == 1 & deal_equity_pct > 0)
equity_model <- lm(deal_equity_pct ~ equity_offered_pct, data = all_deals)
nrow(all_deals)
[1] 874
summary(equity_model)$coefficients
                   Estimate Std. Error  t value     Pr(>|t|)
(Intercept)        8.519643 0.73993946 11.51397 1.173912e-28
equity_offered_pct 1.207227 0.05053385 23.88948 1.975473e-97
confint(equity_model)
                      2.5 %   97.5 %
(Intercept)        7.067372 9.971913
equity_offered_pct 1.108045 1.306410
summary(equity_model)$r.squared
[1] 0.3955808
  1. Interpret the slope and the intercept with units.
  2. A founder offers 10% of the company. Which agreed share does the model give as the fitted value?
  3. Compare the \(R^2\) with the valuation model. What does the difference tell a founder?
  4. Advise the founder in two sentences.
Check your answer

The slope is about 1.21 percentage points of agreed equity per percentage point offered, with a 95% interval of about 1.11 to 1.31. The intercept is about 8.5%, the fitted agreed share for an offer of 0%. No founder offers 0%, so the intercept has no business meaning of its own. For a founder who offers 10%, the fitted share that the founder gives up is \(8.52 + 1.21\times10\approx20.6\)% of the company, about twice the offer. \(R^2\) is about 0.40, against 0.80 for the valuations. The offered share says much less about the agreed share than the claimed valuation says about the agreed valuation. So single deals are spread widely around the line. Advice: for an offer of 10%, expect to give up about 20%, and decide before the pitch how large a share you are willing to give up. These are deals that happened on a television show. They describe what founders who closed a deal gave up, not what would happen to you if you offered a different share.

After class

Individual practice

Shark Tank data Fit equity_offered_pct ~ season using all 1,441 pitches.

  1. Make and read the scatterplot with the fitted line.
  2. Report the fitted equation and units.
  3. Report the standard error of the slope, its t value, its p-value, and its 95% interval.
  4. Report \(R^2\) and the overall F-test.
  5. Inspect the residual plots and write a conclusion of about 120 words.
Check a model answer 
sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
season_model <- lm(equity_offered_pct ~ season, data = sharks)
summary(season_model)$coefficients
              Estimate Std. Error   t value      Pr(>|t|)
(Intercept) 20.4565874 0.46442667  44.04697 4.922706e-269
season      -0.8241241 0.04752298 -17.34159  2.514546e-61
confint(season_model)
                 2.5 %     97.5 %
(Intercept) 19.5455616 21.3676132
season      -0.9173458 -0.7309023
c(r_squared = summary(season_model)$r.squared,
  F = unname(summary(season_model)$fstatistic["value"]))
  r_squared           F 
  0.1728605 300.7308055

The fitted equation is \(\widehat{\text{equity}}=20.457-0.824\,\text{season}\): one later season is associated with about 0.824 percentage points less initial equity offered on average. The standard error is about 0.048, \(t\approx-17.3\), and the p-value is below 0.001. The 95% interval \((-0.917,\,-0.731)\) excludes zero. \(R^2\approx0.173\), so the season explains about 17.3% of the variation in the equity offered, and the overall F-test (\(F\approx300.7=t^2\)) reaches the same conclusion as the slope test. The residual plot shows sixteen vertical stripes (the season takes whole numbers only) with broadly similar spread. The Q–Q plot bends upwards at the right end because of some very high offers. This is a trend over time among recorded pitches, not a causal effect of a later season.

Complete from memory:

  1. A residual is …
  2. The slope’s standard error estimates …
  3. A fan in the residual plot challenges …
  4. \(R^2\) is the proportion of …
  5. In simple regression, the overall F statistic equals …
Check your answers
  1. The observed outcome minus the fitted value, \(e_i=Y_i-\widehat Y_i\).
  2. How much the estimated slope would vary from sample to sample, under the model assumptions.
  3. Constant conditional variance (homoskedasticity), and with it the usual standard errors.

Make it yours

Two sentences about your own venture

Think of your EiA3 venture, or any business you know well. Imagine the one simple regression you would show an investor (revenue on customers, orders on advertising spending). Write two sentences. First, name the number from this tutorial that you would put in the headline (slope, interval, or \(R^2\)), and say why. Second, name the limit from Task 3 or Task 5 that you would state openly next to that number. There is no wrong answer.

See an example, then write your own

Take a student venture that sells boxes of prepared meals on campus. “Headline: the slope with its 95% interval. Each extra €100 of advertising is associated with about 12 to 19 more orders. An investor wants to know how many orders the money brings, and the interval shows how uncertain the number is. Limit: the fan in my residual plot shows that orders vary more in busy weeks. So the interval depends on an assumption that my own residual plot puts in doubt.”

You can discuss this task, and any other task in this tutorial, with Tilly, the course AI assistant: paste your two sentences and ask it to act as an investor who doubts your numbers.
