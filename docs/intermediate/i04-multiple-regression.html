Lecture 4: Multiple regression and what controls do

By the end of Lecture 4, you can

  • state a multiple linear regression model;
  • interpret a coefficient while holding the other included predictors fixed;
  • explain, with a constructed example, why simple and multiple-regression coefficients can differ;
  • fit and compare multiple-regression specifications in R;
  • scale a coefficient to a comparison a reader cares about;
  • distinguish statistical control from causal identification.

Driving question

Two stories can produce the same pattern: perhaps equity offers genuinely fell over time, or perhaps later pitches simply ask for different amounts. Separating overlapping explanations is the core job of multiple regression — and of most business analytics.

Later seasons show smaller equity offers. Is that a time story, or is it really a money story in disguise, since later pitches also ask for different amounts?

In class

The session opens with the usual four-minute entry quiz on the prep videos, then turns to the driving question. Your warm-up produced two season coefficients — about \(-0.824\) without the requested amount and \(-0.814\) with it. This lecture explains what the second number means, why the two can differ, and why here they barely do.

More than one predictor

Stanford explanation (review): 3.3 Multiple Linear Regression.

For predictors \(X_{i1},\ldots,X_{ik}\), the model is

\[ Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_{i1}+\cdots+\beta_kX_{ik}+\varepsilon_i, \]

with conditional mean

\[ E[Y_i\mid X_{i1}=x_1,\ldots,X_{ik}=x_k] =\beta_0+\beta_1x_1+\cdots+\beta_kx_k. \]

The coefficient \(\beta_j\) is the expected change in \(Y\) for a one-unit increase in \(X_j\), holding the other included predictors fixed. That phrase must appear in every multiple-regression slope interpretation you write in this course.

It describes a model-based comparison. It does not require that two real pitches exist that are identical on every feature; it compares fitted conditional means at fixed values of the included variables. The model does the holding fixed — the data need not contain twins.

Why coefficients change: a constructed demonstration

Before touching the Shark Tank data, watch the mechanism operate where we control it completely, because we built the data ourselves. Imagine 200 small firms. A single background variable — firm size — drives both advertising spend and revenue. Advertising itself contributes nothing: the revenue line below contains size but no adspend term.

set.seed(42)
n <- 200
size    <- rnorm(n, mean = 10, sd = 2)          # Z: firm size (drives both)
adspend <- 2 + 0.8 * size + rnorm(n, sd = 1)    # X: bigger firms spend more
revenue <- 5 + 1.5 * size + rnorm(n, sd = 1.5)  # Y: size drives revenue,
demo <- data.frame(revenue, adspend, size)      #    adspend does not
coef(lm(revenue ~ adspend, data = demo))
(Intercept)     adspend 
   5.957361    1.391909 
coef(lm(revenue ~ adspend + size, data = demo))
(Intercept)     adspend        size 
  5.0975426   0.1007622   1.3804961

The simple slope of adspend is about \(1.39\): strongly positive, and highly significant if you check its t statistic. Yet we know advertising does nothing here. Once size enters the model, the adspend slope collapses to about \(0.10\) with a p-value around \(0.39\) — indistinguishable from the zero we built in. The simple slope was entirely an omitted-variable artefact: adspend earned credit for the size differences it was correlated with.

Figure 21.1 makes the mechanism visible. Larger firms (orange) sit high on both axes; smaller firms (blue) sit low on both. Sliding along the overall cloud from left to right, you move from small firms to large firms — so the simple slope compares different kinds of firms. Within each colour, the cloud is nearly flat: at a given size, spending more is not associated with more revenue.

A coefficient changes when an added predictor accounts for variation that the simpler association had absorbed. That is the entire mechanism — and it is why “holding the other included predictors fixed” is not decoration but the substance of the estimate.

The real-data contrast: the season slope barely moves

Now the same comparison on the Shark Tank data. The running money unit from here on is thousands of dollars: ask_1k is the requested amount divided by 1,000, so one unit is $1,000 and 100 units are $100,000 (Lecture 1 showed how a coefficient rescales when the unit changes).

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
sharks$ask_1k <- sharks$ask_amount_usd / 1000
m_simple   <- lm(equity_offered_pct ~ season, data = sharks)
m_adjusted <- lm(equity_offered_pct ~ season + ask_1k, data = sharks)
c(
  simple           = coef(m_simple)["season"],
  adjusted_for_ask = coef(m_adjusted)["season"]
)
          simple.season adjusted_for_ask.season 
             -0.8241241              -0.8143307

The season slope moves from about \(-0.824\) to about \(-0.814\): hardly at all. Contrast this with the constructed example, where adjustment destroyed the simple slope. Here, adjusting for the requested amount leaves the season trend essentially intact, so within these data the time story is not a disguised money story.

Read the stability correctly. It is informative: the measured requested amount does not account for the season trend. It is not proof that omitted-variable bias is absent: an unmeasured variable — venture maturity, founder experience, editorial selection — could still play exactly the role size played in the constructed data. Stability with respect to the variables you have says nothing about the variables you do not have.

The coefficient-interpretation protocol

Use the same five checks every time you interpret a slope:

  1. Estimate and direction: state the fitted change and its sign.
  2. Predictor step and unit: say what a one-unit increase means.
  3. Outcome unit: state the unit in which the outcome changes.
  4. Conditioning: name the other included predictors being held fixed.
  5. Scope: identify the analysed records and say “associated with” unless a research design justifies a causal claim.

The variables in this model use deliberately different units:

Term A one-unit increase means Coefficient unit
season one later season equity percentage points per season
ask_1k $1,000 more requested equity percentage points per $1,000
description_words one additional word equity percentage points per word

Applying the protocol to each slope in turn, always among the 1,441 recorded pitches:

  • Season. At the same requested amount and description length, one later season is associated with about \(0.836\) percentage points less initial equity offered.
  • Requested amount. At the same season and description length, an additional $1,000 requested is associated with about \(0.0029\) percentage points less initial equity offered. The number is tiny because the unit step is tiny; per additional $100,000 the association is about \(0.29\) percentage points, and its t statistic (about \(-5.0\)) does not depend on the unit chosen.
  • Description length. At the same season and request, one additional description word is associated with about \(0.128\) percentage points less initial equity offered. This estimate is not statistically distinguishable from zero at 5% (p \(\approx\) 0.26). Report the point estimate anyway: “not significant” is not the same as “exactly zero” — the data are simply consistent with a range of values that includes zero.
Check: the season slope was −0.814 with two predictors and −0.836 with three. Contradiction?

No. Each coefficient answers a different conditional question: \(-0.814\) holds only ask_1k fixed; \(-0.836\) holds ask_1k and description_words fixed. Adding a predictor changes what is being held fixed, so the coefficient may shift. Here the shift is small — the same stability, and the same limits to it, as before.

Scaling to comparisons readers care about

Nobody plans in single seasons of a TV show or single thousands of dollars. Scale the coefficient to the step a reader cares about: fitted change = coefficient × number of unit steps, holding the other included predictors fixed. This resolves the Mentimeter question posed before the break.

Example 21.1 (Worked example: five seasons, half a million dollars, ten words) Holding the other included predictors fixed in equity_model:

  • Five later seasons: \(5\times(-0.836)=-4.18\) fitted equity percentage points.
  • $500,000 more requested: \(500{,}000/1{,}000 = 500\) units of ask_1k, so \(500\times(-0.0029)=-1.45\) fitted percentage points.
  • Ten additional description words: \(10\times(-0.128)=-1.28\) fitted percentage points — with the reminder that this coefficient is not significantly different from zero, so the scaled version is not either.

Rescaling a predictor changes the numerical coefficient and its SE by the same factor, but not the fitted values, t statistic, or substantive relationship. A “per dollar” coefficient of \(-0.0000029\) would be correct and unreadable; per $1,000 is readable on the data’s scale; per $100,000 is often the best unit for a sentence.

What controls can and cannot do

Including a control can:

  • make a comparison more like-for-like on measured dimensions;
  • reduce residual variation, tightening intervals;
  • reveal that a simple association was partly compositional;
  • increase uncertainty when predictors overlap strongly.

Including a control cannot:

  • adjust for unmeasured factors;
  • fix reverse causality;
  • repair selection into the sample;
  • remove measurement error.

A “control variable” is just an included predictor. Nothing about the word control grants it a causal role; only a research design can do that.

Check: which of these four problems does adding ask_1k address?
  1. later pitches request different amounts; (b) founder experience is not recorded; (c) equity offers may influence how much is asked; (d) only pitches that reached broadcast are in the file.

Only (a). It is a measured compositional difference, and holding ask_1k fixed removes it from the season comparison. Problems (b), (c), and (d) are unmeasured confounding, reverse causality, and sample selection — none of them touched by adding a measured control.

Assumptions extend to the full predictor set

For multiple regression, the Lecture 2 assumption list applies to the whole set of included predictors:

  1. the conditional mean is linear in the included terms;
  2. \(E[\varepsilon_i\mid X_{i1},\ldots,X_{ik}]=0\) — zero conditional mean at every combination of predictor values;
  3. observations are independent under the design;
  4. no predictor is an exact linear combination of the others;
  5. classical SEs require constant conditional error variance;
  6. exact small-sample t/F inference uses conditional normality.

High correlation among predictors — collinearity — does not bias OLS by itself, but it makes individual coefficients unstable and imprecise: the data contain little information to separate overlapping predictors. Exact linear dependence is worse: the coefficients are then not identified at all, and R drops a term. Instability is a precision problem; exact dependence is a no-answer-exists problem.

Read Figure 21.2 with the standing five-step sequence:

  1. Axes: rows are predictors; the horizontal axis is the estimated change in initial equity offered, in percentage points.
  2. Units: each row’s estimate is per one unit of that predictor — per season, per $1,000, per word. The rows are not on a common scale.
  3. Marks: a dot is the coefficient estimate; its horizontal line is the 95% confidence interval; the dashed line marks zero.
  4. Pattern: season and requested amount sit clearly left of zero; the description-words interval crosses zero. The ask_1k row hugs the zero line only because its unit step ($1,000) is tiny — its t statistic is about \(-5\). Visual size on this plot is a unit artefact, not evidence strength.
  5. Limits: intervals reflect model-based sampling uncertainty among these recorded pitches; they say nothing about causation or unmeasured variables.

What an adjusted coefficient can and cannot say

Every slope in equity_model is a model-based comparison among the 1,441 recorded pitches: fitted conditional means at fixed values of the included predictors. “Holding the other included predictors fixed” never extends to variables outside the model — maturity, revenue, founder experience, and editorial selection are all unheld. The stability of the season slope across specifications is informative about the measured controls only; it proves no absence of omitted-variable bias. Initial equity offered is an on-air opening position, not a completed investment, and none of these associations is a causal effect of time, money, or word count.

Answering the driving question

At the same requested amount (and description length), the season trend persists: about \(-0.836\) equity percentage points per season, versus \(-0.824\) unadjusted. The money story does not absorb the time story here — later pitches offer less equity even compared at the same ask. And neither story is causal: an unmeasured driver could still lie behind both, exactly as firm size did in the constructed example.

Next: We can now hold other predictors fixed — as long as they are numerical. But many of the most interesting business comparisons are categorical: channels, segments, team compositions. Lecture 5 shows how categories enter a regression correctly — dummy variables and a reference group chosen deliberately — and how to judge a whole set of category terms at once with the partial F-test, because individually insignificant dummies can still be jointly worth having.

Cumulative mini-task (10 minutes)

Write one paragraph connecting the Lecture 2 diagnostics to equity_model: which residual checks would you run on this three-predictor fit, what would each look for, and what would you do about a highly influential pitch if you found one?

Check a model answer

A residuals-versus-fitted plot checks that the conditional mean is roughly linear in the included terms and that spread is roughly constant; curvature would question assumption 1, a funnel shape would question the classical SEs. Standardised residuals (rstandard) flag pitches the model fits unusually badly, and Cook’s distance flags pitches that individually move the coefficients. A flagged pitch — say, an extreme requested amount — gets investigated, not deleted: check the recorded values, refit with and without it, and report both fits if they disagree. Silent deletion is never an option.

Spaced review: one item from Lecture 3 (5 minutes)

For the simple model equity_offered_pct ~ season, the slope’s t statistic is about \(-17.34\). What is the overall F statistic of this one-predictor model, and why?

Check your answer

\(F = t^2 \approx 300.7\). With a single slope, the overall F-test and the two-sided t-test of that slope test the same hypothesis, and F is exactly the square of t. With several predictors that equivalence disappears — which is why Lecture 5 needs a genuine joint test.

Worked exam interpretation (5 minutes)

Question. A summary table reports, for the three-predictor model, season -0.836 (SE 0.051) and description_words -0.128 (SE 0.114). A student writes: “Season matters and word count does not, so descriptions have no effect on equity.” Give two corrections in the mock-exam Q4 style.

Check a model answer

First, “no effect” misreads non-significance: the interval \((-0.351, 0.095)\) contains zero and values near \(-0.35\), so the data are consistent with no association and with a modest negative one — not significant is not zero. Second, “effect” claims causation; both estimates are associations among recorded pitches, holding the other included predictors fixed, with no design that identifies a causal effect.

Make it yours (5 minutes)

Utility: two sentences

Think of your EiA3 venture — or any business you know well. Name one outcome you care about and two of its drivers that are correlated with each other (say, price and quality tier, or marketing spend and firm age), and write two sentences: which comparison would a regression of the outcome on only one driver mix together, and what would “holding the other fixed” let you see?
