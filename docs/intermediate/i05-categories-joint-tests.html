Lecture 5: Categories as predictors and joint tests

By the end of Lecture 5, you can

  • encode a two-level or multi-level categorical predictor with dummy variables;
  • interpret dummy coefficients relative to a named reference group, in percentage points where the outcome is a percentage;
  • avoid the dummy-variable trap;
  • compute a partial F statistic by hand from an anova table and read the R output;
  • separate joint usefulness from individual significance;
  • separate adjusted associations from causal or essentialist claims.

Driving question

Markets come in categories: sales channels, customer segments, team compositions. Putting categories into a model correctly — and judging whether they belong in it at all — is a craft this lecture teaches.

Adjusted for season and ask, do all-women presenting teams offer different equity than all-men teams — and are the category terms worth having in the model at all?

In class

The session opens with the usual four-minute entry quiz on the prep videos, then turns to the driving question — and, before any model, to the measurement behind it.

Measurement first: what the category records

Stanford explanation (review): 3.5 Extensions of the Linear Model.

The source categories describe the presenting team as all men, all women, or mixed. They do not measure the share of women, gender identity beyond the source categories, or the composition of the full venture. Seven records with an unknown classification are excluded from every model in this lecture; the reason comes at the end. Every sentence this chapter produces inherits this measurement boundary.

A two-level indicator

For a binary predictor \(D_i\in\{0,1\}\),

\[ E[Y_i\mid D_i]=\beta_0+\beta_1D_i. \]

Then

\[ E[Y_i\mid D_i=0]=\beta_0, \qquad E[Y_i\mid D_i=1]=\beta_0+\beta_1. \]

Thus \(\beta_1\) is the expected difference between the 1 group and the 0 reference group. A dummy regression with one indicator is exactly a comparison of two group means, dressed as a regression.

Example 22.1 (Worked example: two group means from two coefficients) Restrict to the all-men and all-women teams (1,158 records) and let women_team equal 1 for all-women teams:

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
known_gender <- subset(sharks, pitcher_gender_group != "unknown")
two_groups <- subset(known_gender,
                     pitcher_gender_group %in% c("all_men", "all_women"))
two_groups$women_team <- as.integer(
  two_groups$pitcher_gender_group == "all_women"
)
coef(lm(equity_offered_pct ~ women_team, data = two_groups))
(Intercept)  women_team 
 13.1973739   0.8210677 
tapply(two_groups$equity_offered_pct, two_groups$women_team, mean)
       0        1 
13.19737 14.01844

Read the two coefficients as two ticks on the equity axis (Figure 22.1): the intercept \(\beta_0\approx 13.20\) is the all-men mean, and \(\beta_0+\beta_1\approx 13.20+0.82=14.02\) is the all-women mean. The slope \(\beta_1\approx 0.82\) is the gap between the ticks — nothing more.

More than two categories

With \(G\) categories and an intercept, use \(G-1\) dummies. Including all \(G\) dummies plus an intercept creates exact linear dependence, because the dummies sum to 1 in every row — the model from Lecture 4 whose coefficients are not identified. This is the dummy-variable trap.

R creates the dummies automatically when a predictor is a factor. Set the reference category deliberately and verify it — this is exactly what your warm-up built:

known_gender$gender <- relevel(factor(known_gender$pitcher_gender_group),
                               ref = "all_men")
known_gender$ask_1k <- known_gender$ask_amount_usd / 1000
levels(known_gender$gender)
[1] "all_men"   "all_women" "mixed"    
contrasts(known_gender$gender)
          all_women mixed
all_men           0     0
all_women         1     0
mixed             0     1

Each row of the contrasts table is a category; each column is one dummy. The reference category all_men is the all-zero row: it lives in the intercept.

Check: four industries and an intercept — how many dummies?

Three. One industry is the reference group carried by the intercept; the three dummy coefficients each compare one remaining industry with it. Four dummies plus an intercept would fall into the trap.

Percent versus percentage points

One habit must be in place before any coefficient is read. The outcome equity_offered_pct is measured in percent, so its regression coefficients are in percentage points — and the sentence “the all-women coefficient is 1.28%” is ambiguous. It could mean:

  • a relative change: offers 1.28% of the all-men level higher (13.0% would become about 13.2%); or
  • an absolute change of 1.28 percentage points: 13.0% becomes 14.28%.

These differ by a factor of about eight here. A coefficient on a percent-scaled outcome is always the absolute kind. The precise sentence replacing the ambiguous one is: “At the same season and requested amount, all-women presenting teams are fitted to offer 1.28 equity percentage points more initially than all-men presenting teams.” Every full-credit dummy interpretation in this course uses percentage points for this outcome.

Both dummy coefficients, each with the full sentence — categories named, unit, held-fixed set, sample boundary:

  • All-women versus all-men. Among pitches with a recorded source classification, all-women presenting teams are fitted to offer about 1.28 equity percentage points more initially than all-men presenting teams at the same season and requested amount (p \(\approx\) 0.008).
  • Mixed versus all-men. Among the same pitches, mixed teams are fitted to differ from all-men teams by about 0.12 percentage points at the same season and requested amount (p \(\approx\) 0.823) — an estimate not statistically distinguishable from zero.

Note the adjusted all-women gap (1.28 points) is larger than the raw gap from the worked example (0.82 points): adjusting for season and ask moved the comparison, which is precisely why the held-fixed set belongs in the sentence. These are two reference-group comparisons, not a claim that gender causes the offers.

Are the category terms worth having at all?

Block 1 read the coefficients one at a time. The driving question also asks something no single t-test answers: do the two category terms jointly add information beyond season and ask?

The logic needs two nested models — the reduced model is the complete model with the tested coefficients forced to zero, fitted on the same rows:

The hypotheses:

\[ H_0:\beta_{all\_women}=\beta_{mixed}=0 \quad\text{against at least one non-zero.} \]

Example 22.2 (Worked example: the partial F statistic by hand) Fit both models and ask R for the anova table:

reduced_gender <- lm(equity_offered_pct ~ season + ask_1k,
                     data = known_gender)
anova(reduced_gender, gender_model)
Analysis of Variance Table

Model 1: equity_offered_pct ~ season + ask_1k
Model 2: equity_offered_pct ~ season + ask_1k + gender
  Res.Df   RSS Df Sum of Sq      F Pr(>F)  
1   1431 83228                             
2   1429 82792  2    436.25 3.7649 0.0234 *
---
Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

Plug the table’s numbers into the formula once by hand. Adding the two category terms reduces the residual sum of squares by \(436.25\) using \(2\) degrees of freedom; the complete model leaves \(82{,}792\) on \(1{,}429\) degrees of freedom:

\[ F=\frac{436.25/2}{82{,}792/1{,}429} =\frac{218.13}{57.94} \approx 3.76. \]

Read the ratio: the numerator is explained variation per tested coefficient; the denominator is unexplained variation per residual degree of freedom. The category terms buy about 3.8 times as much explanation per coefficient as noise alone would — with p \(\approx\) 0.023, more than chance comfortably explains.

At 5%, the two source-category terms are jointly useful in this specification. Hold that next to the individual results: joint p-value \(0.023\), yet the mixed dummy’s own p-value is \(0.823\) in the very same output. Joint usefulness is not individual significance — the F-test asks whether the pair of terms together improves the model, not whether each survives on its own. (The overall F in summary(gender_model), about 84.5 with a tiny p-value, answers a third, cruder question: whether all four predictors jointly beat an intercept-only model.)

Visualise the adjusted comparison

library(ggplot2)
sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
known_gender <- subset(sharks, pitcher_gender_group != "unknown")
known_gender$gender <- relevel(factor(known_gender$pitcher_gender_group),

known_gender$ask_1k <- known_gender$ask_amount_usd / 1000

Read Figure 22.3 with the standing five-step sequence:

  1. Axes: source categories across, fitted initial equity (in percent) up.
  2. Units: each point is a fitted mean at the same average season and requested amount — an adjusted comparison, not raw group means.
  3. Marks: points are fitted values (about 12.9, 14.1, and 13.0); vertical lines are 95% confidence intervals, wider for the smaller categories.
  4. Pattern: the all-women point sits about 1.28 points above the all-men point; the mixed interval overlaps both.
  5. Limits: the intervals express model-based sampling uncertainty. They say nothing about the recording uncertainty in the broad source categories themselves, and nothing causal.

Why unknown is excluded, not a fourth group

Seven records carry the classification unknown. That label records the absence of information, not a kind of team: treating it as a substantive fourth category would estimate a “mean equity of teams the source failed to classify,” a quantity with no business meaning. Excluding the records keeps every coefficient interpretable; the price, stated openly, is that the analysed sample is the classified records only.

What these category coefficients can and cannot say

The categories are broad source codings of the on-screen presenting team — not shares, identities, or full founding teams — so the coefficients compare codings, not people. The analysed sample is the 1,434 classified records of broadcast pitches; selection into the show and into classification travels with every estimate. The two dummy coefficients are two reference-group contrasts at fixed season and ask, and they change labels when the reference changes. None of this supports an essentialist or causal reading: the model reports that offers differ by coded composition in these data, not that composition makes anyone offer differently.

Answering the driving question

At the same season and requested amount, all-women presenting teams are fitted to offer about 1.28 equity percentage points more than all-men teams (p \(\approx\) 0.008); mixed teams are indistinguishable from all-men teams. The two category terms are jointly useful (partial F \(\approx\) 3.76, p \(\approx\) 0.023), so they earn their place in the model — as an adjusted association within these recorded pitches, nothing more.

Next: We can now compare categories at fixed values of the other predictors — but so far every group shares one slope, and every trend is a straight line. Both restrictions are modelling choices, not facts. Lecture 6 relaxes them: interactions let the season trend differ by team composition, and polynomial terms let the trend bend — each with the discipline for reading the result honestly.

Retrieval (10 minutes)

  1. Why are only \(G-1\) dummies used with an intercept?
  2. What does a dummy coefficient compare?
  3. What changes when the reference group changes?
  4. What does a partial F-test evaluate?
  5. Why can a joint F-test reject when one individual t-test does not?
Check your answers
  1. All \(G\) dummies sum to 1, the intercept column: exact collinearity, so the coefficients would not be identified.
  2. A category with the named reference category, holding the other included predictors fixed.
  3. Coefficient labels and values change to describe the same fits from a new vantage point; fitted values and overall fit do not change.
  4. Whether a specified group of coefficients is jointly zero, comparing nested models on the same rows.

\(F=(643.43/2)/(84{,}003/1{,}430)=321.7/58.7\approx 5.48\), matching the table, with p \(\approx\) 0.004. At 5%, the two category terms are jointly useful even in the model that adjusts only for season.

The 120-word interpretation (10 minutes)

Write a 120-word interpretation of the full gender_model that includes the source measurement boundary and the causal limit.

Check a model answer

A complete answer reports: the outcome and sample (initial equity offered, 1,434 broadcast pitches with a recorded source classification); both estimates with units and conditioning (all-women teams fitted 1.28 percentage points above all-men teams, mixed teams 0.12 points above, at the same season and requested amount); uncertainty (p \(\approx\) 0.008 and 0.823; jointly useful, partial F p \(\approx\) 0.023); the measurement boundary (broad source coding of the on-screen team, not shares or identities); and the scope (adjusted associations among selected, recorded pitches — not causal, not essentialist).

Worked exam interpretation (5 minutes)

Question. In the mock-exam Q4 style: a model of equity on season, ask_1k, and industry (four categories, reference Food and Beverage) reports industryTechnology 1.9 (p = 0.04). A student writes: “Technology pitches offer 1.9% more equity, so entering tech makes founders more generous.” Correct the sentence.

Check a model answer

Three repairs. Units: the estimate is 1.9 equity percentage points, not a relative 1.9%. Comparison and conditioning: technology pitches are fitted 1.9 points above Food and Beverage pitches at the same season and requested amount, among the recorded pitches. Scope: nothing here is causal — “entering tech makes founders more generous” reads a reference-group contrast as an effect of industry choice.

From the reading ladder (10 minutes)

Next rung of the reading ladder: open Brooks et al. (2014) — an open-access study of entrepreneurial pitch success. Find where the analysis compares pitches across a category (the presenter’s gender). Identify the categorical predictor and what serves as the reference comparison, and restate the paper’s headline gap in one careful sentence that respects today’s two rules: percentage points are not percent, and an adjusted gap between categories is not a causal claim about any attribute. Then note one way their setting differs from our Shark Tank teaching file.

Check your approach

A good sentence names both categories explicitly, states the outcome and its unit, and attributes the gap to the sample and design (“in these pitch competitions, pitches presented by … were funded … percentage points more often than …”) without sliding into “because investors prefer…”. Differences from our file worth noting: their data include experiments with manipulated recordings, whereas ours are selected, edited television records with a broad source category for the presenting team.

Make it yours (5 minutes)

Utility: two sentences

Think of your EiA3 venture — or any business you know well. Name one categorical feature of your customers or products (segment, channel, region, plan tier), and write two sentences: which category would you set as the reference group and why, and what would a “jointly useful” F-test verdict on the whole set of categories change about how you describe your market?
