Lecture 6: Interactions and curvature

By the end of Lecture 6, you can

  • read a picture of two lines as four numbers: two intercepts and two slopes
  • explain an interaction coefficient as a difference in slopes, with units
  • interpret the other coefficients in a model with an interaction, and use centring to give them a useful meaning
  • report an interaction that is not significant without claiming too much in either direction
  • apply the hierarchy principle and the x * z formula shorthand
  • fit a quadratic term, explain why the model is still a linear regression, and compute a fitted change from both coefficients together
  • test added terms with a partial F-test, and explain why a curve must not be used outside the range of the data.

Must know

Be able to do yourself:

  • write the two lines of an interaction model
  • interpret an interaction as a difference in slopes
  • say what centring changes and what it cannot change
  • calculate a fitted change in a quadratic model with \(\hat\beta_1+\hat\beta_2(2x+1)\)
  • explain why a curve must not be used beyond the range of the data.

Enough to recognise:

  • the I() syntax, which is on the code sheet
  • the name hierarchy principle.

Driving question

An average slope can hide important differences. A relationship may differ between groups, or its slope may change as a predictor grows. Interactions and curved terms let a model show these patterns.

The equity offered (equity_offered_pct) in the Shark Tank file falls over the seasons. Is this trend the same for every kind of presenting team, and is it a straight line at all?

Lecture material

This section adds two terms to the regression model. An interaction lets a slope differ between two groups. A squared term lets a slope change along the predictor itself.

Stanford explanations (review): 3.5 Extensions of the Linear Model and 7.1 Polynomials and Step Functions.

Start with a founder’s question

Does advertising work equally well in every week? Does a trend go on in a straight line forever? Often the answer is “it depends”. The two tools of this lecture let you check this in the data. An interaction lets a slope differ between groups. A curve lets a slope change as the predictor grows.

The picture before the formula

In every model so far, all groups had the same slope. Lecture 5 let the height of the line differ between groups. Now the slope may differ as well. Start with the picture in Figure 23.1:

This answers the prediction question from before class: four numbers, an intercept and a slope for each line. The interaction model writes the same four numbers in a different way:

\[ E[Y\mid X,D] =\beta_0+\beta_1X+\beta_2D+\beta_3XD. \]

Each coefficient is one feature of the picture:

  • \(\beta_0\): where the \(D=0\) line meets the vertical axis
  • \(\beta_1\): the slope of the \(D=0\) line
  • \(\beta_2\): how much higher the \(D=1\) line starts
  • \(\beta_3\): how much steeper the \(D=1\) line is.

The same in a table with one row per group, as you derived it before class:

Group Intercept Slope
\(D=0\) \(\beta_0\) \(\beta_1\)
\(D=1\) \(\beta_0+\beta_2\) \(\beta_1+\beta_3\)

When \(\beta_3=0\) the two lines are parallel, and the model is the dummy model of Lecture 5 with one common slope.

The interaction coefficient is a difference in slopes

Definition

Interaction. A term that is the product of two predictors, for example \(X\times D\). It lets the slope of one predictor depend on the value of the other. Its coefficient is a difference in slopes.

In the additive model the slope of \(X\) is the same at every value of the other predictor. With the product \(XD\) in the model, a one-unit increase in \(X\) changes the conditional mean by

\[ \beta_1+\beta_3D. \]

So \(\beta_3\) is the difference between the slope of the group with \(D=1\) and the slope of the reference group. It is not an effect on its own. Its unit combines the two variables. If \(X\) is measured in seasons and \(D\) is a dummy, \(\beta_3\) is in outcome units per season: here, equity percentage points per season, one group minus the reference group. The same algebra works when the second variable is a number \(Z\) and not a dummy. The slope of \(X\) is then \(\beta_1+\beta_3Z\), a slope that changes with \(Z\).

Centring. Centring a predictor means subtracting its mean. With \(XD\) in the model, \(\beta_2\) compares the two groups at \(X=0\). For the Shark Tank data this would be Season 0, which does not exist. Replacing season with

\[ \text{season\_c} = \text{season} - \overline{\text{season}} \]

moves that comparison to the average season (about 8.8). Centring changes some results and cannot change others:

  • It changes the meaning and the value of the intercept and of the group coefficient. Both now refer to the average season.
  • It cannot change the fitted values, the residuals, \(R^2\), or the interaction coefficient. The lines are the same lines. Only the place where we read the vertical gap between them has moved.
Check: which printed numbers change when season is centred?

The intercept and the women coefficient change. The interaction coefficient, the ask_1k coefficient, all fitted values, and \(R^2\) do not. You will check this in R in the after-class practice.

Always graph a model that contains an interaction. In Figure 23.2, the main result is that the two lines are almost parallel. Once \(XD\) is in the model, neither \(\hat\beta_1\) nor \(\hat\beta_2\) alone describes all teams.

The hierarchy principle and the * shorthand

The hierarchy principle says: if a model contains \(XD\), keep both \(X\) and \(D\) in the model, even when one of them is not significant on its own. The terms \(X\) and \(D\) define the two lines, and the interaction describes the difference between these lines. If you leave out \(D\), the model forces both lines to start at the same height. This distorts the interaction coefficient. The * shorthand in an R formula follows the principle for you:

lm(y ~ x * z, data = df)  # x * z expands to x + z + x:z

An interaction lets the slope differ by group. A squared term lets the slope change as \(x\) itself changes. Both add one new column to the data, a product or a square. In both, you read two coefficients together.

Curvature: polynomials are still linear regression

Shark Tank data Is the season trend a straight line at all? To let it bend, add the square of the predictor.

Definition

Quadratic term. The square of a predictor, \(x^2\), added to a model that also contains \(x\). It lets the fitted line bend, so the slope changes with \(x\). A model with powers of a predictor is called a polynomial.

The quadratic conditional mean

\[ E[Y\mid X=x]=\beta_0+\beta_1x+\beta_2x^2 \]

is curved in \(x\), but it is linear in the unknown coefficients \(\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2\). Linearity in the coefficients is all that lm() requires. The square is one more column of data.

What changes is how you read the output. The slope is no longer one number. Moving from \(x\) to \(x+1\) changes the conditional mean by

\[ (\beta_1(x+1)+\beta_2(x+1)^2) - (\beta_1x+\beta_2x^2) =\beta_1+\beta_2(2x+1). \]

This change depends on the starting value \(x\). The rule is: never read \(\beta_1\) alone while \(x^2\) is in the model. Calculate fitted changes or graph the curve.

linear_season <- lm(equity_offered_pct ~ season + ask_1k, data = sharks)
poly_season <- lm(equity_offered_pct ~ season + I(season^2) + ask_1k,
                  data = sharks)
summary(poly_season)$coefficients

I() tells R to calculate the square. Without I(), R would read ^ as part of the formula syntax and not as a square. The fitted coefficients are \(\hat\beta_1 \approx -1.713\) for season and \(\hat\beta_2 \approx 0.051\) for I(season^2). Together they describe a steep decline in the early seasons that becomes flatter season by season.

Example 23.1 (Worked example: the fitted change from Season 5 to Season 6) Shark Tank data Holding requested amount fixed, the fitted change from season \(x\) to season \(x+1\) is \(\hat\beta_1+\hat\beta_2(2x+1)\). From Season 5 to Season 6, \(x = 5\):

\[ \hat\beta_1+\hat\beta_2(2\times5+1) =-1.713+0.051\times11 =-1.713+0.561 \approx-1.15 \text{ percentage points.} \]

Reading \(\hat\beta_1=-1.713\) alone would make the drop about 0.56 points too large, which is about half of the fitted drop of 1.15 points. The number \(-1.713\) is not a constant slope. It describes the slope at \(x=0\), a season that does not exist. Check the calculation in R with the stored coefficients:

b <- coef(poly_season)
unname(b["season"] + b["I(season^2)"] * (2 * 5 + 1))
[1] -1.151857

The points in Figure 23.3 are the plain means of each season. The curve keeps the requested amount fixed at its sample mean. To extrapolate means to use a fitted model outside the range of the data. Do not extrapolate the quadratic beyond Seasons 1–16. The fitted curve reaches its lowest point just after Season 16 and would rise steeply afterwards. This rise comes from the shape of a quadratic, not from any data, so it is not a forecast.

What flexible terms can and cannot repair

  • Interactions and curves make the model more flexible. They cannot fix problems in how the data were produced.
  • Every added term costs precision. Add a term because your question needs it, not because it turns out to be significant.
  • You can trust a curve only inside the range of the data.

What you can do with it: ask whether a relationship could differ between groups or follow a curve, add that one term, graph the fitted lines, and report combined results.

Answering the driving question

The same trend for every kind of team? The estimated difference in slopes is \(-0.061\) percentage points per season (p \(\approx\) 0.52). The data show no difference between all-men teams and teams with women. They also do not prove that the trends are equal, because the estimate is imprecise.

A straight line? Not quite. The quadratic term is significant (partial \(F \approx 20.1\), p \(<\) 0.001). The fitted decline becomes flatter: from about \(-1.56\) points per season between Seasons 1 and 2 to about \(-0.64\) between Seasons 10 and 11. Use the curve only inside Seasons 1–16.

The founder’s questions at the start of the lecture get the same two kinds of answer. Whether advertising works equally well in every week is a question about an interaction. In the invented example of the Try it task, advertising does not work equally well: the slope is 0.10 orders per euro in ordinary weeks and 0.22 in exam weeks. Whether a trend goes on in a straight line is a question about a curve. The season trend of the equity offered does not go on in a straight line: the fitted drop per season shrinks from about 1.56 to about 0.64 points, and beyond Season 16 the data say nothing.

A founder’s decision (invented example)

You suspect that advertising works better in exam weeks. The model with an interaction gives an advertising slope of 0.10 orders per euro in ordinary weeks and 0.10 + 0.12 = 0.22 in exam weeks. After a price increase, each order now brings 6 euros of margin. So 100 euros of advertising goes with 10 more orders and 60 euros of margin in an ordinary week, which is less than the advertising costs. In an exam week it goes with 22 more orders and 132 euros of margin. The decision: advertise in the exam weeks only. One check first: the interaction of 0.12 has its own interval. If that interval includes zero, the data do not yet show that the two kinds of weeks differ. Then keep spreading the advertising budget over both kinds of weeks for one more study period before you decide.

Worked exam interpretation (5 minutes)

Exam-style question. An invented example. A model of the weekly revenue of market stalls (in €100s) on opening hours per week, a dummy for indoor stalls, and their interaction reports hours 1.20, indoor 2.00, hours:indoor -0.50. A student writes: “One more opening hour raises weekly revenue by €120 for all stalls.” Correct the sentence.

Check a model answer

Two corrections. First, with the interaction in the model, 1.20 is the fitted slope of opening hours only for stalls that are not indoor (indoor = 0). For indoor stalls the slope is \(1.20-0.50=0.70\), that is, €70 of weekly revenue per opening hour. There is no single slope for all stalls. Second, “raises” claims causation. These are fitted associations among the sampled stalls, holding the other included terms fixed. The data do not come from an experiment, so they do not show an effect of opening hours.
