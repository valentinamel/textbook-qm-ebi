Quantitative Methods to Understand E&BI

Welcome

Welcome to the learning line Quantitative Methods to Understand E&BI. Across three courses, we will learn how to turn questions about entrepreneurship, organisations, markets, and innovation into defensible quantitative evidence. This learning line helps you to understand the research papers used in other courses, to write a quantitative thesis, and to analyse data carefully at work.

Our aim is not simply to teach formulas or R commands. We want you to become a careful reader and producer of evidence: someone who can choose a method, explain its logic, interpret its output correctly, and recognise what the available data cannot establish.

Why this, if you want to start a business

Founders decide all the time: which product, which price, which customers, how much to ask from an investor. Each of these decisions gets better with evidence. Today the evidence is easy to get. You have your own sales data, a quick survey, and R or an AI assistant that turns data into numbers within seconds.

The numbers are the easy part. The hard part is to know what a number tells you, when to trust it, and what to do next. An AI assistant will hand you an average, a p-value, or a regression table, together with a confident sentence. Sometimes the sentence is wrong. This learning line gives you what you need to read such output yourself, to check it, and to turn it into a decision.

This is why every lecture starts from a practical question, tries to answer it with data, and only then brings the theory. The theory is what lets you understand what R or an AI assistant is telling you. The same skills help you during your studies: in your venture projects, when you read research in other courses, and in your thesis.

The three courses form one connected line. QM Intermediate and QM Advanced build on the concepts introduced in QM Basic.

How to use this book

The permanent menu on the left is organised into QM Basic, QM Intermediate, QM Advanced, and Supplementary resources. On a small (mobile) screen, open the menu with the button at the top of the page.

Within each course, follow the chapters in order:

  • Lecture chapters introduce you to a question and data and teach you how to conduct analysis in R, together with the theory you need to interpret and understand the output.
  • Tutorial chapters help you recall, combine, and apply what you have learned. They are built for guided practice and exam preparation.
  • Course exam resources include a cumulative formula sheet for QM Basic, a code-and-formula sheet for QM Intermediate, and a 100-point mock exam at the end of each of those courses.
  • Supplementary resources collect reusable help with R, mathematics, external videos, and the How to succeed in this course page with printable weekly checklists.

How a lecture chapter is built

Every lecture chapter has the same three parts.

  • Before class. A short preparation, usually a video, a warm-up task, and one prediction. It carries a stated time budget.
  • Lecture material. The full material of the lecture: the question, the data, the R code, the theory, and worked examples. The lecture itself covers the core of it and leaves time for questions and for trying things out. The text has every step, so you can read it again at your own pace.
  • After class. Practice with model answers. The first step is always to read the lecture material once in full. Then come recall questions and tasks.

A few boxes return in every chapter:

  • Must know lists what you must be able to do yourself and what it is enough to recognise.
  • Definition boxes hold the core terms. They are part of the exam material.
  • Optional boxes are grey and closed. They hold formal versions, derivations, and side topics for those who want them. They are not examined.
  • Try it boxes ask you to attempt a step before you open the answer.
  • A founder’s decision shows how the tool of the lecture settles one decision in an invented venture.

Always do the preparation marked “Before class” before you come to the lecture. The How to succeed page shows what a week looks like, with stated time budgets, a minimum path, and a full path.

Lecture preparation and external videos

“Before class” sections include a clearly marked “Video Preparation” box. Watch the listed required clips before the lecture and make brief notes on the indicated concepts. Basic primarily uses MIT OpenCourseWare. Intermediate uses Stanford’s Statistical Learning series. The External Video Guide separates required preparation, review when needed, and optional extension clips. Only the first category is compulsory. Theory sections in the lecture material also link directly to external explanations that you can replay after a lecture or an absence. External sources may use different notation or cover more material than this course. Follow the notation and scope stated in this textbook.

R and main dataset

You will work with R throughout all three courses. You already met R in Programming for E&BI. If a command or workflow has become rusty, use the R Refresher and the earlier course as references.

Our shared dataset records pitches shown on the US television programme Shark Tank. In a televised pitch, entrepreneurs present a venture or product to a panel of investors known as sharks. They may ask for money in exchange for an ownership share. The investors question them, negotiate, and may announce an agreement during the episode.

Most chapters use a 19-variable teaching view derived from the Shark Tank US Dataset. One row represents one recorded televised pitch. The file contains 1,441 pitches from completed Seasons 1–16. Incomplete Season 17 is excluded. Its variables include pitch and episode identifiers, venture industry and written description, the initial amount and equity requested, whether an agreement was announced on air, recorded deal terms, and the source’s broad presenter-gender-composition category.

This is an observational record of selected, edited television pitches, not a random sample of all entrepreneurs or ventures. An on-air agreement is not evidence that the investment was completed or that the venture later succeeded. The data can describe recorded pitches and associations among their variables, but they do not by themselves identify causal effects.

Tutorials and assessment

All three courses end with an exam with open-ended questions that account for 100% of the grade. In QM Basic, the written exam can ask you to verify, interpret, and describe supplied R output and solve probability and statistics tasks. In QM Intermediate, you will work directly with data in R during an exam administered through TestVision. QM Advanced ends with a written exam. Tutorials in all courses are designed as exam preparation sessions.

Go directly to the QM Basic Formula Sheet, QM Basic Mock Exam, QM Intermediate Code Sheet, or QM Intermediate Mock Exam when preparing for assessment.

Working with AI

We provide a chatbot for this course: Tilly for QM Courseline. It is designed to support and guide you throughout the course. It can help with organisational questions (schedule, grade composition, etc) as well as act as an individual tutor when solving tasks together. You can also ask it to generate additional practice questions to prepare for the exam.

When you use AI, follow Attempt → Ask → Verify → Explain:

  1. Attempt to solve the question yourself and identify where you are stuck.
  2. Ask AI for support in the form of a hint, counterexample, or diagnostic question. Be precise and detailed when creating a prompt for AI. A precise prompt gets a better answer.
  3. Verify the response against the data, course notation, and R output. Verification becomes the key skill when working with AI.
  4. Explain the result in your own words to make the knowledge your own. Ask AI for feedback on this explanation and/or bring it as a question to lectures/tutorials and your classmates.
