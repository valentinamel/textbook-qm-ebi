Tutorial 3: Sampling and confidence intervals

By the end of Tutorial 3, you can

  • calculate standard errors and probabilities for sample means and proportions,
  • construct confidence intervals and qualify conclusions when the sampling design is limited.

Tutorials are exam preparation sessions. The tasks below are exam-style questions. Attempt each task before you open its answer. You may use AI, following Attempt, Ask, Verify, Explain.

Before class

Bring your after-class work from Lectures 7 to 9. If you find the algebra difficult, review Mathematics Refresher: sums, fractions, and roots.

Use the formula sheet with all formulas up to and including Lecture 9.

Complete from memory:

  1. A parameter describes …
  2. An estimator is …
  3. The standard deviation of the sample mean \(\bar X\) is … The standard error of the sample mean is …
  4. The CLT describes …
  5. A written answer for a confidence interval has five parts, in this order: …
  6. The confidence interval for a mean with \(s\) has the formula … It may be used when …
  7. The confidence interval for a proportion has the formula … It may be used when …
Check your preparation
  1. A parameter describes a population.
  2. An estimator is a rule that estimates a parameter from a sample. Its value is random, because the sample is random.
  3. The standard deviation of the sample mean is \(\operatorname{SD}(\bar X)=\sigma/\sqrt n\). The standard error is \(\operatorname{SE}(\bar X)=S/\sqrt n\), with the value \(s/\sqrt n\). It is the estimated standard deviation of \(\bar X\), used when \(\sigma\) is unknown.
  4. The distribution of the sample mean across random samples. It is approximately normal for a large random sample, also when the population is not normal.
  5. Formula, with the reason why it may be used. Given values. Calculations: the standard error, then the margin of error. Interval, with the unit. Conclusion in the words of the question.
  6. \(\bar X\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\,S/\sqrt n\). The population is normal, or \(n\) is large.
  7. \(\widehat P\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat P(1-\widehat P)/n}\). The condition is \(n\hat p\ge10\) and \(n(1-\hat p)\ge10\).

In the tutorial

A complete written answer names the quantity and shows the calculation. It reports the value with its unit and interprets this value in context. It also states one important limit of the model or the data.

More tasks to practise at home are in the final section of this page, Extra practice for home.

In practice \(\sigma\) is unknown, so an interval for a mean uses \(s\) and a t value. In this book, the values you need are given in the task or on the formula sheet.

Task 1: Population, frame, and claim

A student randomly selects 100 rows from the Shark Tank file and estimates the mean equity offered (equity_offered_pct).

  1. State the population about which this sample allows direct conclusions.
  2. Explain why this sample does not represent all Shark Tank applicants.
  3. Explain why this sample does not represent all US entrepreneurs.
  4. State one change needed to target all US entrepreneurs.
Check your answer
  1. The population about which the sample allows direct conclusions is the 1,441 recorded pitches in completed Seasons 1 to 16.
  2. Applicants who did not appear on television are not in the file, so the frame does not represent all applicants.
  3. The show selects a special group of entrepreneurs. Its pitches are not a random sample of US entrepreneurs, so the frame does not cover that target population.
  4. To target all US entrepreneurs, you would need a suitable national sampling frame and a random selection from that frame.

Task 2: Standard errors and a larger sample

Shark Tank data A student draws a new random sample of 100 pitches from the Shark Tank file, with the seed 2025. In this sample the mean equity offered is \(\bar x=12.32\)%, and the sample SD is \(s=7.99\) percentage points. Of the 100 pitches, 65 reached an on-air deal, so the sample deal proportion is \(\hat p=0.65\).

  1. Calculate the standard error of the sample mean of the equity offered, \(\operatorname{SE}(\bar X)\).
  2. Calculate the standard error of the sample proportion of deals, \(\operatorname{SE}(\widehat P)\) (Lecture 8).
  3. Check the success-failure condition for \(\widehat P\).
  4. Predict how each standard error changes if \(n\) increases from 100 to 400 and the spread in the sample stays similar.
  5. The sample SD in part 1 divides by \(n-1\). State the value of \(n-1\) here, and explain in one sentence why the divisor is \(n-1\) and not \(n\).
  6. Explain why the results of parts 1 and 2 are standard errors and not the standard deviations of \(\bar X\) and \(\widehat P\).
Check your answer

  1. The standard error of the mean is \(\operatorname{SE}(\bar X)=S/\sqrt n\). Its value in this sample is

    \[ \frac{s}{\sqrt n}=\frac{7.9912}{\sqrt{100}}=0.7991. \]

    The standard error of the mean is about 0.80 percentage points of equity, about 6.5% of the sample mean of 12.32%.

  2. The standard error of the proportion is \(\operatorname{SE}(\widehat P)=\sqrt{\widehat P(1-\widehat P)/n}\). Its value in this sample is

    \[ \sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}} =\sqrt{\frac{0.65(0.35)}{100}}=0.0477. \]

    The standard error of the proportion is about 4.8 percentage points of the deal rate, about 7.3% of the sample proportion of 0.65.

  3. The sample contains 65 successes (deals) and 35 failures (no deal). Both counts are at least 10, so the success-failure condition holds.

  4. If \(n\) becomes four times as large, both standard errors are divided by \(\sqrt 4=2\).

  5. The divisor is \(n-1=99\). The deviations are measured from the sample mean, so they must sum to zero. As a result, only 99 of them can vary freely. Dividing by 99 removes the downward bias that this causes (Lecture 7).

  6. The standard deviations of the two estimators are \(\sigma/\sqrt n\) and \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\). They contain the population values \(\sigma\) and \(p\), which a sample does not give. The standard errors use \(s\) and \(\hat p\) from the sample in their place. A standard error is an estimated standard deviation of an estimator.

Verification in R:

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
set.seed(2025)
sample_rows <- sample(seq_len(nrow(sharks)), size = 100, replace = FALSE)

s_equity <- sd(pitch_sample$equity_offered_pct)

  deals = sum(pitch_sample$deal_on_show),
  no_deals = 100 - sum(pitch_sample$deal_on_show),
  se_mean_n400 = s_equity / sqrt(400),
  se_proportion_n400 = sqrt(p_hat * (1 - p_hat) / 400))

Task 3: The exact normal case

A founder makes scented candles. The burn time of one candle, \(X\), is modelled as \(X\sim N(40,6^2)\), in hours. A tester burns a random sample of \(n=9\) candles and records the sample mean \(\bar X\). Supplied values for the whole task:

P(Z <= 0.6) = 0.7257
P(Z <= 1.8) = 0.9641
  1. State the distribution of \(\bar X\) for \(n=9\), with its mean and its standard deviation. Explain why you may use this distribution although the sample is small.
  2. Calculate the probability that one candle burns less than 36.4 hours.
  3. Calculate the probability that the mean burn time of the 9 candles is below 36.4 hours.
  4. Explain in one sentence why the answers to parts 2 and 3 differ.
Check your answer
  1. The population is normal. Then the sample mean is exactly normal for every sample size, also for a small one (Lecture 8): \(\bar X\sim N(40,\,6^2/9)\). Its mean is 40 hours and its standard deviation is \(\operatorname{SD}(\bar X)=6/\sqrt9=2\) hours.
  2. One candle: \(z=(36.4-40)/6=-0.6\). With the symmetry rule and the complement rule, \(P(X<36.4)=P(Z\le-0.6)=1-0.7257=0.2743\).
  3. The mean of 9 candles: \(z=(36.4-40)/2=-1.8\). So \(P(\bar X<36.4)=P(Z\le-1.8)=1-0.9641=0.0359\).
  4. The mean of 9 candles varies three times less than one candle, so 36.4 hours is an ordinary value for one candle (about 27%) and an unusual value for the mean of 9 candles (about 4%).

Verification in R:

c(sd_mean_9 = 6 / sqrt(9),
  p_one_below_36.4 = pnorm(36.4, mean = 40, sd = 6),
  p_mean_below_36.4 = pnorm(36.4, mean = 40, sd = 6 / sqrt(9)))
        sd_mean_9  p_one_below_36.4 p_mean_below_36.4 
       2.00000000        0.27425312        0.03593032

Task 4: A proportion through the CLT

A meal-kit start-up knows from its customer database that 45% of its subscribers paused their subscription at least once last year, so \(p=0.45\). A survey of a random sample of \(n=200\) subscribers is planned, with \(\widehat P\) the sample proportion of subscribers who paused. Supplied value: \(P(Z\le -1.99)=0.023\).

  1. Calculate the standard deviation of the sample proportion, \(\operatorname{SD}(\widehat P)\). Then check the success-failure condition.
  2. State the approximate sampling distribution of \(\widehat P\).
  3. Approximate the probability that the survey shows a pause rate of at most 38%, that is, \(P(\widehat P\le0.38)\).
  4. Interpret the result in one sentence.
Check your answer

  1. Here \(p\) is given, so the result is a standard deviation and not a standard error: \(\operatorname{SD}(\widehat P)=\sqrt{0.45(0.55)/200}\approx0.0352\), about 3.5 percentage points. The expected counts are \(np=90\) and \(n(1-p)=110\), both at least 10.

  2. So \(\widehat P\mathrel{\dot\sim}N(0.45,\,0.0352^2)\).

  3. Standardise:

    \[ z=\frac{0.38-0.45}{0.0352}\approx-1.99, \qquad P(\widehat P\le0.38)\approx P(Z\le-1.99)\approx0.023. \]

  4. If 45% of all subscribers paused, only about 2% of random samples of 200 subscribers would show a pause rate at or below 38%. Such a sample would be surprising. Lecture 10 builds the hypothesis test on this idea.

Verification in R:

sd_phat <- sqrt(0.45 * 0.55 / 200)

  z = (0.38 - 0.45) / sd_phat,
  p_at_most_038 = pnorm(0.38, mean = 0.45, sd = sd_phat))

Task 5: A confidence interval for a mean

A founder runs a bouldering gym. Her business plan assumes that a visit lasts 120 minutes on average. She draws a random sample of 16 visits from the 5,000 visits of last year in the check-in system. The sample has a mean of 136 minutes and a standard deviation of \(s=28\) minutes. The visit times are roughly normally distributed. Supplied values: \(t_{0.975,\,15}=2.131\) and \(t_{0.995,\,15}=2.947\).

  1. Construct a 95% confidence interval for the mean visit time of last year. Write the five parts of a complete answer for an interval for a mean (Lecture 9). In part 5, say what the interval tells you about the 120 minutes in the business plan.
  2. Construct the 99% confidence interval. Only parts 2 to 5 change.
  3. Compare the two conclusions about the 120 minutes. Then explain how the width of the interval changes with the confidence level.
Check your answer

  1. The 95% interval in five parts:

    1. Formula. \(\bar X\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\,S/\sqrt n\). It may be used because the visit times are roughly normal. The sample is small, so a large \(n\) is not the reason here.
    2. Given values. \(\bar x=136\) minutes, \(s=28\) minutes, \(n=16\), \(df=15\). The confidence level is 95%, so \(\alpha=0.05\) and the critical value is \(t_{0.975,\,15}=2.131\).
    3. Calculations. The standard error is \(s/\sqrt n=28/\sqrt{16}=7\) minutes. The margin of error is \(2.131\times7=14.92\) minutes.
    4. Interval. \((136-14.92,\ 136+14.92)=(121.08,\ 150.92)\) minutes.
    5. Conclusion. The value 120 minutes lies outside the interval. At the 5% level we can conclude that the mean visit time of last year differs from 120 minutes. The whole interval lies above 120, so visits last longer than the plan assumes. The lower end is 121.08 minutes, only about 1% above 120. So the difference may be small.

  2. The 99% interval, parts 2 to 5:

    1. Given values. As before, but the confidence level is 99%, so \(\alpha=0.01\) and the critical value is \(t_{0.995,\,15}=2.947\).
    2. Calculations. The standard error stays 7 minutes. The margin of error is \(2.947\times7=20.63\) minutes.
    3. Interval. \((136-20.63,\ 136+20.63)=(115.37,\ 156.63)\) minutes.
    4. Conclusion. The value 120 minutes lies inside the interval. At the 1% level we cannot conclude that the mean visit time differs from 120 minutes.

  3. A higher confidence level needs a larger critical value, so the margin of error grows from 14.92 minutes at 95% to 20.63 minutes at 99%. The 99% interval is about 1.4 times as wide as the 95% interval. With the same sample, we can conclude that the mean differs from 120 minutes at the 5% level, but not at the stricter 1% level. For this reason every conclusion names its level. One limit: the interval describes the visits of last year, not the visits of next year.

Task 6: A confidence interval for a proportion

A founder sells sneakers online. Her parcel service reports that customers return 15% of all sneaker orders. Last year her shop had 6,000 orders. She draws a random sample of 400 of these orders. Customers returned 40 of these 400 orders. Let \(p\) be the proportion of returned orders among all 6,000 orders.

  1. Construct a 95% confidence interval for \(p\). Write the five parts of a complete answer for an interval for a proportion (Lecture 9), with the check of the condition. Check the 10% condition first. In part 5, say what the interval tells you about the 15% reported by the parcel service.
  2. The founder says: “My return rate is 10%, so I am 5 percentage points below the 15%.” Say in at most four sentences what is wrong with this sentence and what she can correctly say.
  3. State the formula of the standard deviation of \(\widehat P\). Then explain why you cannot calculate it here.
Check your answer

  1. The sample contains 400 of the 6,000 orders, that is 6.7%. This is at most 10%, so we may treat the sample as iid. The interval in five parts:

    1. Formula. \(\widehat P\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat P(1-\widehat P)/n}\). The condition holds: \(n\hat p=40\) and \(n(1-\hat p)=360\) are both at least 10.
    2. Given values. 40 returned orders, \(n=400\), \(\hat p=40/400=0.10\). The confidence level is 95%, so \(z_{0.975}=1.960\).
    3. Calculations. The standard error is \(\sqrt{0.10\times0.90/400}=\sqrt{0.000225}=0.015\). The margin of error is \(1.960\times0.015=0.0294\).
    4. Interval. \((0.10-0.0294,\ 0.10+0.0294)=(0.071,\ 0.129)\), that is, from 7.1% to 12.9% of the orders.
    5. Conclusion. The value 0.15 lies outside the interval. At the 5% level we can conclude that her proportion of returned orders differs from 15%. The whole interval lies below 0.15.

  2. The sentence treats the estimate as the exact rate. The 10% is the estimate from 400 orders. The plausible values run from 7.1% to 12.9%. So her return rate is between 2.1 and 7.9 percentage points below the 15%.

  3. The standard deviation of the sample proportion is \(\operatorname{SD}(\widehat P)=\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\). It contains the unknown \(p\), which is the number that the interval tries to estimate. The standard error uses \(\hat p\) in its place.

Verification in R:

p_hat_returns <- 40 / 400
se_returns <- sqrt(p_hat_returns * (1 - p_hat_returns) / 400)

  successes = 400 * p_hat_returns, failures = 400 * (1 - p_hat_returns),
  se = se_returns, margin = 1.960 * se_returns,
  lower = p_hat_returns - 1.960 * se_returns,
  upper = p_hat_returns + 1.960 * se_returns,
  sample_share_of_orders = 400 / 6000,
  below_15_at_most = 0.15 - (p_hat_returns - 1.960 * se_returns),
  below_15_at_least = 0.15 - (p_hat_returns + 1.960 * se_returns))

Task 7: A sample size for a target margin of error

The founder of the bouldering gym in Task 5 plans a new and larger sample. She wants to estimate the mean visit time with a margin of error of at most 5 minutes. She uses the sample SD of Task 5 as a planning value: \(\sigma=28\) minutes.

  1. Calculate the sample size that she needs at the 95% level (\(z_{0.975}=1.960\)).
  2. Calculate the sample size for a margin of 2.5 minutes at the 95% level. Compare it with part 1.
  3. Calculate the sample size for a margin of 5 minutes at the 99% level (\(z_{0.995}=2.576\)).
  4. Her business partner asks for a 95% confidence interval whose total width is at most 4 minutes, from the lower end to the upper end. Calculate the sample size for this interval.
Check your answer

The formula of Lecture 9, with \(m\) the margin of error, is

\[ n\ge\left(\frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma}{m}\right)^2 . \]

  1. \(n\ge(1.960\times28/5)^2=10.976^2\approx120.5\), so she needs 121 visits. Always round up: with 120 visits the margin would be slightly above 5 minutes.
  2. \(n\ge(1.960\times28/2.5)^2=21.952^2\approx481.9\), so 482 visits. Half the margin needs about four times the sample.
  3. \(n\ge(2.576\times28/5)^2=14.4256^2\approx208.1\), so 209 visits. More confidence with the same margin needs more observations: 209 in place of 121.
  4. The formula needs the margin of error, which is half the width. So \(m=4/2=2\) minutes, and \(n\ge(1.960\times28/2)^2=27.44^2\approx752.95\). She needs 753 visits. If you put the full width of 4 into the formula, you get 189 visits, about a quarter of the correct number. The interval from 189 visits would be about twice as wide as asked.

The planning value is a guess. If the true \(\sigma\) is larger than 28 minutes, the margin of the new sample will be larger than planned.

After class

Individual practice

Do this task alone: no notes, no AI, only the formula sheet. A random sample of 49 customers of a subscription service has a mean monthly spending of €78 and a sample SD of €21. Of the 49 customers, 31 renewed their subscription.

  1. Calculate the standard error of the mean spending.
  2. Calculate the standard error of the renewal proportion. Then check the success-failure condition.
  3. State what would happen to both standard errors if the sample size were \(n=196\).
  4. Construct a 95% confidence interval for the mean monthly spending of all customers, in the five parts of a complete answer. The degrees of freedom are 48. Use the closest value on the formula sheet: \(t_{0.975,\,60}=2.000\). The business plan of the founder assumes a mean spending of €85. Say in part 5 what the interval tells you about this value.
  5. Write one sentence that explains the difference between sampling uncertainty and selection bias.
  6. Treat \(\mu=78\) and \(\sigma=21\) as the population values. Use the CLT to approximate the probability that a new sample of 49 customers shows a mean above €81. Supplied value: \(P(Z\le 1)=0.841\).
  7. Use \(\sigma=21\) as a planning value. How many customers must a new survey include to estimate the mean spending with a margin of error of at most €3, at the 95% level (\(z_{0.975}=1.96\))?
Check a model answer

  1. The standard error of the mean is \(21/\sqrt{49}=3\) euros, about 4% of the sample mean of 78 euros.

  2. The renewal estimate is \(\hat p=31/49\approx0.633\), with standard error \(\sqrt{0.633(0.367)/49}\approx0.0689\), about 6.9 percentage points. The sample has 31 successes and 18 failures, so the success-failure condition holds.

  3. Increasing the sample size from 49 to 196 would halve both standard errors if the spread stayed similar.

  4. The interval for the mean spending in five parts:

    1. Formula. \(\bar X\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\,S/\sqrt n\). It may be used because \(n=49\) is large.
    2. Given values. \(\bar x=78\) euros, \(s=21\) euros, \(n=49\), \(df=48\). The confidence level is 95%, and the closest value on the formula sheet is \(t_{0.975,\,60}=2.000\).
    3. Calculations. The standard error is \(21/\sqrt{49}=3\) euros. The margin of error is \(2.000\times3=6\) euros.
    4. Interval. \((78-6,\ 78+6)=(72,\ 84)\) euros.
    5. Conclusion. The value 85 euros lies outside the interval. At the 5% level we can conclude that the mean monthly spending differs from 85 euros. The whole interval lies below 85, so customers spend less than the plan assumes: at least 1 euro less, about 1% of 85 euros.

  5. The standard errors and the interval describe random variation from sample to sample, not bias created by an unrepresentative sampling frame.

  6. For the new sample, the standard deviation of the sample mean is \(\operatorname{SD}(\bar X)=21/\sqrt{49}=3\) euros and \(\bar X\mathrel{\dot\sim}N(78,3^2)\), so \(z=(81-78)/3=1\) and \(P(\bar X>81)\approx1-P(Z\le1)\approx0.16\).

  7. For a margin of €3, \(n\ge(1.96\times21/3)^2=13.72^2\approx188.2\), so the survey needs 189 customers.

Check your understanding

Complete from memory:

  1. A sampling distribution describes …
  2. The standard deviation of a sample mean equals … The standard error of a sample mean equals …
  3. The success-failure condition requires …
  4. The CLT says … becomes approximately normal.
  5. A larger sample does not fix …
  6. A claimed value that lies outside a 95% confidence interval means … A claimed value that lies inside means …
Check your answers
  1. How a statistic would vary across repeated random samples.
  2. \(\sigma/\sqrt n\). The standard error is the estimated version, \(s/\sqrt n\).
  3. \(np\ge10\) and \(n(1-p)\ge10\). For a confidence interval the check uses \(\hat p\).
  4. The sample mean. For a large sample its sampling distribution is approximately normal. The single observations do not become normal.
  5. Selection bias, which comes from the way units entered the frame or the sample.
  6. Outside: at the 5% level we can conclude that the parameter differs from the claimed value. Inside: we cannot conclude that it differs, which is not proof that the claimed value is true.

Task 8: One pitch versus one mean

Shark Tank data Suppose the population mean of the equity offered is \(\mu=13.21\)%, the population SD is \(\sigma=8.44\) percentage points. You draw independent random samples of \(n=100\) pitches. Supplied values: \(P(Z\le 2.12)=0.983\) and \(P(Z\le 0.21)=0.583\).

  1. State the approximate sampling distribution of \(\bar X\), with the standard deviation of the sample mean.
  2. Calculate \(P(\bar X>15)\).
  3. A classmate models a single pitch as \(X\sim N(13.21, 8.44^2)\) and calculates \(P(X>15)\). Compute that value too. Then explain why the two answers are so different.
  4. The equity offered is strongly right-skewed (Lecture 1). So which of the two calculations uses a bad model? And why does the CLT still justify the calculation in part 2?
Check your answer

  1. The CLT gives approximately \(\bar X\mathrel{\dot\sim}N(13.21,\,8.44^2/100)\). The standard deviation of the sample mean is \(\operatorname{SD}(\bar X)=8.44/\sqrt{100}=0.844\) percentage points.

  2. With this distribution,

    \[ P(\bar X>15) =1-P\!\left(Z\le\frac{15-13.21}{0.844}\right) =1-P(Z\le2.12) \approx0.017. \]

  3. For a single pitch, \(z=(15-13.21)/8.44\approx0.21\), so \(P(X>15)\approx1-0.583\approx0.42\). The mean of 100 pitches varies ten times less than one pitch (\(\sigma/\sqrt{100}\)), so 15 is an ordinary value for one pitch but an extreme value for a mean of 100.

  4. A normal curve is symmetric and the equity offered is strongly right-skewed. So the normal model for a single pitch in part 3 is a bad model. The CLT concerns the sample mean. The mean of 100 independent draws has a nearly normal sampling distribution, even when the population is skewed. This is why the calculation in part 2 is justified.

Verification in R:

sd_mean <- 8.44 / sqrt(100)
c(sd_of_mean = sd_mean,
  z_mean = (15 - 13.21) / sd_mean,
  p_mean_above_15 = 1 - pnorm(15, mean = 13.21, sd = sd_mean),
  z_single = (15 - 13.21) / 8.44,
  p_single_above_15 = 1 - pnorm(15, mean = 13.21, sd = 8.44))
       sd_of_mean            z_mean   p_mean_above_15          z_single

p_single_above_15

Task 9: The CLT for skewed order values

The candle founder of Task 3 also looks at the orders in her web shop. The value of one order is strongly right-skewed: most orders are small and a few are very large. The mean is \(\mu=45\) euros and the standard deviation is \(\sigma=30\) euros. Supplied value: \(P(Z\le 2.5)=0.9938\).

  1. For a random sample of \(n=100\) orders, calculate the probability that the sample mean is above 52.50 euros. Name the result (the theorem) that allows this calculation.
  2. Explain why you cannot do the same calculation for a random sample of 9 orders.
Check your answer
  1. The population is not normal, but \(n=100\) is large. The CLT gives approximately \(\bar X\mathrel{\dot\sim}N(45,\,30^2/100)\), with \(\operatorname{SD}(\bar X)=30/\sqrt{100}=3\) euros. Then \(z=(52.50-45)/3=2.5\) and \(P(\bar X>52.50)\approx1-0.9938=0.0062\). A sample mean that is 7.50 euros (about 17%) above the population mean occurs in fewer than 1 of 100 samples.
  2. The order values are not normal, so the exact normal case does not hold. A sample of 9 is too small for the CLT. No result of Lecture 8 gives the distribution of \(\bar X\) in this case.

Task 11: A value for a sample mean

A founder sells granola in bags of 250 grams. The filling machine puts \(X\sim N(250,4^2)\) grams into one bag. Every hour she weighs a random sample of \(n=16\) bags and calculates the sample mean \(\bar X\). She wants an alarm value \(a\): she checks the machine when \(\bar X>a\). When the machine works as described, only 5% of the hourly samples may give an alarm. Supplied value: the 0.95 quantile of \(Z\) is \(z_{0.95}=1.645\).

  1. State the distribution of \(\bar X\), with its standard deviation.
  2. Calculate the value \(a\) with \(P(\bar X>a)=0.05\).
  3. Would the same rule make sense with single bags instead of the mean of 16 bags? Calculate the value for a single bag and compare.
Check your answer
  1. The population is normal, so the sample mean is exactly normal: \(\bar X\sim N(250,\,4^2/16)\), with \(\operatorname{SD}(\bar X)=4/\sqrt{16}=1\) gram.
  2. If 5% of the sample means lie above \(a\), then 95% lie at or below it. So \(a\) is the 0.95 quantile of \(\bar X\): \(a=250+1.645\times1=251.65\) grams.
  3. For a single bag the value is \(250+1.645\times4=256.58\) grams. Single bags vary four times more than the mean of 16 bags. So the mean of 16 bags detects a change in the filling of the machine much earlier. Its alarm value is only 1.65 grams above 250, about 0.7% of the bag weight.

Verification in R:

c(sd_mean = 4 / sqrt(16),

  alarm_by_hand = 250 + 1.645 * 1,
  alarm_single = 250 + 1.645 * 4,
  shift_in_percent = 100 * 1.645 / 250)

shift_in_percent
