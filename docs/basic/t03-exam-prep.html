Tutorial 3: Normal models, sampling, and standard errors

By the end of Tutorial 3, you can

  • identify a valid population and sampling frame;
  • distinguish parameters, estimators, and estimates;
  • calculate standard errors for a mean and a proportion;
  • calculate and interpret quartiles and the IQR of a realised sample;
  • use normal-model and CLT probabilities without confusing observations and statistics;
  • qualify conclusions when the sampling design is limited.

Tutorials are exam preparation sessions: every task below follows the format of the final exam. The standing routine for each task is the same — the TA states the task, pairs work on it first for a few minutes with AI use allowed, and the solution is then discussed in plenary, starting from the pair attempts.

This tutorial covers Lectures 6–8. The t distribution belongs to Lecture 9 and is rehearsed in Tutorial 4; everything here runs on the normal model and the CLT.

Before class

Bring your after-class work from Lectures 6–8. If the algebra is slowing you down, review Mathematics Refresher: sums, fractions, and roots.

Use the cumulative formula sheet through Lecture 8.

Complete from memory:

  1. Standardising an observation \(x\) from \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) means calculating …
  2. A parameter describes …
  3. An estimator is …
  4. The standard error of \(\bar X\) is …
  5. The CLT describes …
Check your preparation
  1. \(z=(x-\mu)/\sigma\): the number of population SDs between \(x\) and the mean.
  2. A parameter describes a population.
  3. An estimator is a sample-based random rule used to estimate a parameter.
  4. \(\sigma/\sqrt n\), estimated by \(S/\sqrt n\) when \(\sigma\) is unknown.
  5. The approximate distribution of a properly standardised sum or mean: nearly normal for a large independent sample.

In class

Attempt each task before opening its answer. A complete written answer names the quantity, shows the calculation, reports the value and units, interprets it in context, and states an important model or measurement boundary.

Task 1 — Population, frame, and claim

A student randomly selects 100 rows from the teaching file and estimates the mean initial equity offered.

  1. State the population to which this design directly supports inference.
  2. Explain why it does not represent all Shark Tank applicants.
  3. Explain why it does not represent all US entrepreneurs.
  4. State one change needed to target all US entrepreneurs.
Check your answer

The direct finite population is the 1,441 recorded pitches in completed Seasons 1–16. Applicants who were not televised are absent, so the frame does not represent all applicants. The show also selects a specialised group of entrepreneurs, not a probability sample of US entrepreneurs. Targeting all US entrepreneurs would require an appropriate national sampling frame and random selection from that frame.

Task 2 — Reproduce the realised sample

The sample is drawn with a visible seed, exactly as in Lecture 7, so every reader gets the same 100 rows:

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
set.seed(123)
sample_rows <- sample(seq_len(nrow(sharks)), size = 100, replace = FALSE)
pitch_sample <- sharks[sample_rows, ]

c(
  n = nrow(pitch_sample),
  mean_equity = mean(pitch_sample$equity_offered_pct),
  sd_equity = sd(pitch_sample$equity_offered_pct),
  q1_equity = unname(quantile(pitch_sample$equity_offered_pct, 0.25)),
  median_equity = median(pitch_sample$equity_offered_pct),
  q3_equity = unname(quantile(pitch_sample$equity_offered_pct, 0.75)),
  deal_proportion = mean(pitch_sample$deal_on_show)
)
              n     mean_equity       sd_equity       q1_equity   median_equity 
     100.000000       12.760000        7.707887        8.000000       10.000000 
      q3_equity deal_proportion 
      17.750000        0.640000
  1. Identify the parameter, estimator, and estimate for mean equity.
  2. Identify the parameter, estimator, and estimate for the deal proportion.
  3. Calculate the IQR of equity and interpret it.
  4. Explain why a different seed can produce different estimates and a different IQR.
Check your answer

For equity, the parameter is the population mean \(\mu\), the estimator is \(\bar X\), and the estimate is \(\bar x=12.76\). For deal outcome, the parameter is the population proportion \(p\), the estimator is \(\widehat P\), and the estimate is \(\hat p=0.64\). The sample quartiles are \(Q_1=8\) and \(Q_3=17.75\), so \(\operatorname{IQR}=17.75-8=9.75\) percentage points: the middle half of the sampled initial equity offers lies from 8% to 17.75%. A different seed selects a different random set of rows, so all realised sample statistics, including the IQR, can change.

Task 3 — Standard errors and sample size

Use the sample output above.

  1. Estimate \(\operatorname{SE}(\bar X)\).
  2. Estimate \(\operatorname{SE}(\widehat P)\).
  3. Check the success–failure condition for \(\widehat P\).
  4. Predict how each SE changes if \(n\) increases from 100 to 400 while the estimated spread remains similar.
  5. The sample SD in part 1 was calculated with sd(), which divides by \(n-1\). State that number here and explain it in one sentence.
Check your answer

\[ \widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\bar X)=7.7079/\sqrt{100}=0.7708. \]

\[ \widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\widehat P) =\sqrt{0.64(0.36)/100}=0.0480. \]

The sample contains 64 successes and 36 failures, so both are at least 10. Increasing \(n\) fourfold divides both SEs by \(\sqrt 4=2\). The divisor is \(n-1=99\): the deviations are measured from the estimated sample mean and must sum to zero, so only 99 of them vary freely, and dividing by 99 removes the resulting downward bias (Lecture 7).

Task 4 — One pitch versus one mean

Suppose the population mean is \(\mu=13.21\), the population SD is \(\sigma=8.44\), and samples of \(n=100\) are drawn independently.

  1. State the approximate sampling distribution of \(\bar X\).
  2. Calculate \(P(\bar X>15)\).
  3. A classmate instead models a single pitch as \(X\sim N(13.21, 8.44^2)\) and calculates \(P(X>15)\). Compute that value too, and explain why the two answers are so different.
  4. Which of the two normal models did Lecture 6 reject for equity offers, and why does the CLT nevertheless justify the calculation in part 2?
Check your answer

The CLT gives approximately \(\bar X\mathrel{\dot\sim}N(13.21,\,8.44^2/100)\), with SE \(0.844\). Therefore

\[ P(\bar X>15) =1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{15-13.21}{0.844}\right) =1-\Phi(2.12) \approx0.017. \]

For a single pitch, \(z=(15-13.21)/8.44\approx0.21\), so \(P(X>15)\approx0.42\). The mean of 100 pitches varies ten times less than one pitch (\(\sigma/\sqrt{100}\)), so 15 is an ordinary value for one pitch but an extreme value for a mean of 100. Lecture 6 rejected the normal model for the pitch-level variable — equity offers are strongly right-skewed — so part 3 is a bad model. The CLT concerns the sample mean: averaging 100 independent draws produces a nearly normal sampling distribution even from a skewed population, which is why part 2 is defensible.

Task 5 — A proportion through the CLT

The frame deal proportion is \(p=0.612\). A random sample of \(n=100\) pitches is planned, with sample deal proportion \(\widehat P\).

  1. Calculate \(\operatorname{SE}(\widehat P)\) and check the success–failure condition.
  2. State the approximate sampling distribution of \(\widehat P\).
  3. Approximate the probability that the sample shows a deal rate of at most 50%, i.e. \(P(\widehat P\le0.50)\).
  4. Interpret the result in one sentence.
Check your answer

\(\operatorname{SE}(\widehat P)=\sqrt{0.612(0.388)/100}\approx0.0487\); the expected counts are \(np=61.2\) and \(n(1-p)=38.8\), both at least 10. Hence \(\widehat P\mathrel{\dot\sim}N(0.612,\,0.0487^2)\). Standardise:

\[ z=\frac{0.50-0.612}{0.0487}\approx-2.30, \qquad P(\widehat P\le0.50)\approx\Phi(-2.30)\approx0.011. \]

If the frame deal proportion is 61.2%, only about 1% of random samples of 100 would show a deal rate at or below 50% — such a sample would be surprising, the seed of the testing logic that Lecture 9 formalises.

After class

Timed individual practice — 12 minutes

Redo this unassisted and against the clock: no notes, no AI, formula sheet only — exam conditions. A random sample of 64 customers has mean monthly spending €82 and sample SD €24. Among the 64 customers, 40 renewed their subscription.

  1. Estimate the SE of mean spending.
  2. Estimate the SE of the renewal proportion and check its condition.
  3. State what would happen to both SEs at \(n=256\).
  4. Write one sentence distinguishing sampling uncertainty from selection bias.
  5. Treating \(\mu=82\) and \(\sigma=24\) as the population values, use the CLT to approximate the probability that a new sample of 64 shows a mean above €85.
Check a model answer

The estimated SE of the mean is \(24/\sqrt{64}=3\) euros. The renewal estimate is \(40/64=0.625\), with estimated SE \(\sqrt{0.625(0.375)/64}\approx0.0605\); the sample has 40 successes and 24 failures, so the success–failure condition holds. Increasing the sample size from 64 to 256 would halve both SEs if the spread remained similar. These SEs describe random sample-to-sample variation, not bias created by an unrepresentative sampling frame. For the new sample, \(\bar X\mathrel{\dot\sim}N(82,3^2)\), so \(z=(85-82)/3=1\) and \(P(\bar X>85)\approx1-\Phi(1)\approx0.16\).

Check your understanding

Complete from memory:

  1. A sampling distribution describes …
  2. The standard error of a mean equals …
  3. The success–failure condition requires …
  4. The CLT says … becomes approximately normal.
  5. A larger sample does not fix …
Check your answers
  1. How a statistic would vary across repeated random samples.
  2. \(\sigma/\sqrt n\) (estimated by \(S/\sqrt n\)).
  3. \(np\ge10\) and \(n(1-p)\ge10\).
  4. The standardised sample mean (and so, approximately, the sampling distribution of the mean) — not the original observations.
  5. Selection bias: how units entered the frame or the sample.

Make it yours (5 minutes)

Utility: two sentences

Think of your EiA venture — or any business you know well. Pick one rate or average you could estimate from about 100 observations (conversion rate, average basket, renewal share), and write two sentences: first state the SE you would roughly expect and what range of sample outcomes would therefore be unsurprising, then say what observed value would genuinely surprise you — the instinct that next week’s hypothesis tests make exact.

See an example, then write your own

Take a student-run campus meal-prep box venture. “From 100 orders, a renewal share near 60% has an SE of about 5 points, so observed shares between roughly 50% and 70% would not surprise me. A share of 45% would — that is more than two SEs below what I believe, and that instinct is exactly what next week’s tests make precise.”

You can discuss this task — and any other task in this tutorial — with Tilly, the course AI assistant on Canvas: ask it to sanity-check your SE guess for your own numbers.
