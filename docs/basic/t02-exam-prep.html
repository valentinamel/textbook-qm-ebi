Tutorial 2: Conditioning and relationships

By the end of Tutorial 2, you can

  • calculate conditional probabilities and apply Bayes’ rule and independence
  • find covariance and correlation from a joint table and interpret them without causal claims
  • read a correlation table in a research paper and check an AI answer

Tutorials are exam preparation sessions. Tutorial 2 takes place after Lecture 6, but it practises Lectures 4 and 5 only. The tasks below are exam-style questions. You first try each task in pairs. You may use AI for this, following Attempt, Ask, Verify, Explain. Then the class discusses the solution together.

Before class

Bring your after-class work from Lectures 4 and 5. Complete these statements without looking at the chapters again:

  1. In \(P(A\mid B)\), the reference class is …
  2. Variance averages …
  3. Events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent when …
  4. A joint PMF records …
  5. Covariance averages …
  6. Random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent when …

Use the formula sheet up to and including Lecture 5.

Review Mathematics Refresher: fractions and rearranging equations and summation notation if needed.

Check your preparation
  1. The reference class is \(B\), the event after the vertical bar.
  2. Variance averages squared deviations from a variable’s mean.
  3. \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\).
  4. A joint PMF records the probability of every possible pair of values.
  5. Covariance averages products of deviations from the two means.
  6. \(p_{X,Y}(x,y)=p_X(x)\,p_Y(y)\) in every cell of the joint table.

Tasks 1 to 3 and Task 5 use the Shark Tank file and the two events from Lectures 4 and 5, defined in the same way:

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
D <- sharks$deal_on_show == 1   # on-air deal

Task 1: Conditional probability and Bayes

Shark Tank data R reports the counts of the 1,441 pitches by deal and by period:

                Period
Deal             Seasons 1-8 Seasons 9-16
  No on-air deal         330          229
  On-air deal            377          505
  1. Calculate and interpret \(P(D\mid L)\).
  2. Calculate and interpret \(P(L\mid D)\).
  3. Verify \(P(D\cap L)=P(D\mid L)P(L)\).
  4. Use the law of total probability with the partition \(L^c\) and \(L\) to recover \(P(D)\).
  5. Show how Bayes’ rule gives \(P(L\mid D)\) from \(P(D\mid L)\).
Check your answer

\(P(D\mid L)=505/734\approx 0.688\): among late-season records, 68.8% reached an on-air deal. \(P(L\mid D)=505/882\approx 0.573\): among deal records, 57.3% came from late seasons. The joint probability is \(P(D\mid L)P(L)=(505/734)(734/1441)=505/1441\approx 0.350\).

The law of total probability gives

\[ P(D)=\frac{377}{707}\frac{707}{1441} +\frac{505}{734}\frac{734}{1441} =\frac{882}{1441}\approx 0.612. \]

Bayes’ rule gives

\[ P(L\mid D)=\frac{P(D\mid L)P(L)}{P(D)} =\frac{(505/734)(734/1441)}{882/1441} =\frac{505}{882}\approx 0.573. \]

Task 2: Independence of events (extra practice for home)

Shark Tank data Lecture 4 defined independence of events in two equivalent ways: through conditioning and through the product form. R reports:

          P_D   P_D_given_L     P_D_and_L P_D_times_P_L 
    0.6120749     0.6880109     0.3504511     0.3117717
  1. Use the definition with conditioning to decide whether \(D\) and \(L\) are independent in these records.
  2. Use the product form to reach the same conclusion.
  3. Explain why “not independent” does not mean “late season causes a deal.”
  4. Explain why the early and late events are disjoint but not independent.
Check your answer

The deal probability is about 0.612 overall and 0.688 within late seasons, so conditioning on \(L\) changes it. In the same way, the observed joint probability is about 0.350, while the product \(P(D)P(L)\) is about 0.312. So \(D\) and \(L\) are not independent in these records. This is an association in televised pitches that the producers selected. It does not show that the season causes deals, because other factors also changed over the seasons. Early and late are disjoint because they cannot occur together. Observing one makes the other impossible, so disjoint events with positive probability are dependent.

x <- as.integer(D)
y <- as.integer(L)

  1. Verify that the joint PMF sums to 1.
  2. Recover the marginal PMF of \(X\) by summing rows.
  3. Verify \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[X^2]-\left(E[X]\right)^2\).
  4. Verify \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]\).
  5. Interpret the sign of the covariance.
  6. Explain what the correlation adds to the covariance, and what it does not prove.
  7. Calculate the conditional means \(E[X\mid Y=1]\) and \(E[X\mid Y=0]\) from the joint PMF, and say what they mean.
  8. The sum \(X+Y\) counts how many of the two events, deal and late season, happened for a pitch. Calculate \(E[X+Y]\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)\) from the values in the output.
Check your answer
  1. The four joint probabilities sum to 1: \(0.229+0.159+0.262+0.350=1\).
  2. Summing each row across the two \(Y\) columns gives \(P(X=0)=0.229+0.159=0.388\) and \(P(X=1)=0.262+0.350=0.612\). As counts, these are \(559/1441\) and \(882/1441\).
  3. Because \(X\) takes only the values 0 and 1, \(X^2=X\) and \(E[X^2]=E[X]\). So \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[X]-\left(E[X]\right)^2=0.612-0.612^2\approx 0.237\), as in the output.
  4. \(E[XY]=P(X=1,Y=1)\approx 0.3505\), and \(E[X]E[Y]\approx 0.6121\times0.5094\approx 0.3118\). The difference is about 0.0387, as in the output.
  5. The covariance is positive. The value 1 occurs for both variables together a little more often than it would under independence: deals and late seasons go together.
  6. The correlation divides the covariance by both standard deviations. The result, about 0.16, has no units and lies between \(-1\) and \(1\): a weak positive linear association. It does not show a cause, and it does not say how important the association is in practice. A correlation of zero would not always mean independence, as the U-shaped pattern in Lecture 5 shows.
  7. Divide each column of the joint PMF by its sum. The column sums are \(P(Y=1)\approx 0.5094\) and \(P(Y=0)\approx 0.4906\). Because \(X\) is 0 or 1, only the \(X=1\) entry counts: \(E[X\mid Y=1]\approx 0.3505/0.5094\approx 0.688\) and \(E[X\mid Y=0]\approx 0.2616/0.4906\approx 0.533\). Among late-season pitches the average of the deal indicator is 0.688, so 68.8% reached a deal. Among early-season pitches it is 53.3%.
  8. \(E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]=0.612+0.509=1.121\). \(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y) +2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0.237+0.250+2\times0.039\approx 0.565\). Without the covariance term the result would be 0.487. The sum varies more because deals and late seasons go together.

Task 4: Read a correlation table from a paper

Research papers in management usually start their results with one table of means, standard deviations (SD), and correlations. The table below is an invented example in the layout of such a table. It does not come from a real study. It describes a survey of 240 employees with four variables:

  • creative self-efficacy: how much an employee believes in his or her own ability to be creative, rated from 1 to 7.
  • job tenure: the number of years in the current job.
  • supervisor support: how much support the employee gets from the supervisor, rated from 1 to 7.
  • creative performance: how creative the employee’s work is, rated from 1 to 7.

The columns “Mean” and “SD” describe each variable alone. The columns 1, 2, and 3 contain correlations. Each number is the correlation between the variable in that row and the variable with that number. For example, the 0.31 in row 3 and column 1 is the correlation between supervisor support and creative self-efficacy.

Variable Mean SD 1 2 3
1. Creative self-efficacy (1 to 7) 5.10 0.90
2. Job tenure (years) 6.40 4.20 0.08
3. Supervisor support (1 to 7) 4.80 1.10 0.31 0.05
4. Creative performance (1 to 7) 4.20 1.00 0.36 0.12 0.28
  1. Find the correlation between creative self-efficacy and creative performance, and interpret its sign and strength in one sentence.
  2. Which pair of variables has the weakest linear association?
  3. A manager concludes: “Raising supervisor support will raise creative performance.” Name one third variable, or one other reason, that could produce the correlation of 0.28 without support causing performance.
  4. State one thing this table cannot tell you about an individual employee.
Check your answer
  1. The correlation is 0.36, in row 4 and column 1: a moderate positive linear association. Employees with higher creative self-efficacy tended to be rated higher on creative performance in this sample.
  2. The weakest association is between job tenure and supervisor support, with a correlation of 0.05.
  3. Several other explanations fit the same number. Employees in creative roles may get more support and also perform more creatively. Or supervisors may give more support to employees who already perform well. A correlation table cannot separate these explanations.
  4. The table describes how the variables move together across 240 employees. It says nothing certain about any single employee. Many employees with high self-efficacy have average performance ratings.

Task 5: Check the AI

Shark Tank data You asked an AI assistant about the deal indicator \(X\) and the late-season indicator \(Y\) of Task 3. It answered:

“The covariance of deal and late season is only 0.039, so the two are almost independent. The correlation of 0.16 shows that a late season causes a deal for 16% of the pitches.”

Check the answer sentence by sentence. Find what is wrong and write the corrected answer.

Check your answer

The first sentence is wrong. The size of a covariance depends on the units of the two variables, so a small number does not mean a weak association. Independence needs \(P(X=1,Y=1)=P(X=1)P(Y=1)\), and here 0.350 differs from 0.312, so \(X\) and \(Y\) are not independent. The second sentence is also wrong. A correlation is not a share of pitches, and it does not show a cause. Corrected answer: deals and late seasons go together, with a weak positive linear association (\(r\approx0.16\)). The records do not show that the season causes deals.

Task 6: Total probability and Bayes’ rule from given rates

An invented example. A start-up prints spare parts for household appliances on two 3D printers. The old printer makes 60% of all parts and the new printer makes 40%. Of the parts from the old printer, 5% fail the quality check. Of the parts from the new printer, 10% fail, because the team is still learning how to set up the new printer. One part is selected at random. Let \(N\) be the event that the part comes from the new printer, and \(F\) the event that the part fails the check.

  1. Write the four given numbers as probabilities, with the symbols \(N\), \(N^c\), and \(F\). Name the reference class of \(P(F\mid N)\).
  2. Calculate \(P(F\cap N)\) and \(P(F\cap N^c)\) with the multiplication rule.
  3. Calculate \(P(F)\) with the law of total probability.
  4. Draw the tree for 1,000 parts and state the four counts at its end. Use the counts to calculate \(P(N\mid F)\).
  5. Calculate \(P(N\mid F)\) again with the formula of Bayes’ rule.
  6. Decide whether \(N\) and \(F\) are independent. Show the comparison that you use.
  7. A colleague reads your result and says: “So 57% of the parts from the new printer fail.” Explain what is wrong, and write the correct sentence.
Check your answer
  1. \(P(N)=0.40\), \(P(N^c)=0.60\), \(P(F\mid N)=0.10\), and \(P(F\mid N^c)=0.05\). The reference class of \(P(F\mid N)\) is the parts from the new printer.
  2. \(P(F\cap N)=P(F\mid N)\,P(N)=0.10\times0.40=0.04\) and \(P(F\cap N^c)=P(F\mid N^c)\,P(N^c)=0.05\times0.60=0.03\).
  3. \(P(F)=0.04+0.03=0.07\): 7% of all parts fail. This overall rate lies between the two rates of 5% and 10%. It is closer to 5%, because the old printer makes more parts.
  4. Of 1,000 parts, 400 come from the new printer and 600 from the old one. New printer: 40 fail and 360 pass. Old printer: 30 fail and 570 pass. In total \(40+30=70\) parts fail, and 40 of them come from the new printer: \(P(N\mid F)=40/70\approx0.571\).
  5. The formula gives the same value: \[ P(N\mid F)=\frac{P(F\mid N)\,P(N)} {P(F\mid N)\,P(N)+P(F\mid N^c)\,P(N^c)} =\frac{0.04}{0.04+0.03}=\frac{0.04}{0.07}\approx0.571. \]
  6. \(P(F\mid N)=0.10\), while \(P(F)=0.07\). Learning that a part comes from the new printer raises the probability that it fails, so \(N\) and \(F\) are not independent. The product form says the same: \(P(F)\,P(N)=0.07\times0.40=0.028\), which is not equal to \(P(F\cap N)=0.04\).
  7. The colleague reversed the condition. The 57% is \(P(N\mid F)\): of the failed parts, 57% come from the new printer. The share of the new printer’s parts that fail is \(P(F\mid N)=10\%\). A correct sentence is: the new printer makes 40% of all parts and 57% of the failed parts.

Task 7: Read a joint PMF with three values per variable

An invented example. A founder sells houseplants online. One order is selected at random. \(X\) is the number of plants in the order (1, 2, or 3) and \(Y\) is the number of decorative pots in the order (0, 1, or 2). The joint PMF is:

\(p_{X,Y}(x,y)\) \(Y=0\) \(Y=1\) \(Y=2\)
\(X=1\) 0.25 0.10 0.05
\(X=2\) 0.10 0.15 0.05
\(X=3\) 0.05 0.15 0.10
  1. Check that the table is a valid joint PMF.
  2. Find the marginal PMF of \(X\) and the marginal PMF of \(Y\).
  3. Calculate \(P(X\le2,\ Y=2)\), the probability that an order has at most two plants and two pots. Then calculate \(P(X\le2\text{ or }Y=2)\) with the addition rule.
  4. Find the conditional PMF of \(X\) given \(Y=0\), and check that it sums to 1.
  5. Calculate the conditional mean \(E[X\mid Y=0]\) and the mean \(E[X]\). Interpret the difference in one sentence.
  6. Check the product form of independence in the cell \((x,y)=(1,0)\). What do you conclude, and why is one cell enough here?
Check your answer
  1. All nine entries are at least 0. The three rows sum to 0.40, 0.30, and 0.30, so all entries together sum to 1.
  2. Sum along each row for \(X\): \(p_X(1)=0.25+0.10+0.05=0.40\), \(p_X(2)=0.10+0.15+0.05=0.30\), and \(p_X(3)=0.05+0.15+0.10=0.30\). Sum down each column for \(Y\): \(p_Y(0)=0.25+0.10+0.05=0.40\), \(p_Y(1)=0.10+0.15+0.15=0.40\), and \(p_Y(2)=0.05+0.05+0.10=0.20\).
  3. The event “at most two plants and two pots” covers the cells \((1,2)\) and \((2,2)\), so \(P(X\le2,\ Y=2)=0.05+0.05=0.10\). With \(P(X\le2)=0.40+0.30=0.70\) and \(P(Y=2)=0.20\), the addition rule gives \(P(X\le2\text{ or }Y=2)=0.70+0.20-0.10=0.80\). Check: only the cells \((3,0)\) and \((3,1)\) are outside this event, and \(1-0.05-0.15=0.80\).
  4. Divide the \(Y=0\) column by its sum, 0.40: \(p_{X\mid Y}(1\mid0)=0.25/0.40=0.625\), \(p_{X\mid Y}(2\mid0)=0.10/0.40=0.25\), and \(p_{X\mid Y}(3\mid0)=0.05/0.40=0.125\). The three values sum to 1.
  5. \(E[X\mid Y=0]=1(0.625)+2(0.25)+3(0.125)=0.625+0.50+0.375=1.5\) plants. \(E[X]=1(0.40)+2(0.30)+3(0.30)=0.40+0.60+0.90=1.9\) plants. Orders without a pot contain 1.5 plants on average. That is 0.4 plants fewer than the average of all orders, about 21% fewer.
  6. The entry is \(p_{X,Y}(1,0)=0.25\). The product of its margins is \(p_X(1)\,p_Y(0)=0.40\times0.40=0.16\). The two numbers differ, so \(X\) and \(Y\) are not independent. Independence needs the product form in every cell, so one cell that does not match is enough to show dependence.

Task 8: Covariance and correlation from the same table

Use the joint PMF of Task 7 for the number of plants \(X\) and the number of pots \(Y\). Its marginal PMFs are \(p_X=0.40,\ 0.30,\ 0.30\) for the values 1, 2, 3 and \(p_Y=0.40,\ 0.40,\ 0.20\) for the values 0, 1, 2.

  1. Calculate \(E[X]\) and \(E[Y]\).
  2. Calculate \(E[XY]\) as a double sum: in every cell multiply \(x\), \(y\), and the probability of the cell, and add the nine products.
  3. Calculate \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)\) with \(E[XY]-E[X]\,E[Y]\) and interpret its sign.
  4. Calculate \(\operatorname{Var}(X)\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)\) with the computational form.
  5. Calculate the correlation of \(X\) and \(Y\). Interpret its direction and its strength.
  6. The founder says: “An order with three plants always has a pot in it.” Use the joint PMF of Task 7 to show that this is wrong. Then say what the correlation of part 5 can and cannot tell you about one order.
Check your answer
  1. \(E[X]=1(0.40)+2(0.30)+3(0.30)=1.9\) plants and \(E[Y]=0(0.40)+1(0.40)+2(0.20)=0.8\) pots.
  2. The three cells with \(y=0\) give the product 0. The other six cells give \(1\times1\times0.10=0.10\), \(1\times2\times0.05=0.10\), \(2\times1\times0.15=0.30\), \(2\times2\times0.05=0.20\), \(3\times1\times0.15=0.45\), and \(3\times2\times0.10=0.60\). Their sum is \(E[XY]=0.10+0.10+0.30+0.20+0.45+0.60=1.75\).
  3. \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=1.75-1.9\times0.8=1.75-1.52=0.23\). The sign is positive: orders with more plants than average tend to have more pots than average.
  4. \(E[X^2]=1(0.40)+4(0.30)+9(0.30)=0.40+1.20+2.70=4.30\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=4.30-1.9^2=4.30-3.61=0.69\). \(E[Y^2]=0(0.40)+1(0.40)+4(0.20)=1.20\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)=1.20-0.8^2=1.20-0.64=0.56\).
  5. The standard deviations are \(\operatorname{SD}(X)=\sqrt{0.69}\approx0.831\) and \(\operatorname{SD}(Y)=\sqrt{0.56}\approx0.748\). The correlation is \[ \rho_{XY}=\frac{0.23}{0.831\times0.748}=\frac{0.23}{0.6216}\approx0.37. \] It is a moderate positive linear association, and it has no units.
  6. The cell \((3,0)\) has probability 0.05. Among the orders with three plants this is \(0.05/0.30\approx0.17\), about one order in six, so an order with three plants does not always have a pot. A correlation of 0.37 describes a tendency across all orders. It cannot say what one order contains.

Task 9: Two independent variables (extra practice for home)

An invented example. A founder makes soap and sells it in gift sets through two shops: a concept store in Tilburg and a museum shop in Groningen. \(X\) is the number of gift sets sold on a Saturday in Tilburg, and \(Y\) is the number sold on the same Saturday in Groningen. The two shops have different customers, so the founder assumes that \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent. The marginal PMFs are:

Value 0 1 2
\(p_X(x)\) 0.4 0.5 0.1
\(p_Y(y)\) 0.5 0.4 0.1
  1. Build the joint PMF of \(X\) and \(Y\). Show the calculation for the cell \((x,y)=(1,1)\), and check that the nine entries sum to 1.
  2. Calculate \(E[X]\), \(E[Y]\), and \(E[XY]\). Calculate \(E[XY]\) as a double sum over the cells of your table.
  3. Calculate \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)\). Which statement of Lecture 5 gives this result without a calculation?
  4. Calculate \(E[X+Y]\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)\). Use \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=0.41\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)=0.44\).
  5. A friend suggests another joint table with the same two marginal PMFs. In the friend’s table, \(p_{X,Y}(2,2)=0.03\). Can \(X\) and \(Y\) be independent in the friend’s table? Explain.
Check your answer

  1. For independent variables, every cell is the product of its two margins. For the cell \((1,1)\): \(p_X(1)\,p_Y(1)=0.5\times0.4=0.20\).

    \(p_{X,Y}(x,y)\) \(Y=0\) \(Y=1\) \(Y=2\) \(p_X(x)\)
    \(X=0\) 0.20 0.16 0.04 0.4
    \(X=1\) 0.25 0.20 0.05 0.5
    \(X=2\) 0.05 0.04 0.01 0.1
    \(p_Y(y)\) 0.5 0.4 0.1 1

    The three rows sum to 0.4, 0.5, and 0.1, so the nine entries sum to 1.

  2. \(E[X]=0(0.4)+1(0.5)+2(0.1)=0.7\) and \(E[Y]=0(0.5)+1(0.4)+2(0.1)=0.6\) gift sets. In the double sum, every cell with \(x=0\) or \(y=0\) gives the product 0. The other four cells give \(E[XY]=1\times1\times0.20+1\times2\times0.05+2\times1\times0.04+ 2\times2\times0.01=0.20+0.10+0.08+0.04=0.42\).

  3. \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0.42-0.7\times0.6=0.42-0.42=0\). Lecture 5 states that independence implies zero covariance, because \(E[XY]=E[X]\,E[Y]\) for independent variables.

  4. \(E[X+Y]=0.7+0.6=1.3\) gift sets. \(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=0.41+0.44+2\times0=0.85\). For independent variables the variances add.

  5. No. Independence needs \(p_{X,Y}(2,2)=p_X(2)\,p_Y(2)=0.1\times0.1=0.01\). The friend’s entry of 0.03 is three times as large. One cell that does not match is enough, so \(X\) and \(Y\) are dependent in the friend’s table. The two marginal PMFs alone do not determine the joint PMF.

Task 10: The sum of two variables, with positive and negative covariance (extra practice for home)

An invented example. A founder sells ice cream from a cart in the park. Her daily revenue from ice cream, \(X\), has a mean of 320 euros and a standard deviation of 50 euros. She wants to add a second product and compares two options. With each option, the daily revenue from the second product, \(Y\), has a mean of 180 euros and a standard deviation of 30 euros.

  • Lemonade sells well on the hot days on which ice cream also sells well: \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=600\).
  • Hot chocolate sells well on the cool days on which ice cream sells badly: \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=-600\).
  1. Calculate \(\operatorname{Var}(X)\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)\).
  2. Calculate the expected total revenue \(E[X+Y]\) for each option.
  3. Calculate the variance and the standard deviation of the total revenue with lemonade.
  4. Calculate the variance and the standard deviation of the total revenue with hot chocolate.
  5. Calculate the correlation of \(X\) and \(Y\) for each option.
  6. Which option gives the more stable total revenue? Explain in one or two sentences why the two options have the same expected total and a different spread.
Check your answer
  1. \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=50^2=2{,}500\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)=30^2=900\).
  2. \(E[X+Y]=320+180=500\) euros for both options. The rule for the expected value of a sum does not use the covariance.
  3. Lemonade: \(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=2{,}500+900+2\times600=4{,}600\), so \(\operatorname{SD}(X+Y)=\sqrt{4{,}600}\approx67.82\) euros.
  4. Hot chocolate: \(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=2{,}500+900+2\times(-600)=2{,}200\), so \(\operatorname{SD}(X+Y)=\sqrt{2{,}200}\approx46.90\) euros.
  5. Lemonade: \(\rho_{XY}=600/(50\times30)=0.4\). Hot chocolate: \(\rho_{XY}=-600/(50\times30)=-0.4\). Both are moderate linear associations, one positive and one negative.
  6. Hot chocolate gives the more stable total. Its standard deviation is about 31% smaller (47 against 68 euros), and the expected total is 500 euros in both cases. With a negative covariance, a weak day for one product tends to be a good day for the other, so the two revenues partly cancel each other out. With a positive covariance, good days and bad days come together, and the total varies more.

Check a model answer

Among the pitches in Seasons 1 to 8, 53.3% reached an on-air deal. Among the pitches in Seasons 9 to 16, 68.8% did. The difference of 15.5 percentage points describes these records. It does not show a causal effect of the season. Season and equity initially offered have a moderate negative linear association (\(r\approx-0.42\)). Equity offered and the deal indicator have a weak negative linear association (\(r\approx-0.11\)). The entrepreneurs chose their offers themselves, so pitches with low and high offers may differ in other ways too. The outcome is a deal on air, not a completed investment.

Check your answers
  1. The conditioning event gives the denominator or reference class.
