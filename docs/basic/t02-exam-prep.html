Tutorial 2: Conditioning, relationships, and the normal model

By the end of Tutorial 2, you can

  • calculate conditional probabilities and apply Bayes’ rule and independence,
  • find covariance and correlation from a joint table and interpret them without causal claims,
  • read a correlation table in a research paper and check an AI answer,
  • calculate probabilities and values from uniform and normal models.

Tutorials are exam preparation sessions. The tasks below are exam-style questions. Attempt each task before you open its answer. You may use AI, following Attempt, Ask, Verify, Explain.

Before class

Bring your after-class work from Lectures 4 to 6. Complete these statements without looking at the chapters again:

  1. In \(P(A\mid B)\), the reference class is …
  2. Variance averages …
  3. Events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent when …
  4. A joint PMF records …
  5. Covariance averages …
  6. Random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent when …
  7. Standardising an observation \(x\) from \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) means calculating …

Use the formula sheet up to and including Lecture 6.

Review Mathematics Refresher: fractions and rearranging equations and summation notation if needed.

Check your preparation
  1. The reference class is \(B\), the event after the vertical bar.
  2. Variance averages squared deviations from a variable’s mean.
  3. \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\).
  4. A joint PMF records the probability of every possible pair of values.
  5. Covariance averages products of deviations from the two means.
  6. \(p_{X,Y}(x,y)=p_X(x)\,p_Y(y)\) in every cell of the joint table.
  7. \(z=(x-\mu)/\sigma\): the number of population SDs between \(x\) and the mean.

Task 1 uses the Shark Tank file. The event \(D\) is an on-air deal. The event \(M\) is a modest offer: the founders offered at most 10% of their company (equity_offered_pct of 10 or less, the median offer in the file).

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
D <- sharks$deal_on_show == 1           # on-air deal
M <- sharks$equity_offered_pct <= 10    # modest offer: at most 10%

Task 1: Two events, Bayes’ rule, and covariance

Shark Tank data One of the 1,441 pitches is selected at random, each with the same probability. R reports the counts of the pitches by deal and by offer:

                Offer
Deal             Above 10% At most 10%  Sum
  No on-air deal       263         296  559
  On-air deal          325         557  882
  Sum                  588         853 1441

Let \(X\) be the 0/1 deal indicator and \(Y\) the 0/1 modest-offer indicator. R also reports their joint PMF and a few summaries:

   Y
X       0     1
  0 0.183 0.205
  1 0.226 0.387
        E_X         E_Y        E_XY  covariance correlation 
     0.6121      0.5920      0.3865      0.0242      0.1011

  1. Calculate \(P(D\mid M)\) and \(P(M\mid D)\). Name the reference class of each and interpret each in one sentence.

  2. Show how Bayes’ rule gives \(P(M\mid D)\) from \(P(D\mid M)\).

  3. Use the law of total probability with the partition \(M^c\) and \(M\) to recover \(P(D)\).

  4. Verify \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]\) from the output and interpret its sign in one sentence.

  5. You asked an AI assistant about \(X\) and \(Y\). It answered:

    “The covariance of deal and modest offer is only 0.024, so the two are almost independent. The correlation of 0.10 shows that a modest offer causes a deal for 10% of the pitches.”

    The answer contains two errors. Name and explain each error in at most three sentences. Then write the corrected answer in at most two sentences.

Answer structure for parts 1 to 4: formula, numbers inserted, result, and one sentence of interpretation.

Check your answer
  1. \(P(D\mid M)=557/853\approx0.653\). The reference class is the 853 pitches with a modest offer: 65.3% of them reached an on-air deal. \(P(M\mid D)=557/882\approx0.632\). The reference class is the 882 deals: 63.2% of them came from pitches that offered at most 10%. The two numbers answer different questions, because the denominators differ.
  2. Bayes’ rule gives \[ P(M\mid D)=\frac{P(D\mid M)P(M)}{P(D)} =\frac{(557/853)(853/1441)}{882/1441} =\frac{557}{882}\approx0.632. \]
  3. The law of total probability gives \[ P(D)=\frac{325}{588}\frac{588}{1441} +\frac{557}{853}\frac{853}{1441} =\frac{882}{1441}\approx0.612. \]
  4. \(E[XY]=P(X=1,Y=1)\approx0.3865\) and \(E[X]E[Y]\approx0.6121\times0.5920\approx0.3623\). The difference is about 0.0242, as in the output. The covariance is positive: deals and modest offers occur together a little more often than they would under independence.
  5. The first sentence is wrong. The size of a covariance depends on the units of the two variables, so a small number does not mean a weak association. Independence needs \(P(X=1,Y=1)=P(X=1)P(Y=1)\), and here 0.387 differs from 0.362, so \(X\) and \(Y\) are not independent. The second sentence is also wrong. A correlation is not a share of pitches, and it does not show a cause. Corrected answer: deals and modest offers go together, with a weak positive linear association (\(r\approx0.10\)). The records do not show that a modest offer causes a deal, because the founders chose their offers themselves.

Task 2: Total probability and Bayes’ rule from given rates

A start-up prints spare parts for household appliances on two 3D printers. The old printer makes 60% of all parts and the new printer makes 40%. Of the parts from the old printer, 5% fail the quality check. Of the parts from the new printer, 10% fail, because the team is still learning how to set up the new printer. One part is selected at random. Let \(N\) be the event that the part comes from the new printer, and \(F\) the event that the part fails the check.

  1. Write the four given numbers as probabilities, with the symbols \(N\), \(N^c\), and \(F\). Name the reference class of \(P(F\mid N)\).
  2. Calculate \(P(F\cap N)\) and \(P(F\cap N^c)\) with the multiplication rule.
  3. Calculate \(P(F)\) with the law of total probability.
  4. Draw the tree for 1,000 parts and state the four counts at its end. Use the counts to calculate \(P(N\mid F)\).
  5. Calculate \(P(N\mid F)\) again with the formula of Bayes’ rule.
  6. Decide whether \(N\) and \(F\) are independent. Show the comparison that you use.
  7. A colleague reads your result and says: “So 57% of the parts from the new printer fail.” Explain in one sentence what is wrong, and write the correct sentence.

Answer structure for each calculation: formula, numbers inserted, result, and one sentence of interpretation.

Check your answer
  1. \(P(N)=0.40\), \(P(N^c)=0.60\), \(P(F\mid N)=0.10\), and \(P(F\mid N^c)=0.05\). The reference class of \(P(F\mid N)\) is the parts from the new printer.
  2. \(P(F\cap N)=P(F\mid N)\,P(N)=0.10\times0.40=0.04\) and \(P(F\cap N^c)=P(F\mid N^c)\,P(N^c)=0.05\times0.60=0.03\).
  3. \(P(F)=0.04+0.03=0.07\): 7% of all parts fail. This overall rate lies between the two rates of 5% and 10%. It is closer to 5%, because the old printer makes more parts.
  4. Of 1,000 parts, 400 come from the new printer and 600 from the old one. New printer: 40 fail and 360 pass. Old printer: 30 fail and 570 pass. In total \(40+30=70\) parts fail, and 40 of them come from the new printer: \(P(N\mid F)=40/70\approx0.571\).
  5. The formula gives the same value: \[ P(N\mid F)=\frac{P(F\mid N)\,P(N)} {P(F\mid N)\,P(N)+P(F\mid N^c)\,P(N^c)} =\frac{0.04}{0.04+0.03}=\frac{0.04}{0.07}\approx0.571. \]
  6. \(P(F\mid N)=0.10\), while \(P(F)=0.07\). Learning that a part comes from the new printer raises the probability that it fails, so \(N\) and \(F\) are not independent. The product form says the same: \(P(F)\,P(N)=0.07\times0.40=0.028\), which is not equal to \(P(F\cap N)=0.04\).
  7. The colleague reversed the condition. The 57% is \(P(N\mid F)\): of the failed parts, 57% come from the new printer. The share of the new printer’s parts that fail is \(P(F\mid N)=10\%\). A correct sentence is: the new printer makes 40% of all parts and 57% of the failed parts.

fail_new <- 0.10
fail_old <- 0.05

                         new_pass = p_new * (1 - fail_new),
                         old_fail = (1 - p_new) * fail_old,
                         old_pass = (1 - p_new) * (1 - fail_old))
printer_tree
new_fail new_pass old_fail old_pass 
      40      360       30      570 
p_fail <- fail_new * p_new + fail_old * (1 - p_new)
c(P_F_and_N = fail_new * p_new, P_F_and_not_N = fail_old * (1 - p_new),
  P_F = p_fail, P_N_given_F = fail_new * p_new / p_fail,

Task 3: Covariance and correlation from the same table

A founder sells houseplants online. One order is selected at random. \(X\) is the number of plants in the order (1, 2, or 3) and \(Y\) is the number of decorative pots in the order (0, 1, or 2). The joint PMF is:

\(p_{X,Y}(x,y)\) \(Y=0\) \(Y=1\) \(Y=2\)
\(X=1\) 0.25 0.10 0.05
\(X=2\) 0.10 0.15 0.05
\(X=3\) 0.05 0.15 0.10

Its marginal PMFs are \(p_X=0.40,\ 0.30,\ 0.30\) for the values 1, 2, 3 and \(p_Y=0.40,\ 0.40,\ 0.20\) for the values 0, 1, 2.

  1. Calculate \(E[X]\) and \(E[Y]\).
  2. Calculate \(E[XY]\) as a double sum: in every cell multiply \(x\), \(y\), and the probability of the cell, and add the nine products.
  3. Calculate \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)\) with \(E[XY]-E[X]\,E[Y]\) and interpret its sign.
  4. Calculate \(\operatorname{Var}(X)\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)\) with the computational form.
  5. Calculate the correlation of \(X\) and \(Y\). Interpret its direction and its strength.
  6. The founder says: “An order with three plants always has a pot in it.” Use the joint PMF to show that this is wrong. Then say in one sentence what the correlation of part 5 can and cannot tell you about one order.

Answer structure for each calculation: formula, numbers inserted, result, and one sentence of interpretation.

Check your answer
  1. \(E[X]=1(0.40)+2(0.30)+3(0.30)=1.9\) plants and \(E[Y]=0(0.40)+1(0.40)+2(0.20)=0.8\) pots.
  2. The three cells with \(y=0\) give the product 0. The other six cells give \(1\times1\times0.10=0.10\), \(1\times2\times0.05=0.10\), \(2\times1\times0.15=0.30\), \(2\times2\times0.05=0.20\), \(3\times1\times0.15=0.45\), and \(3\times2\times0.10=0.60\). Their sum is \[ E[XY]=0.10+0.10+0.30+0.20+0.45+0.60=1.75. \]
  3. \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=1.75-1.9\times0.8=1.75-1.52=0.23\). The sign is positive: orders with more plants than average tend to have more pots than average.
  4. The computational form gives \[ \begin{aligned} E[X^2]&=1(0.40)+4(0.30)+9(0.30)=0.40+1.20+2.70=4.30,\\ \operatorname{Var}(X)&=4.30-1.9^2=4.30-3.61=0.69,\\ E[Y^2]&=0(0.40)+1(0.40)+4(0.20)=1.20,\\ \operatorname{Var}(Y)&=1.20-0.8^2=1.20-0.64=0.56. \end{aligned} \]
  5. The standard deviations are \(\operatorname{SD}(X)=\sqrt{0.69}\approx0.831\) and \(\operatorname{SD}(Y)=\sqrt{0.56}\approx0.748\). The correlation is \[ \rho_{XY}=\frac{0.23}{0.831\times0.748}=\frac{0.23}{0.6216}\approx0.37. \] It is a moderate positive linear association, and it has no units.
  6. The cell \((3,0)\) has probability 0.05. Among the orders with three plants this is \(0.05/0.30\approx0.17\), about one order in six, so an order with three plants does not always have a pot. A correlation of 0.37 describes a tendency across all orders. It cannot say what one order contains.

Verification in R:

plant_pot <- matrix(c(0.25, 0.10, 0.05,
                      0.10, 0.15, 0.05,
                      0.05, 0.15, 0.10), nrow = 3, byrow = TRUE,
                    dimnames = list(plants = 1:3, pots = 0:2))
plants <- 1:3
pots <- 0:2
p_plants <- rowSums(plant_pot)
p_pots <- colSums(plant_pot)
mean_plants <- sum(plants * p_plants)
mean_pots <- sum(pots * p_pots)
var_plants <- sum(plants^2 * p_plants) - mean_plants^2
var_pots <- sum(pots^2 * p_pots) - mean_pots^2
cov_plants_pots <- sum(outer(plants, pots) * plant_pot) - mean_plants * mean_pots

  SD_X = sqrt(var_plants), SD_Y = sqrt(var_pots),
  correlation = cov_plants_pots / sqrt(var_plants * var_pots),
  no_pot_given_3_plants = plant_pot["3", "0"] / p_plants[["3"]])

Task 4: A normal model in both directions

A founder runs an escape room. The time that a team needs to solve the room, \(Y\), is modelled as \(Y\sim N(45,5^2)\), in minutes. The standard deviation is 5 minutes.

Supplied values: \(P(Z\le0.8)=0.7881\), \(P(Z\le1.2)=0.8849\), and the 0.95 quantile \(z_{0.95}=1.645\).

  1. Calculate the probability that a team needs at most 49 minutes.
  2. Calculate the probability that a team needs between 39 and 49 minutes.
  3. The founder wants a time limit that 5% of the teams exceed. Calculate this limit.
  4. Say in one sentence what the supplied quantile 1.645 means for a standard normal variable \(Z\).

Answer structure for each calculation: formula, numbers inserted, result, and one sentence of interpretation.

Check your answer

  1. Standardise: \(z=(49-45)/5=0.8\). So \(P(Y\le49)=P(Z\le0.8)=0.7881\). About 79% of the teams need at most 49 minutes.

  2. The two z values are \((39-45)/5=-1.2\) and \(0.8\). The supplied values contain only positive z values, so use the symmetry rule and then the complement rule: \(P(Z\le-1.2)=P(Z>1.2)=1-0.8849=0.1151\). Then

    \[ P(39\le Y\le49)=P(Z\le0.8)-P(Z\le-1.2)=0.7881-0.1151=0.6730. \]

    About 67% of the teams need between 39 and 49 minutes.

  3. If 5% of the teams exceed the limit, 95% are at or below it. The limit is the 0.95 quantile of \(Y\): \(y=\mu+z\sigma=45+1.645\times5=53.23\) minutes. A limit of about 53 minutes fits.

  4. 95% of the values of \(Z\) are at or below 1.645, and 5% are above it.

Parts 1 and 2 go from a value to a probability. Part 3 goes from a probability to a value. One limit: the normal curve is a model. The answers are only as good as the fit of this model to the recorded times.

Verification in R:

c(p_at_most_49 = pnorm(49, mean = 45, sd = 5),
  p_39_to_49 = pnorm(49, mean = 45, sd = 5) - pnorm(39, mean = 45, sd = 5),
  by_hand = 0.7881 - (1 - 0.8849),
  limit = qnorm(0.95, mean = 45, sd = 5),
  limit_by_hand = 45 + 1.645 * 5)
 p_at_most_49    p_39_to_49       by_hand         limit limit_by_hand 
    0.7881446     0.6730749     0.6730000    53.2242681    53.2250000

Task 5: Conditional means and the sum of two indicators

Shark Tank data Take the deal indicator \(X\), the modest-offer indicator \(Y\), and the R output of Task 1 again. R also reports the two variances:

 Var_X  Var_Y 
0.2374 0.2415
  1. Calculate the conditional means \(E[X\mid Y=1]\) and \(E[X\mid Y=0]\) from the joint PMF. Say in one sentence what they mean.
  2. The sum \(X+Y\) counts how many of the two events, deal and modest offer, happened for a pitch. Calculate \(E[X+Y]\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)\) from the values in the output.
Check your answer
  1. Divide each column of the joint PMF by its sum. The column sums are \(P(Y=1)\approx0.5920\) and \(P(Y=0)\approx0.4080\). Because \(X\) is 0 or 1, only the \(X=1\) entry counts: \[ E[X\mid Y=1]\approx\frac{0.3865}{0.5920}\approx0.653,\qquad E[X\mid Y=0]\approx\frac{0.2255}{0.4080}\approx0.553. \] Among pitches with a modest offer, 65.3% reached a deal. Among pitches that offered more than 10%, 55.3% did.
  2. \(E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]=0.612+0.592=1.204\). The variance is \[ \operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y) +2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0.237+0.242+2\times0.024\approx0.527. \] Without the covariance term the result would be 0.479, about 9% less. The sum varies more because deals and modest offers go together.

Task 6: Independence of events

Shark Tank data This task uses the deal event \(D\) and the modest-offer event \(M\) of Task 1. If you start in a new R session, load the file and define the two events again:

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
D <- sharks$deal_on_show == 1           # on-air deal
M <- sharks$equity_offered_pct <= 10    # modest offer: at most 10%

Lecture 4 defined independence of events in two equivalent ways: through conditioning and through the product form. R reports:

          P_D   P_D_given_M     P_D_and_M P_D_times_P_M 
    0.6120749     0.6529894     0.3865371     0.3623178
  1. Use the definition with conditioning to decide whether \(D\) and \(M\) are independent in these records.
  2. Use the product form to reach the same conclusion.
  3. Explain why “not independent” does not mean “a modest offer causes a deal.”
  4. Explain why the event \(M^c\) (an offer above 10%) and the event \(M\) are disjoint but not independent.
Check your answer
  1. The deal probability is about 0.612 overall and 0.653 among modest offers, so conditioning on \(M\) changes it.
  2. In the same way, the observed joint probability is about 0.387, while the product \(P(D)P(M)\) is about 0.362. So \(D\) and \(M\) are not independent in these records.
  3. This is an association in televised pitches that the producers selected. It does not show that a modest offer causes deals, because founders who offer less equity may differ in other ways, for example in the stage or the quality of their venture.
  4. \(M\) and \(M^c\) are disjoint because they cannot occur together. Observing one makes the other impossible, so disjoint events with positive probability are dependent.

Task 7: Read a correlation table from a paper

Research papers in management usually start their results with one table of means, standard deviations (SD), and correlations. The table below is an invented example in the layout of such a table. It does not come from a real study. It describes a survey of 240 employees with four variables:

  • creative self-efficacy: how much an employee believes in his or her own ability to be creative, rated from 1 to 7,
  • job tenure: the number of years in the current job,
  • supervisor support: how much support the employee gets from the supervisor, rated from 1 to 7,
  • creative performance: how creative the employee’s work is, rated from 1 to 7.

The columns “Mean” and “SD” describe each variable alone. The columns 1, 2, and 3 contain correlations. Each number is the correlation between the variable in that row and the variable with that number. For example, the 0.31 in row 3 and column 1 is the correlation between supervisor support and creative self-efficacy.

Variable Mean SD 1 2 3
1. Creative self-efficacy (1 to 7) 5.10 0.90
2. Job tenure (years) 6.40 4.20 0.08
3. Supervisor support (1 to 7) 4.80 1.10 0.31 0.05
4. Creative performance (1 to 7) 4.20 1.00 0.36 0.12 0.28
  1. Find the correlation between creative self-efficacy and creative performance, and interpret its sign and strength in one sentence.
  2. Which pair of variables has the weakest linear association?
  3. A manager concludes: “Raising supervisor support will raise creative performance.” Name one third variable, or one other reason, that could produce the correlation of 0.28 without support causing performance.
  4. State one thing this table cannot tell you about an individual employee.
Check your answer
  1. The correlation is 0.36, in row 4 and column 1: a moderate positive linear association. Employees with higher creative self-efficacy tended to be rated higher on creative performance in this sample.
  2. The weakest association is between job tenure and supervisor support, with a correlation of 0.05.
  3. Several other explanations fit the same number. Employees in creative roles may get more support and also perform more creatively. Or supervisors may give more support to employees who already perform well. A correlation table cannot separate these explanations.
  4. The table describes how the variables move together across 240 employees. It says nothing certain about any single employee. With a correlation of 0.36, many employees with high self-efficacy will still have average performance ratings.

Task 8: Read a joint PMF with three values per variable

Take the houseplant table of Task 3 again.

  1. Check that the table is a valid joint PMF.
  2. Calculate \(P(X\le2,\ Y=2)\), the probability that an order has at most two plants and two pots. Then calculate \(P(X\le2\text{ or }Y=2)\) with the addition rule.
  3. Find the conditional PMF of \(X\) given \(Y=0\), and check that it sums to 1.
  4. Calculate the conditional mean \(E[X\mid Y=0]\) and the mean \(E[X]\). Interpret the difference in one sentence.
  5. Check the product form of independence in the cell \((x,y)=(1,0)\). What do you conclude, and why is one cell enough here?
Check your answer
  1. All nine entries are at least 0. The three rows sum to 0.40, 0.30, and 0.30, so all entries together sum to 1.
  2. The event “at most two plants and two pots” covers the cells \((1,2)\) and \((2,2)\), so \(P(X\le2,\ Y=2)=0.05+0.05=0.10\). With \(P(X\le2)=0.40+0.30=0.70\) and \(P(Y=2)=0.20\), the addition rule gives \(P(X\le2\text{ or }Y=2)=0.70+0.20-0.10=0.80\). Check: only the cells \((3,0)\) and \((3,1)\) are outside this event, and \(1-0.05-0.15=0.80\).
  3. Divide the \(Y=0\) column by its sum, 0.40: \(p_{X\mid Y}(1\mid0)=0.25/0.40=0.625\), \(p_{X\mid Y}(2\mid0)=0.10/0.40=0.25\), and \(p_{X\mid Y}(3\mid0)=0.05/0.40=0.125\). The three values sum to 1.
  4. The two means are \[ \begin{aligned} E[X\mid Y=0]&=1(0.625)+2(0.25)+3(0.125)=0.625+0.50+0.375=1.5,\\ E[X]&=1(0.40)+2(0.30)+3(0.30)=0.40+0.60+0.90=1.9, \end{aligned} \] in plants. Orders without a pot contain 1.5 plants on average. That is 0.4 plants fewer than the average of all orders, about 21% fewer.
  5. The entry is \(p_{X,Y}(1,0)=0.25\). The product of its margins is \(p_X(1)\,p_Y(0)=0.40\times0.40=0.16\). The two numbers differ, so \(X\) and \(Y\) are not independent. Independence needs the product form in every cell, so one cell that does not match is enough to show dependence.

Task 9: Two independent variables

A founder makes soap and sells it in gift sets through two shops: a concept store in Tilburg and a museum shop in Groningen. \(X\) is the number of gift sets sold on a Saturday in Tilburg, and \(Y\) is the number sold on the same Saturday in Groningen.   1. For independent variables, every cell is the product of its two margins. For the cell \((1,1)\): \(p_X(1)\,p_Y(1)=0.5\times0.4=0.20\).

Task 10: The sum of two variables, with positive and negative covariance

A founder sells ice cream from a cart in the park. Her daily revenue from ice cream, \(X\), has a mean of 320 euros and a standard deviation of 50 euros. She wants to add a second product and compares two options. With each option, the daily revenue from the second product, \(Y\), has a mean of 180 euros and a standard deviation of 30 euros.

  • Lemonade sells well on the hot days on which ice cream also sells well: \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=600\).
  • Hot chocolate sells well on the cool days on which ice cream sells badly: \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=-600\).
  1. Calculate \(\operatorname{Var}(X)\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)\).
  2. Calculate the expected total revenue \(E[X+Y]\) for each option.
  3. Calculate the variance and the standard deviation of the total revenue with lemonade.
  4. Calculate the variance and the standard deviation of the total revenue with hot chocolate.
  5. Calculate the correlation of \(X\) and \(Y\) for each option.
  6. Which option gives the more stable total revenue? Explain in one or two sentences why the two options have the same expected total and a different spread.
Check your answer
  1. \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=50^2=2{,}500\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)=30^2=900\).
  2. \(E[X+Y]=320+180=500\) euros for both options. The rule for the expected value of a sum does not use the covariance.
  3. Lemonade: \(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=2{,}500+900+2\times600=4{,}600\), so \(\operatorname{SD}(X+Y)=\sqrt{4{,}600}\approx67.82\) euros.
  4. Hot chocolate: \(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=2{,}500+900+2\times(-600)=2{,}200\), so \(\operatorname{SD}(X+Y)=\sqrt{2{,}200}\approx46.90\) euros.
  5. Lemonade: \(\rho_{XY}=600/(50\times30)=0.4\). Hot chocolate: \(\rho_{XY}=-600/(50\times30)=-0.4\). Both are moderate linear associations, one positive and one negative.
  6. Hot chocolate gives the more stable total. Its standard deviation is about 31% smaller (47 against 68 euros), and the expected total is 500 euros in both cases. With a negative covariance, a weak day for one product tends to be a good day for the other, so the two revenues partly cancel each other out. With a positive covariance, good days and bad days come together, and the total varies more.

Task 11: A uniform model

A founder runs a studio that prints T-shirts for student associations. The time to print one order, \(X\), is equally likely to be anywhere between 20 and 60 minutes: \(X\sim U(20,60)\).

  1. A colleague sketches the density as a flat line at height 0.02 between 20 and 60 minutes, and zero elsewhere. Show that this is not a valid density, and give the correct height.
  2. Calculate \(P(30\le X\le45)\).
  3. Calculate \(P(X>50)\).
  4. Calculate \(P(10\le X\le30)\). Note that a part of this interval lies outside the possible values of \(X\).
  5. State \(P(X=40)\). Then explain why the height of the density is not a probability.
Check your answer
  1. A valid density is never negative and has a total area of 1. The sketch is never negative, but its area is \((60-20)\times0.02=0.8\), not 1. The correct height is \(1/(60-20)=0.025\) per minute. Then the area is \(40\times0.025=1\).
  2. A probability is an area: width times height. So \(P(30\le X\le45)=(45-30)\times0.025=0.375\).
  3. \(P(X>50)=(60-50)\times0.025=0.25\).
  4. The density is zero below 20 minutes, so only the part from 20 to 30 counts: \(P(10\le X\le30)=(30-20)\times0.025=0.25\). A calculation with the full width of 20 minutes gives 0.5, which is wrong.
  5. \(P(X=40)=0\). A single value has width zero, so the area above it is zero. The height 0.025 is a probability per minute. It becomes a probability only when you multiply it by a width in minutes.
