Tutorial 4: Hypothesis tests, errors, and power

By the end of Tutorial 4, you can

  • carry out a two-sided test for a mean and for a proportion in five steps, with a critical value and a rejection region
  • write the conclusion in the words of the question, also when \(H_0\) is not rejected
  • connect a test decision with the confidence interval and with the p-value
  • describe a Type I error and a Type II error in context, and say how the significance level and the sample size can change a decision
  • read a row of regression coefficients without claiming more than the row shows.

Tutorials are exam preparation sessions. Every task below follows the format of the final exam. You first try each task in pairs. You may use AI for this, with the four steps Attempt, Ask, Verify, Explain. Then the class discusses the solution together.

This tutorial covers Lecture 10: the five steps of a test, the link between a test and a confidence interval, the p-value, the two errors, and power. The tutorial also uses the confidence intervals of Lecture 9 again. All tests in the exam are two-sided.

Before class

Total preparation time: about 15 minutes.

Bring your after-class work from Lectures 9 and 10. If you need it, review Mathematics Refresher: intervals, degrees of freedom, and standardisation.

Use the formula sheet with all formulas up to and including Lecture 10.

Write from memory:

  1. the five steps of a test, in order
  2. the test statistic for a mean when \(s\) is used, and its distribution if \(H_0\) is true
  3. the test statistic for a proportion, and its distribution if \(H_0\) is true
  4. the rejection rule of a two-sided test at the significance level \(\alpha\)
  5. the definition of a two-sided p-value, and how it gives the same decision as the critical value
  6. the definitions of a Type I error and a Type II error.
Check your preparation
  1. Hypotheses, with the significance level. Test statistic, with its distribution if \(H_0\) is true and the condition. Critical value and rejection region. Observed value. Conclusion.
  2. \(T=(\bar X-\mu_0)/(S/\sqrt n)\). If \(H_0\) is true, \(T\) has the \(t_{n-1}\) distribution. The condition: the population is normal, or \(n\) is large.
  3. \(Z=(\widehat P-p_0)/\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}\). If \(H_0\) is true, \(Z\) is approximately \(N(0,1)\), provided \(np_0\ge10\) and \(n(1-p_0)\ge10\). The denominator is the standard deviation of \(\widehat P\) under \(H_0\).
  4. Reject \(H_0\) if \(|t_{obs}|\ge t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\), or if \(|z_{obs}|\ge z_{1-\alpha/2}\) for a proportion. In words: the observed value is at or below minus the critical value, or at or above the critical value.
  5. The probability of a test statistic at least as far from zero as the observed value, in either direction. You calculate this probability as if \(H_0\) were true: \(2\,P(T\ge|t_{obs}|)\). The p-value is at most \(\alpha\) exactly when the observed value lies in the rejection region.
  6. A Type I error is rejecting \(H_0\) when \(H_0\) is true. Its probability is at most \(\alpha\). A Type II error is not rejecting \(H_0\) when \(H_1\) is true.

Try every task before you open its answer. Every conclusion must name the parameter, the units, the population, and the direction of the difference. It must also name the limits of the evidence. Write every test in the five steps and every confidence interval in the five parts, numbered 1 to 5.

Task 1: A test for a mean and its confidence interval (20 minutes)

Shark Tank data An advisor says that pitches offer 14.5% equity on average. Let \(\mu\) be the mean equity offered (equity_offered_pct) among all 1,441 recorded pitches. The sample of 100 pitches has the mean \(\bar x=12.76\)% and the standard deviation \(s=7.71\) percentage points. The degrees of freedom are 99. Use the closest values on the formula sheet: \(t_{0.975,\,100}=1.984\) and \(t_{0.995,\,100}=2.626\).

  1. Test \(H_0:\mu=14.5\) against \(H_1:\mu\ne14.5\) at the 5% level. Write the five steps.
  2. Repeat steps 1, 3, and 5 of the test at the 1% level. State the decision and say whether it changes.
  3. Construct the 95% confidence interval for \(\mu\) in five parts.
  4. Explain how the interval of part 3 shows the decision of part 1. Also explain what the interval tells you in addition to the decision.
  5. R reports a two-sided p-value of 0.026 for this test. Show that the p-value gives the same decisions at the 5% level and at the 1% level.
  6. The equity offered is strongly right-skewed. Explain why the test may still use a t critical value with 99 degrees of freedom.
Check your answer

Part 1, the test in five steps:

  1. Hypotheses. \(H_0:\mu=14.5\) against \(H_1:\mu\ne14.5\). Here \(\mu\) is the mean equity offered among all 1,441 recorded pitches, in percent. The significance level is \(\alpha=0.05\).
  2. Test statistic. \(T=(\bar X-14.5)/(S/\sqrt n)\). If \(H_0\) is true, \(T\) has the t distribution with \(n-1=99\) degrees of freedom. The population is not normal, but \(n=100\) is large.
  3. Critical value and rejection region. The critical value is \(t_{0.975,\,99}\approx1.984\). Reject \(H_0\) if \(t_{obs}\le-1.984\) or \(t_{obs}\ge1.984\).
  4. Observed value. The standard error is \(s/\sqrt n=7.71/\sqrt{100} =0.771\). Then \(t_{obs}=(12.76-14.5)/0.771=-1.74/0.771=-2.26\).
  5. Conclusion. The observed value \(-2.26\) is below \(-1.984\), so it lies in the rejection region. Reject \(H_0\). At the 5% level we can conclude that the mean equity offered among the recorded pitches differs from 14.5%. The sign of \(t_{obs}\) is negative, so the mean lies below 14.5%.

Part 2, the test at the 1% level:

Step 1. Hypotheses. As before, with \(\alpha=0.01\).

Step 3. Critical value and rejection region. The critical value is \(t_{0.995,\,99}\approx2.626\). Reject \(H_0\) if \(t_{obs}\le-2.626\) or \(t_{obs}\ge2.626\).

Step 5. Conclusion. The observed value \(-2.26\) lies between \(-2.626\) and 2.626, so it does not lie in the rejection region. Do not reject \(H_0\). At the 1% level we cannot conclude that the mean equity offered differs from 14.5%.

The decision changes with \(\alpha\). The stricter 1% level needs stronger evidence, and the same sample does not give it.

Part 3, the interval in five parts:

  1. Formula. \(\bar X\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\,S/\sqrt n\). It may be used because \(n=100\) is large.
  2. Given values. \(\bar x=12.76\)%, \(s=7.71\) percentage points, \(n=100\), \(df=99\). The confidence level is 95%, so the critical value is \(t_{0.975,\,99}\approx1.984\).
  3. Calculations. The standard error is \(7.71/\sqrt{100}=0.771\). The margin of error is \(1.984\times0.771=1.53\) percentage points.
  4. Interval. \((12.76-1.53,\ 12.76+1.53)=(11.23,\ 14.29)\) percent.
  5. Conclusion. The value 14.5 lies outside the interval. At the 5% level we can conclude that the mean equity offered differs from 14.5%, and the whole interval lies below 14.5%.

Part 4. The test and the interval use the same critical value and the same standard error. So the test rejects \(H_0:\mu=14.5\) at the 5% level exactly when 14.5 lies outside the 95% interval. The interval also shows the size of the difference: the mean lies between 0.21 and 3.27 percentage points below the 14.5% of the advisor. The lower end, 0.21 points, is about 1.5% of 14.5, so the difference may be small.

Part 5. The p-value 0.026 is below 0.05, so we reject \(H_0\) at the 5% level. The p-value is above 0.01, so we do not reject \(H_0\) at the 1% level. The critical values give the same decisions. The p-value is at most \(\alpha\) exactly when the observed value lies in the rejection region.

Part 6. The test uses a t value because \(\sigma\) is unknown and \(s\) is used in its place. The sample is large, so by the CLT the test statistic is approximately normal, although single offers are skewed. With 99 degrees of freedom the t distribution is almost the same as the standard normal: the critical value is 1.984 against 1.960. The rule is: with \(s\), always use the t distribution with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom.

One limit: the conclusion is about the recorded pitches in the file, not about all entrepreneurs.

Task 2: A test for a proportion and its confidence interval (15 minutes)

Shark Tank data A commentator claims that three quarters of all pitches end with a deal. Let \(p\) be the proportion of pitches with a deal (deal_on_show) among all 1,441 recorded pitches. In the sample of 100 pitches, 64 ended with a deal.

  1. Test \(H_0:p=0.75\) against \(H_1:p\ne0.75\) at the 5% level. Write the five steps, with the check of the condition.
  2. Construct the 95% confidence interval for \(p\) in five parts, with the check of the condition.
  3. The test divides by 0.0433. The interval uses 0.048. Name these two quantities and explain why their values differ.
  4. Does the interval lead to the same conclusion about 0.75 as the test? Is this always so for a proportion?
Check your answer

Part 1, the test in five steps:

  1. Hypotheses. \(H_0:p=0.75\) against \(H_1:p\ne0.75\). Here \(p\) is the proportion of pitches with a deal among all 1,441 recorded pitches. The significance level is \(\alpha=0.05\).
  2. Test statistic. \(Z=(\widehat P-p_0)/\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}\). If \(H_0\) is true, \(Z\) is approximately \(N(0,1)\). The condition holds: \(np_0=100\times0.75=75\) and \(n(1-p_0)=100\times0.25=25\) are both at least 10.
  3. Critical value and rejection region. The critical value is \(z_{0.975}=1.960\). Reject \(H_0\) if \(z_{obs}\le-1.960\) or \(z_{obs}\ge1.960\).
  4. Observed value. The denominator is the standard deviation of \(\widehat P\) under \(H_0\): \(\sqrt{0.75\times0.25/100}=0.0433\). The estimate is \(\hat p=64/100=0.64\). Then \(z_{obs}=(0.64-0.75)/0.0433=-2.54\).
  5. Conclusion. The observed value \(-2.54\) is below \(-1.960\), so it lies in the rejection region. Reject \(H_0\). At the 5% level we can conclude that the proportion of deals among the recorded pitches differs from 75%. The sign of \(z_{obs}\) is negative, so the proportion lies below 75%.

Part 2, the interval in five parts:

  1. Formula. \(\widehat P\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat P(1-\widehat P)/n}\). The condition holds: \(n\hat p=64\) and \(n(1-\hat p)=36\) are both at least 10.
  2. Given values. 64 deals, \(n=100\), \(\hat p=0.64\). The confidence level is 95%, so \(z_{0.975}=1.960\).
  3. Calculations. The standard error is \(\sqrt{0.64\times0.36/100}=0.048\). The margin of error is \(1.960\times0.048=0.094\).
  4. Interval. \((0.64-0.094,\ 0.64+0.094)=(0.546,\ 0.734)\), that is, from 54.6% to 73.4% of the pitches.
  5. Conclusion. The value 0.75 lies outside the interval. At the 5% level we can conclude that the proportion of deals differs from 75%, and the whole interval lies below 75%.

Part 3. The value 0.0433 is the standard deviation of \(\widehat P\) under \(H_0\), \(\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}\). The test asks how surprising the sample would be if \(H_0\) were true. So the test takes \(p=p_0=0.75\) as given and does not estimate anything. The value 0.048 is the standard error, \(\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\). The interval has no claimed value of \(p\), so it uses the estimate \(\hat p=0.64\).

Part 4. Here the interval and the test agree: 0.75 lies outside the interval, and the test rejects \(H_0\). For a proportion this agreement is not certain, because the two denominators differ. When the observed value is very close to the critical value, the test and the interval can give different answers. Use the procedure that the question names.

R reports a p-value of about 0.011 for this test. This p-value is below 0.05, so it gives the same decision.

Task 3: Read a regression coefficient row (8 minutes)

Suppose that you regress the equity offered (in percentage points) on the amount requested. The regression output shows this row for the amount requested. The sample is large, so the critical value at the 5% level is about 1.96.

Predictor Estimate Std. Error t value p-value 95% interval
Amount requested, per $100,000 -0.290 0.058 -5.00 <0.001 (-0.404, -0.176)
  1. State the tested hypotheses.
  2. Explain every number in the row, with its units.
  3. Give the statistical conclusion at the 5% level.
  4. State two claims that the row cannot justify.
Check your answer

Write \(\beta_1\) for the population coefficient of the amount requested. The test is \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) against \(H_1:\beta_1\ne0\). The estimate \(\hat\beta_1=-0.290\) says that each extra $100,000 requested goes together with 0.290 percentage points less equity offered. The standard error, 0.058, measures the sampling uncertainty of the estimate in the same units. The observed value of the test statistic is \(t_{obs}=-0.290/0.058=-5.00\): the estimate lies five standard errors below zero. The interval says that plausible values of the coefficient run from \(-0.404\) to \(-0.176\) percentage points per $100,000.

The observed value \(-5.00\) is far below \(-1.96\), so it lies in the rejection region. Reject \(H_0\) at the 5% level: the coefficient differs from zero, and the negative sign shows that it lies below zero. The p-value below 0.001 and the 95% interval, which does not contain zero, show the same decision. The row alone does not show that asking for more money causes lower equity offers, and it does not show that the conditions of the model hold.

Task 4: Errors, power, and what changes a decision (15 minutes)

Parts 1 to 5 are about the test of Task 3:

  1. describe a Type I error
  2. describe a Type II error
  3. state how a larger sample affects the standard error and the power
  4. explain why raising \(\alpha\) has a cost as well as a benefit
  5. state which of the two errors is possible after the decision in Task 3.

Parts 6 to 9 use an invented example. A founder runs a barbershop and plans 40 minutes for one haircut. She draws a random sample of 36 haircuts from the booking system of last month. The sample has a mean of 44 minutes and a standard deviation of \(s=12\) minutes. Haircut times are roughly normally distributed. She tests \(H_0:\mu=40\) against \(H_1:\mu\ne40\), where \(\mu\) is the mean time of all haircuts of last month. Supplied values: \(t_{0.95,\,35}=1.690\) and \(t_{0.995,\,35}=2.724\).

  1. Calculate the observed value \(t_{obs}\).
  2. Decide at the 10% level and at the 1% level. State the critical value and the rejection region each time.
  3. Suppose that the same mean and the same standard deviation come from a sample of 144 haircuts. Calculate \(t_{obs}\) again, and decide at the 1% level. The degrees of freedom are 143. Use the closest value on the formula sheet: \(t_{0.995,\,120}=2.617\).
  4. Explain how \(\alpha\) and \(n\) changed the decision. State which error is possible after each of the three decisions.
Check your answer

Parts 1 to 5. A Type I error is concluding that the population coefficient of the amount requested differs from zero when it equals zero. A Type II error is not rejecting \(H_0\) when the population coefficient is in fact not zero. A larger independent sample usually reduces the standard error and raises the power. Raising \(\alpha\) also raises the power, but it raises the probability of a Type I error at the same time. A wider rejection region leads to more rejections, both when \(H_1\) is true and when \(H_0\) is true. In Task 3 we rejected the null hypothesis, so only a Type I error is possible. A Type II error is possible only after a decision not to reject.

Part 6. Step 4. The standard error is \(s/\sqrt n=12/\sqrt{36}=2\) minutes. Then \(t_{obs}=(44-40)/2=2.00\).

Part 7. At the 10% level: Step 3. Each tail holds 5%, so the critical value is \(t_{0.95,\,35}=1.690\). Reject \(H_0\) if \(t_{obs}\le-1.690\) or \(t_{obs}\ge1.690\). Step 5. The observed value 2.00 lies in the rejection region. Reject \(H_0\): at the 10% level we can conclude that the mean haircut time differs from 40 minutes. The positive sign shows that the mean lies above 40 minutes. At the 1% level: Step 3. The critical value is \(t_{0.995,\,35}=2.724\). Reject \(H_0\) if \(t_{obs}\le-2.724\) or \(t_{obs}\ge2.724\). Step 5. The observed value 2.00 does not lie in this rejection region. Do not reject \(H_0\): at the 1% level we cannot conclude that the mean haircut time differs from 40 minutes.

Part 8. Step 3. The critical value is \(t_{0.995,\,120}=2.617\), the closest listed value for \(df=143\). Reject \(H_0\) if \(t_{obs}\le-2.617\) or \(t_{obs}\ge2.617\). Step 4. With 144 haircuts the standard error is \(12/\sqrt{144}=1\) minute, so \(t_{obs}=(44-40)/1=4.00\). Step 5. The observed value 4.00 lies in the rejection region, so reject \(H_0\). At the 1% level we can conclude that the mean haircut time differs from 40 minutes. The positive sign shows that the mean lies above 40 minutes.

Part 9. A smaller \(\alpha\) moves the critical value away from zero, from 1.690 to 2.724. With the same data, we then no longer reject \(H_0\). This protects against a Type I error, but it makes a Type II error more likely. A larger sample makes the standard error smaller, from 2 minutes to 1 minute. The same difference of 4 minutes, 10% of the planned 40 minutes, then gives a larger observed value, 4.00 instead of 2.00. This means higher power. After the two rejections only a Type I error is possible. After the decision not to reject, with 36 haircuts at the 1% level, only a Type II error is possible.

Task 5 (optional): One-sided alternatives and the exam convention (8 minutes, extra practice for home)

Shark Tank data This task belongs to the optional box on one-sided alternatives in Lecture 10. It is not part of the exam.

Task 1 gave \(t_{obs}=-2.26\) with \(df=99\) and a two-sided p-value of about 0.026.

  1. Give the p-values for the one-sided alternatives \(H_1:\mu<14.5\) and \(H_1:\mu>14.5\). Assume that the researcher chose each alternative before looking at the data.
  2. A student first sees that the sample mean is below 14.5. Then the student wants to report the left-sided p-value “because the direction is now known”. Explain the error.
  3. State the convention of this course for tests and one reason for it.
Check your answer

Left-sided: \(P(T\le-2.26)\approx0.013\), half of the two-sided p-value. Right-sided: \(P(T\ge-2.26)\approx0.987\), one minus that half. Suppose a researcher chooses the tail after seeing the estimate. Then the 5% always lies on the side where the estimate happens to fall. The true Type I error rate is then about 10%, not 5%. A one-sided alternative must follow from the research question. The researcher must choose it before looking at the sample. The convention of this course: all tests are two-sided. In business questions a difference in either direction usually matters. Also, with this convention nobody can choose the tail after seeing the data.

Task 6: Repair the conclusion (6 minutes)

The following conclusion about the row of Task 3 contains two errors. Find them and write a better conclusion.

The coefficient is significant, so asking for more money causes founders to offer less equity, and there is less than a 0.1% chance that the null hypothesis is true.

Check your answer

The p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and a significant result does not show a cause. A better conclusion: “Each extra $100,000 requested goes together with about 0.29 percentage points less equity offered (95% interval from 0.18 to 0.40 points less). If the population coefficient were zero, an estimate this far from zero would occur in fewer than 0.1% of samples. Nobody assigned the requested amounts to the pitches at random, so this is an association, not a causal effect.”

Task 7: A test that does not reject (12 minutes)

An invented example. A supplier of snack vending machines states in its brochure that one machine sells 120 items per week on average. A founder is thinking about signing a contract with this supplier. She draws a random sample of 25 of the 800 machines of the supplier. Last week these 25 machines sold 114 items on average, with a standard deviation of \(s=20\) items. Weekly sales per machine are roughly normally distributed. Supplied value: \(t_{0.975,\,24}=2.064\).

  1. Test at the 5% level whether the mean weekly sales of all 800 machines differ from 120 items. Write the five steps.
  2. The founder writes: “The test shows that the brochure is right. The mean is 120 items.” Assess this conclusion and write a better one.
  3. Calculate the 95% confidence interval for the mean weekly sales. Parts 3 and 4 of the procedure are enough. Use the interval to support your answer to part 2.
  4. State which error is possible after the decision of part 1, and describe it in the context of the founder.
Check your answer

The test in five steps:

  1. Hypotheses. \(H_0:\mu=120\) against \(H_1:\mu\ne120\). Here \(\mu\) is the mean number of items that one machine sold last week, among all 800 machines of the supplier. The significance level is \(\alpha=0.05\).
  2. Test statistic. \(T=(\bar X-120)/(S/\sqrt n)\). If \(H_0\) is true, \(T\) has the t distribution with \(n-1=24\) degrees of freedom. The sample is small, so the condition is that the population is roughly normal.
  3. Critical value and rejection region. The critical value is \(t_{0.975,\,24}=2.064\). Reject \(H_0\) if \(t_{obs}\le-2.064\) or \(t_{obs}\ge2.064\).
  4. Observed value. The standard error is \(s/\sqrt n=20/\sqrt{25}=4\) items. Then \(t_{obs}=(114-120)/4=-1.50\).
  5. Conclusion. The observed value \(-1.50\) lies between \(-2.064\) and 2.064, so it does not lie in the rejection region. Do not reject \(H_0\). At the 5% level we cannot conclude that the mean weekly sales per machine differ from 120 items.

The conclusion of the founder claims too much. “Do not reject \(H_0\)” is not the same as “accept \(H_0\)”. The sample gave too little evidence against 120 items. The sample did not show that the mean is 120 items. A better conclusion: “The sample of 25 machines gives no evidence at the 5% level that the mean weekly sales differ from the 120 items of the brochure. But the sample is also in line with clearly lower values.”

The interval supports this. The margin of error is \(2.064\times4=8.26\) items, so the 95% interval is \((114-8.26,\ 114+8.26)=(105.74,\ 122.26)\) items. It contains 120, and it also contains 106. A mean of 106 items would be 14 items below the brochure, about 12% less. With this sample, you cannot tell which of these two values is closer to the truth.

After a decision not to reject, only a Type II error is possible. Here it would mean: the mean weekly sales of the 800 machines differ from 120 items, but the test did not detect this difference. With 25 machines the standard error is 4 items. A larger sample has a smaller standard error and more power.

Timed individual practice: 40 minutes

Solve this task alone and with a timer, under exam conditions: no notes, no AI, only the formula sheet. An invented example. A random sample of 81 customers of a subscription service gives a mean spending of 42 euros and a sample SD of 9 euros. Of the 81 customers, 55 renew their subscription.

  1. Let \(\mu\) be the population mean spending. Test \(H_0:\mu=40\) against \(H_1:\mu\ne40\) at the 5% level, in five steps. Supplied value: \(t_{0.975,\,80}=1.990\).
  2. Construct the 95% confidence interval for \(\mu\) in five parts. Say in part 5 how the interval fits the decision of part 1.
  3. Let \(p\) be the population renewal proportion. Test \(H_0:p=0.60\) against \(H_1:p\ne0.60\) at the 5% level, in five steps. Then calculate the 95% confidence interval for \(p\). For the interval, parts 3 and 4 of the procedure are enough.
  4. Supplied values: the two-sided p-value is 0.049 for the test of part 1 and 0.15 for the test of part 3. Show that the p-values give the same decisions as your critical values.
  5. State the Type I error for each test. State which error is possible after each of your two decisions.
  6. The sample mean lies 2 euros above 40 euros. Explain why the test result for the mean is still only just significant.
  7. Write a report of six sentences about the mean spending. Write one sentence on each of these topics: the estimate, the uncertainty, the decision, the practical importance, the population, and causality.
Check a model answer

Part 1, the test for the mean:

  1. Hypotheses. \(H_0:\mu=40\) against \(H_1:\mu\ne40\), where \(\mu\) is the mean spending of all customers, in euros. The significance level is \(\alpha=0.05\).
  2. Test statistic. \(T=(\bar X-40)/(S/\sqrt n)\). If \(H_0\) is true, \(T\) has the t distribution with \(n-1=80\) degrees of freedom. The sample of 81 customers is large.
  3. Critical value and rejection region. The critical value is \(t_{0.975,\,80}=1.990\). Reject \(H_0\) if \(t_{obs}\le-1.990\) or \(t_{obs}\ge1.990\).
  4. Observed value. The standard error is \(9/\sqrt{81}=1\) euro. Then \(t_{obs}=(42-40)/1=2.00\).
  5. Conclusion. The observed value 2.00 is above 1.990, so it lies in the rejection region, but only just. Reject \(H_0\). At the 5% level we can conclude that the mean spending differs from 40 euros, and the positive sign places it above 40 euros.

Part 2, the interval for the mean:

  1. Formula. \(\bar X\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\,S/\sqrt n\). It may be used because \(n=81\) is large.
  2. Given values. \(\bar x=42\) euros, \(s=9\) euros, \(n=81\), \(df=80\). The confidence level is 95%, so the critical value is \(t_{0.975,\,80}=1.990\).
  3. Calculations. The standard error is 1 euro. The margin of error is \(1.990\times1=1.99\) euros.
  4. Interval. \((42-1.99,\ 42+1.99)=(40.01,\ 43.99)\) euros.
  5. Conclusion. The value 40 euros lies just outside the interval. This fits the decision of part 1: at the 5% level the mean spending differs from 40 euros, and the interval lies above 40.

Part 3, the test for the proportion:

  1. Hypotheses. \(H_0:p=0.60\) against \(H_1:p\ne0.60\), where \(p\) is the proportion of all customers who renew. The significance level is \(\alpha=0.05\).
  2. Test statistic. \(Z=(\widehat P-p_0)/\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}\). If \(H_0\) is true, \(Z\) is approximately \(N(0,1)\). The condition holds: \(81\times0.60=48.6\) and \(81\times0.40=32.4\) are both at least 10.
  3. Critical value and rejection region. The critical value is \(z_{0.975}=1.960\). Reject \(H_0\) if \(z_{obs}\le-1.960\) or \(z_{obs}\ge1.960\).
  4. Observed value. The standard deviation of \(\widehat P\) under \(H_0\) is \(\sqrt{0.60\times0.40/81}=0.0544\). The estimate is \(\hat p=55/81=0.679\). Then \(z_{obs}=(0.679-0.60)/0.0544=1.45\).
  5. Conclusion. The observed value 1.45 lies between \(-1.960\) and 1.960, so it does not lie in the rejection region. Do not reject \(H_0\). At the 5% level we cannot conclude that the renewal proportion differs from 60%.

The interval for the proportion uses the standard error: \(\sqrt{0.679\times0.321/81}=0.0519\). The margin of error is \(1.960\times0.0519=0.102\), so the 95% interval is \((0.577,\ 0.781)\). It contains 0.60, in line with the decision of the test.

Part 4. The p-value 0.049 is below 0.05, so the test for the mean rejects \(H_0\). The p-value 0.15 is above 0.05, so the test for the proportion does not reject \(H_0\). These are the decisions of the critical values.

Part 5. A Type I error for the mean is to conclude that the mean spending differs from 40 euros when \(\mu=40\). A Type I error for the proportion is to conclude that the renewal proportion differs from 60% when \(p=0.60\). The test for the mean rejected \(H_0\), so only a Type I error is possible there. The test for the proportion did not reject \(H_0\), so only a Type II error is possible there.

Part 6. The standard error is 1 euro, so the sample mean lies exactly two standard errors above 40 euros. The critical value is 1.990. The decision depends on the difference divided by its standard error, not on the size of the difference alone.

Part 7. A model report: “The 81 sampled customers spend 42 euros on average, 2 euros above the benchmark of 40 euros. The 95% interval for the mean spending runs from 40.01 to 43.99 euros. The test rejects \(H_0:\mu=40\) at the 5% level, but only just: the observed value is 2.00 against the critical value 1.990. A difference of 2 euros is 5% of the benchmark, so its importance depends on the margin per customer. The result holds for the population from which we drew the customers at random. It does not show what causes the higher spending.”

Check your understanding (5 minutes)

Complete from memory:

  1. A 95% confidence interval is correctly described as …
  2. Reject \(H_0:\mu=\mu_0\) at the level \(\alpha\) exactly when the confidence interval with level \(1-\alpha\) …
  3. A Type I error is … Power is …
  4. “Do not reject \(H_0\)” is different from “accept \(H_0\)” because …
  5. The test for a proportion divides by … The interval for a proportion uses …
  6. A regression coefficient row tests …
Check your answers
  1. The result of a procedure that contains the fixed parameter in about 95% of random samples. It is never “a 95% chance that the parameter is inside”.
  2. Does not contain \(\mu_0\) (for a two-sided t test and a t interval from the same sample).
  3. Rejecting \(H_0\) when it is true. The probability of rejecting \(H_0\) when \(H_1\) is true.
  4. A sample may fail to reject \(H_0\) only because it is too small or too variable. A real difference from the value of \(H_0\) can still exist.
  5. The standard deviation of \(\widehat P\) under \(H_0\), \(\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}\). The standard error, \(\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\).
  6. \(H_0:\beta_j=0\) against \(H_1:\beta_j\ne0\).
