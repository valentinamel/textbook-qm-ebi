Lecture 7: Samples, parameters, and estimators

By the end of Lecture 7, you can

  • distinguish a population, sampling frame, sample, and target population;
  • explain simple random sampling with and without replacement, and apply the 10% working rule;
  • distinguish parameters, estimators, and estimates, with the capital and lower-case notation discipline;
  • explain why a sample proportion is a sample mean;
  • explain the \(n-1\) degrees of freedom in a sample variance;
  • describe how an estimate varies from sample to sample while the parameter sits still;
  • classify sampling methods and name the main threat of each.

Driving question

Checking every record, customer, or transaction is usually too expensive, so firms audit a sample and act on what it shows. That only works if a small sample can be trusted to speak for the whole.

We can only afford to inspect 100 of the 1,441 pitches. What can 100 randomly chosen records tell us about all of them — and what can they never tell us about entrepreneurs in general?

In class

The session opens with the usual four-minute entry quiz on the prep videos, then turns to the driving question. Your prep prediction — do two random samples give equal means? — stays on screen, unresolved, until the sampling activity below settles it with evidence.

Define the population before calculating

A population is the complete set of units about which a question is asked. A sample is the subset observed for analysis. A sampling frame is the list or mechanism from which the sample is selected. The target population is the broader group to which we hope to generalise.

These sets need not coincide. The teaching file is a near-census of recorded US Shark Tank pitches from completed Seasons 1–16, but it is not a random sample of all entrepreneurs, all funding pitches, or even all applicants to the show. Randomly sampling rows from this file supports inference to the 1,441-record population. It does not remove the show’s selection process.

Check: classify two scenarios

Scenario A. A food-delivery startup wants to know how satisfied “its customers” are. It emails a survey to the app users who opted in to marketing emails; 800 reply. Identify the target population, the frame, and the sample.

Scenario B. A consultant uses our 100 sampled pitches to advise “US entrepreneurs seeking investors.” What is the frame, and what is the target population being claimed?

Answers. In A, the target population is all customers; the frame is the opted-in email list (which already excludes many customers); the sample is the 800 who chose to reply — a voluntary-response subset of an incomplete frame. In B, the frame is the 1,441 recorded Shark Tank pitches; the claimed target population is all US entrepreneurs seeking investors. Random row sampling licenses conclusions about the frame, not about that far larger target group.

Simple random sampling

In a simple random sample of size \(n\), every set of \(n\) population units has the same chance of selection.

  • With replacement: a selected unit is returned before the next draw. Draws are independent and each draw has the same distribution.
  • Without replacement: a unit cannot be selected twice. Draws are dependent, although every draw has the same marginal distribution.

When a without-replacement sample is a small fraction of a large population, the dependence is weak. This course uses the 10% condition as a practical working rule: if \(n\le 0.10N\), the usual independent-sample standard-error formulas are a good approximation.

The running sample for this lecture and the next is drawn with a visible seed, so every reader gets the same 100 rows:

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
set.seed(123)
sample_rows <- sample(seq_len(nrow(sharks)), size = 100, replace = FALSE)
pitch_sample <- sharks[sample_rows, ]

nrow(pitch_sample)
[1] 100

Here \(100/1441\approx 0.069<0.10\), so the working rule is satisfied.

The live sampling activity: watching an estimator vary

In class this segment runs live. The whole class watches five different random samples being drawn, one after another, and tracks one number per sample: the mean equity offered. The target of the exercise is the population mean of the file,

mu_equity <- mean(sharks$equity_offered_pct)
mu_equity
[1] 13.20761

about 13.21 percentage points — knowable here only because we happen to hold the full file. In a real study you would hold only the sample, which is exactly why the behaviour of the sample mean matters.

Read the figure slowly, because Lecture 8 is built on it. Each green dot is one estimate: the realised mean of one realised sample. The gold dashed line is the parameter: one fixed, unmoving number. The dots scatter around the line — none hits it exactly, none is far from it, and no dot is “wrong.” The scatter is not a mistake in the procedure; it is the procedure. This resolves the prep prediction: two students drawing their own random samples will almost never get equal means. Any two of the five dots differ, and yours (in the after-class task below) will differ again.

In class the five-dot plot stays on screen through the break, with one question to chew on: if we drew a sixth sample, where would its mean land? You cannot say exactly — but by now you can say something about the range in which it would probably fall. Making that “something” precise is the entire subject of Lecture 8.

Parameters, estimators, and estimates

MIT explanation (review): 20.3 The Sample Mean and Some Terminology.

A parameter is a fixed numerical property of a population, usually unknown. An estimator is a rule based on random sample variables. Its value changes from sample to sample. An estimate is the number obtained from one realised sample.

For a random sample \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\):

\[\bar X=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i\]

is an estimator of the population mean \(\mu\). After observing values \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\),

\[\bar x=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i\]

is the realised estimate. Capital letters describe random quantities before sampling; lower-case letters describe observed values.

For a 0/1 variable, the sample proportion is a sample mean:

\[\widehat P=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i.\]

Its realised value is written \(\hat p\).

The usual sample variance and standard deviation are

\[S^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i-\bar X)^2, \qquad S=\sqrt{S^2}.\]

The denominator \(n-1\) makes \(S^2\) an unbiased estimator of population variance under independent random sampling.

The realised estimates for our seed-123 sample:

c(
  sample_mean_equity = mean(pitch_sample$equity_offered_pct),
  sample_sd_equity = sd(pitch_sample$equity_offered_pct),
  sample_deal_proportion = mean(pitch_sample$deal_on_show)
)
    sample_mean_equity       sample_sd_equity sample_deal_proportion 
             12.760000               7.707887               0.640000

For this realised sample, \(\bar x=12.76\) percentage points, \(s=7.71\) percentage points, and \(\hat p=0.64\).

Example 9.1 (Worked example: three objects for the deal proportion) Identify the parameter, the estimator, and the estimate for the proportion of deals, using our realised sample.

  • Parameter: \(p\), the proportion of the 1,441 records with an on-air deal — one fixed number. Because the file is a near-census of its frame, we can even look it up: \(882/1441\approx 0.612\). In a real study it would be unknown.
  • Estimator: \(\widehat P=\frac{1}{100}\sum_{i=1}^{100}X_i\), where \(X_i\) is the deal indicator of the \(i\)-th sampled record — a rule whose value is random because the sample is.
  • Estimate: \(\hat p=0.64\), the number the rule produced for the seed-123 sample. A different seed would produce a different \(\hat p\) while \(p\) stayed exactly where it was.

Why the sample variance has \(n-1\) degrees of freedom

Once \(\bar X\) has been calculated from the same sample, the deviations must satisfy

\[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i-\bar X)=0.\]

If \(n-1\) deviations are known, the final deviation is forced to make the sum zero. Only \(n-1\) deviations can vary freely: the sample variance has \(n-1\) degrees of freedom.

Example 9.2 (Worked example: three deviations, two degrees of freedom) Suppose a sample has \(n=3\) observations and the first two deviations from the sample mean are \(2\) and \(-5\).

Step 1. The deviations must sum to zero: \(2+(-5)+d_3=0\).

Step 2. Solve: \(d_3=3\). The third deviation cannot be chosen freely — it is forced.

Step 3. Count the freedom: three deviations, but only two carried any information once the sample mean was fixed. The sample variance for this sample has \(3-1=2\) degrees of freedom.

Using the fitted sample mean makes squared deviations around \(\bar X\) slightly too small on average as a measure of population spread. Dividing by \(n-1\), rather than \(n\), corrects that downward bias under the random-sample model. Degrees of freedom count remaining independent pieces of information; they are not a percentage or a probability.

Sampling method determines the evidence boundary

The number of rows does not identify the sampling design. Ask how units entered the data:

Method How units enter Main implication
Simple random sample selected by a known random mechanism supports design-based generalisation to the frame
Stratified random sample randomly selected within defined groups can guarantee representation of important groups
Convenience sample selected because they are easy to reach vulnerable to selection bias
Voluntary-response sample units choose whether to respond people with stronger views may be over-represented
Census of a frame every unit in the available frame is recorded no sampling error for that frame, but coverage error can remain

The Shark Tank teaching file is close to a census of its recorded-pitch frame. When we draw 100 rows for an inference exercise, the randomisation is created by our row-sampling procedure. It does not make the television programme’s selection of pitches random.

Try it: classify four recruitment stories

In class this is the pair task. For each story, name the sampling method from the table and state its main threat.

Story 1 (given, as a model). “A ministry lists all registered firms, groups them by province, and randomly selects firms within each province.” Method: stratified random sample. Main implication: representation of every province is guaranteed by design.

Story 2 (you). “A startup posts a poll on its own Instagram account asking followers to rate a prototype; 412 followers respond.”

Story 3 (you). “A student emails the survey to friends, family, and two student associations she belongs to.”

Story 4 (you). “A researcher numbers all 8,214 firms in the Chamber of Commerce register and selects 300 using a random number generator.”

Check your answer

Story 2: voluntary-response sample — followers already like the brand, and those with the strongest opinions are most likely to reply. Story 3: convenience sample — easy-to-reach contacts are systematically unlike the wider population, so selection bias is the main threat. Story 4: simple random sample from the register — it supports generalisation to the registered-firm frame, though firms missing from the register remain out of reach (coverage error).

Random selection reduces systematic over-representation within the sampling frame. It does not guarantee that one realised sample is perfectly balanced: by chance, a sample can contain unusually many large equity offers or unusually many deals. Larger samples reduce this random instability — the five-dot plot would tighten if each dot summarised 1,000 draws instead of 100.

What random selection can and cannot repair

Random sampling supports generalisation from the sample to the frame it was drawn from: our 100 rows speak, with quantifiable uncertainty, for the 1,441 recorded pitches. Random assignment — allocating units to treatments by chance — supports causal comparison. They answer different design questions and neither substitutes for the other. And neither repairs the show’s selection: the frame itself was assembled by producers choosing telegenic pitches, so no amount of careful random row-sampling turns conclusions about this file into conclusions about entrepreneurs in general. Finally, “unbiased” is a promise about the procedure across many samples, not about your one realised estimate: \(\bar x = 12.76\) is not \(\mu = 13.21\), and no single sample can tell you how far off it is.

Answering the driving question

One hundred randomly chosen records give estimates of the file’s parameters: the seed-123 sample estimates the mean equity offer at \(\bar x=12.76\) against the true \(\mu=13.21\), and the deal proportion at \(\hat p=0.64\) against \(p\approx 0.612\). Five repeated draws put the sample mean between about 12.0 and 13.8 while the parameter never moved — random samples inform us about the frame, with a random error whose size Lecture 8 will quantify. What they can never tell us: anything reliable about entrepreneurs in general, because the frame is a television programme’s curated selection, and our row-sampling randomness cannot undo that.

Next: We now speak carefully about samples: the parameter sits still while the estimator varies from sample to sample — you watched five sample means land in five different places. Lecture 8 studies the pattern behind those dots: the sampling distribution of the mean, the standard error that measures its spread, and the central limit theorem, which explains why averages turn bell-shaped even when the underlying data are wildly skewed. That pattern is what will let us attach a margin of error to any estimate.

Check your answers
  1. The target population is the group of substantive interest; the sampling frame is the set from which units can actually be selected.
  2. Selecting one unit changes which units remain available.
  3. An estimator is a random rule before data are observed; an estimate is its realised numerical value.
  4. A 0/1 indicator sums to the number of successes, so its mean is the success proportion.

Worked exam interpretation (5 minutes)

Question. A retailer’s loyalty programme has 52,000 members. An analyst draws 400 members at random; their mean January spend is €87.40. A slide then claims: “the average member spends €87.40 per month.” Identify the parameter, the estimator, and the estimate, and give two distinct objections to the claim.

Check a model answer

The parameter is the mean January spend across all 52,000 members (fixed, unknown). The estimator is the sample mean \(\bar X\) of 400 randomly drawn members’ spends. The estimate is \(\bar x=87.40\) euros. Objections: first, the estimate carries sampling error — another random 400 members would give a different mean, so €87.40 should be reported with its uncertainty (Lectures 8–9); second, the claim generalises a January figure to “per month,” which the data cannot support — the frame covers one month’s spending only.
