Lecture 4: Conditioning, Bayes’ rule, and independence

By the end of Lecture 4, you can

  • calculate conditional probabilities and say which group each one refers to,
  • rebuild an overall probability from the rates of separate groups,
  • update a probability after new evidence with Bayes’ rule,
  • check whether two events are independent and tell this apart from disjoint.

Driving question

New information changes our assessment of chances. If you know that a pitch comes from a late season, the chance of a deal changes. The difficulty is to read such a chance the right way round. “Deals among late-season pitches” and “late-season pitches among deals” are different numbers.

You will pitch in a late season. Which of these two sentences tells you how likely a deal is?

  • A. 68.8% of late-season pitches reached an on-air deal.
  • B. 57.3% of the pitches that reached a deal came from the late seasons.

Lecture material

Lecture 2 gave each event one probability, computed from all pitches in the file. In practice you usually know something extra about a case: the season of a pitch, the sector of a startup, or a signal from a screening tool. Such information should change your assessment of the chances. This lecture shows how to update a probability when new information arrives, and how to avoid reading the update the wrong way round, which is the trap in the driving question.

The lecture builds four tools on one counts table. Conditional probability gives the chance of an event within a group. The law of total probability rebuilds an overall chance from the chances within groups. Bayes’ rule turns a conditional probability around. Independence describes the case in which new information does not change the chance at all.

Start with a founder’s question

Shark Tank data A founder hears that most deals on the show come from the later seasons. The founder concludes that a pitch in a later season is likely to end in a deal. Is that the same statement? Here are two sentences about the 1,441 pitches in the Shark Tank file:

  • Sentence A. “68.8% of late-season pitches reached an on-air deal.”
  • Sentence B. “57.3% of the pitches that reached a deal came from the late seasons.”

Both sentences use the same 505 records: the late-season pitches that reached a deal. But they answer different questions and give different numbers. This lecture shows what each sentence claims. Later, the lecture uses the same idea in another situation: a venture fund that screens the pitches it receives with an AI tool.

  2. \(P(L)\), the probability that a pitch is from the late seasons.

Conditional probability

MIT explanation (review): 02.2 Conditional Probabilities.

Shark Tank data A probability describes how uncertain you are, given what you know. When you learn something new, some outcomes are no longer possible. If you know that a pitch comes from a late season, the early-season pitches can no longer be the one you are looking at. A good probability model drops these outcomes and spreads the full probability of 1 over the outcomes that are left. This updating is what items 4 and 5 above ask for: what is the probability of one event if you already know that another event happens?

You answer such a question in two steps. First, keep only the pitches that match what you know, for item 4 the 734 late-season pitches. This group is called the reference class. Second, take the share of the reference class in which the event happens. For item 4, 505 of the 734 late-season pitches reached a deal. Restricting attention to a reference class is called conditioning on it. Figure 4.1 shows both steps as areas.

Read Figure 4.1 from left to right. Panel 1 shows what you know before any extra information. The square stands for all 1,441 pitches, so its area is 1. The deals are split across both periods: 377 early deals and 505 late deals. Panel 2 shows what happens when you learn that the pitch is from a late season. The early column drops out, because it is no longer possible. What is left, the late column, is stretched to fill the whole square, so the probabilities add up to 1 again. The solid part now covers 68.8% of the square. That is the deal probability given a late season. Panel 3 conditions on the other event. It keeps only the 882 deals and stretches them to fill the square. The late part now covers 57.3%. Panels 2 and 3 start from the same 505 late-season deals but divide them by different groups, so they give different numbers.

The symbol for this is a vertical bar, read as “given”. Item 4 is written \(P(D\mid L)\), “the probability of \(D\) given \(L\)”. Item 5 is written \(P(L\mid D)\). The event after the bar is always the reference class.

Definition

Conditional probability. The probability that event \(A\) happens, given that event \(B\) happens. For events \(A\) and \(B\) with \(P(B)>0\),

\[P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}.\]

In words: keep only the outcomes in \(B\), then take the share of them that are also in \(A\).

The formula does the same two steps as the count. For item 4,

\[P(D\mid L)=\frac{P(D\cap L)}{P(L)} =\frac{505/1441}{734/1441}=\frac{505}{734}.\]

The reference class gives the denominator. Dividing by \(P(B)\) rescales the probabilities inside \(B\) so that they add up to 1 again. This is the stretching step from panel 1 to panel 2 of Figure 4.1.

For a fixed event \(B\), conditional probabilities follow all three rules from Lecture 2. So every rule that Lecture 2 derived from them also holds inside the reference class. The condition is that the event after the bar stays the same in the whole calculation. Two examples follow. The first is the complement rule, \(P(A^c\mid B)=1-P(A\mid B)\). Among late-season pitches, the share without a deal is

\[P(D^c\mid L)=1-P(D\mid L)=1-\frac{505}{734}=\frac{229}{734}\approx 0.312.\]

The second is the addition rule. For two events \(A\) and \(C\) and the same reference class \(B\),

\[P(A\cup C\mid B)=P(A\mid B)+P(C\mid B)-P(A\cap C\mid B).\]

Some calculations mix different reference classes, for example \(P(A\mid B)\) and \(P(A\mid B^c)\). For these you need the law of total probability, which a later section explains.

Example 4.1 (Worked example: what each of the two sentences means) Shark Tank data Sentence A asks about \(P(D\mid L)\). Conditioning on \(L\) means that you hide everything except the Late column:

Early Late (\(L\))
No deal · 229
Deal · 505
Total · 734

Within that one column, 505 of 734 records are deals:

\[P(D\mid L)=\frac{505}{734}\approx 0.688.\]

Sentence B asks about \(P(L\mid D)\). Conditioning on \(D\) means that you hide everything except the Deal row:

Early Late Total
No deal · · ·
Deal (\(D\)) 377 505 882

Within that one row, 505 of 882 records are late-season:

\[P(L\mid D)=\frac{505}{882}\approx 0.573.\]

The numerator is 505 in both calculations: the same cell of the table. The denominators differ, and so do the questions. Sentence A looks only at the 734 late-season pitches. Sentence B looks only at the 882 deal pitches.

To match a sentence with its number, look at the words after “of” or “among”. Sentence A, “68.8% of late-season pitches reached an on-air deal”, names the late-season pitches as the group, so it is \(P(D\mid L)=505/734\approx 68.8\%\). Sentence B, “57.3% of the pitches that reached a deal came from the late seasons”, names the deals as the group, so it is \(P(L\mid D)=505/882\approx 57.3\%\). A report that says “68.8% of deal pitches came from late seasons” has put the right number into the wrong sentence.

The same two numbers in R. In the code, D and L are lists of 1,441 TRUE/FALSE values, one for each pitch. Square brackets select entries: D[L] keeps the deal values of only those pitches where L is TRUE, so it is the list of 734 late-season pitches. This is the step that keeps only the reference class. mean() of TRUE/FALSE values is the share of TRUE values, just as the mean of a 0/1 variable is its share of 1s. So mean(D[L]) is the share of deals among late-season pitches. In the same way, L[D] keeps the 882 deal pitches and mean(L[D]) is the share of them from a late season. The first output counts the size of each reference class, with sum(), which counts the TRUE values. The second output gives the two shares:

R confirms both reference classes (734 late-season pitches and 882 deals) and both shares: 0.688 for Sentence A and 0.573 for Sentence B. For comparison, the deal rate among all pitches is \(882/1441\approx 0.612\). So late-season pitches have a deal rate about 7.6 percentage points higher than all pitches together.

The multiplication rule

MIT explanation (review): 02.6 The Multiplication Rule.

Shark Tank data Often you know a rate within a group and the size of the group, but not how often both things happen together. A bank, for example, may know that 30% of its loans go to small businesses and that 5% of those loans are not repaid. The share of all loans that are small-business loans and not repaid is then \(0.05\times 0.30=0.015\), or 1.5% of all loans. The multiplication rule makes this step general.

The probability of an intersection, \(P(A\cap B)\), is also called a joint probability: both events happen together. Multiply both sides of the definition of conditional probability by \(P(B)\). The result is the multiplication rule. It builds a joint probability from a conditional one:

\[P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)\,P(B).\]

The same holds with the roles of \(A\) and \(B\) exchanged, because \(A\cap B\) and \(B\cap A\) are the same event:

\[P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)\,P(B)=P(B\mid A)\,P(A).\]

This line explains why the two sentences share a numerator and have different denominators. Both products equal \(505/1441\), but each one uses a different reference class.

Try it: verify the joint probability two ways

Shark Tank data Use the counts table of the section “One table, five probabilities” to check the equation above for \(D\) (on-air deal) and \(L\) (late season).

Step 1 (given). The direct way: \(P(D\cap L)=505/1441\approx 0.350\).

Step 2 (given). The first product: \(P(D\mid L)\,P(L)=\dfrac{505}{734}\cdot\dfrac{734}{1441}\). The 734 cancels. Cancel it and confirm that \(505/1441\) is left.

Step 3 (you). Write the second product, \(P(L\mid D)\,P(D)\), as two fractions from the table. Show that it also equals \(505/1441\).

Step 4 (you). Confirm all three ways in R.

Check your answer

\(P(L\mid D)\,P(D)=\dfrac{505}{882}\cdot\dfrac{882}{1441}=\dfrac{505}{1441} \approx 0.350\). The 882 cancels, just as the 734 did.

The R code computes the joint probability in the three ways. mean(D & L) is the share of pitches that are both deals and late-season. The other two lines multiply a conditional share by the share of its reference class. All three outputs are 0.350: 35% of all pitches are late-season deals, whichever way you compute it.

The multiplication rule is the building block of the next two tools. The law of total probability adds up several joint probabilities, and Bayes’ rule divides one joint probability by a total.

The law of total probability

MIT explanation (review): 02.7 Total Probability Theorem.

Shark Tank data Data often come as rates for separate groups: the deal rate in each period of the show, the repayment rate in each segment of a loan portfolio, or the conversion rate in each sales channel. A decision may still need the overall rate. The law of total probability combines the group rates into one overall rate.

Lecture 2 introduced a partition: events that do not overlap and together cover every outcome. The early seasons and the late seasons are a partition of the pitches. You can rebuild an overall probability from the parts of a partition in three steps. Take the conditional probability of \(A\) within each part. Multiply it by the probability of that part, which gives a joint probability. Then add the joint probabilities.

Definition

Law of total probability. For a partition \(B_1,\ldots,B_k\) with every \(P(B_i)>0\),

\[P(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P(A\mid B_i)\,P(B_i).\]

In words: the overall probability of \(A\) is a weighted average of its probabilities within the groups. The weight of each group is the probability of that group.

With only two groups, \(B\) and its complement \(B^c\), the sum has two terms:

\[P(A)=P(A\mid B)\,P(B)+P(A\mid B^c)\,P(B^c).\]

Example 4.2 (Worked example: rebuilding P(D) from the deal rates of the two periods) Shark Tank data The early and late events \(L^c\) and \(L\) form a partition, so

\[ \begin{aligned} P(D)&=P(D\mid L^c)\,P(L^c)+P(D\mid L)\,P(L)\\ &=\frac{377}{707}\cdot\frac{707}{1441} +\frac{505}{734}\cdot\frac{734}{1441}\\ &=\frac{377+505}{1441}=\frac{882}{1441}\approx 0.612. \end{aligned} \]

Two kinds of number appear here. \(377/707\approx 0.533\) and \(505/734\approx 0.688\) are the deal rates within each period. \(707/1441\approx 0.491\) and \(734/1441\approx 0.509\) are the weights: the share of all pitches that falls in each period. The overall deal probability is a weighted average of the two rates. So it must lie between 0.533 and 0.688, and it is a little closer to the rate of the larger group.

In R, mean(D[!L]) is the deal rate among early pitches (!L means “not late”), and mean(!L) is the share of early pitches. The second line multiplies each rate by its weight and adds the two products:

D <- sharks$deal_on_show == 1
L <- sharks$season >= 9
c(
  direct       = mean(D),
  from_periods = mean(D[!L]) * mean(!L) + mean(D[L]) * mean(L)
)
      direct from_periods 
   0.6120749    0.6120749

Both outputs are 0.612. Rebuilding the overall deal probability from the two periods gives exactly the share of deals among all pitches, as the law promises.

Panel 1 of Figure 4.1 shows the same calculation as areas. The width of each column is the probability of that period, the weight: 0.491 for the early and 0.509 for the late seasons. The height of the solid part is the deal rate within that period: 0.533 and 0.688. So the area of each solid part is width times height, which is a joint probability. For the late seasons this is \(0.509\times 0.688\approx 0.350\), and for the early seasons \(0.491\times 0.533\approx 0.262\). The two solid parts do not overlap, so \(P(D)\) is their total area, \(0.262+0.350=0.612\). In the figure you can also see why the overall rate lies between the two group rates. Flattening the two solid parts to one common height that keeps their total area gives a height of 0.612, below the late part and above the early part. The law of total probability returns in the next section, where it gives the denominator of Bayes’ rule.

Bayes’ rule: count first, formula second

MIT explanation (review): 02.8 Bayes’ Rule.

Many business decisions start from a signal: a flag from a screening tool, a positive review, or a click on an ad. The quality of a signal is usually described the other way round, as the share of good cases that show the signal. The decision needs the share of good cases among those that show the signal. Bayes’ rule turns the one into the other.

Return to the prediction question from before class. It is about another situation than the Shark Tank show, with invented numbers. A venture fund receives many pitches from founders by email. Most of them never lead to an investment: only 10% end in a deal with the fund. To save time, the fund uses an AI tool that reads each pitch and flags (marks) the pitches that it considers likely to reach a deal. A tool that sorts cases into two groups in this way is called a classifier. Three numbers describe it:

  • the base rate: 10% of the pitches that the fund receives reach a deal,
  • the sensitivity: among actual deals, 80% get flagged, \(P(\text{flag}\mid\text{deal})=0.80\),
  • the false-positive rate: among non-deals, 20% still get flagged, \(P(\text{flag}\mid\text{no deal})=0.20\).

A pitch has just been flagged. How likely is a deal now: near 80%, 50%, or 30%? Count first, and use the formula afterwards.

Example 4.3 (Worked example: 1,000 imaginary pitches) Imagine 1,000 pitches and apply the three numbers to them one after the other, as in Figure 4.2.

  1. Split by the base rate. Of 1,000 pitches, 100 are deals and 900 are not.
  2. Flag the deals. Sensitivity 80%: of the 100 deals, 80 are flagged.
  3. Flag some non-deals too. False-positive rate 20%: of the 900 non-deals, 180 are flagged.
  4. Collect the flagged pitches. In total \(80+180=260\) pitches have a flag, and only 80 of them are deals:

\[P(\text{deal}\mid\text{flag})=\frac{80}{260}\approx 0.31.\]

The classifier finds 80% of deals, but fewer than a third of the flagged pitches are deals. The tree shows the reason. The 900 non-deals are a large group, so even a 20% false-positive rate produces 180 false flags. That is more than double the 80 true flags. A flag moves the deal probability from 10% to 31%. This is real evidence, but it is far from certainty.

Now the formula. The question turns a condition around. We describe the quality of the classifier with \(P(\text{flag}\mid\text{deal})\), but the person who makes the decision needs \(P(\text{deal}\mid\text{flag})\). The multiplication rule gives the numerator, and the law of total probability gives the denominator.

Definition

Bayes’ rule. A formula that turns a conditional probability around: from \(P(A\mid B)\) to \(P(B\mid A)\). For events \(A\) and \(B\) with positive probability,

\[P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid B)\,P(B)}{P(A)}.\]

When \(B_1,\ldots,B_k\) form a partition, you can write out the denominator \(P(A)\) with the law of total probability:

\[P(B_i\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid B_i)\,P(B_i)} {\sum_{j=1}^{k} P(A\mid B_j)\,P(B_j)}.\]

For the classifier, the partition is deal and no deal:

\[ \begin{aligned} P(\text{deal}\mid\text{flag}) &=\frac{0.80\times 0.10}{0.80\times 0.10+0.20\times 0.90}\\ &=\frac{0.08}{0.08+0.18}=\frac{0.08}{0.26}\approx 0.308. \end{aligned} \]

Every term is a branch of the tree, divided by 1,000:

Formula term Value Tree count
\(P(\text{flag}\mid\text{deal})\,P(\text{deal})\) \(0.08\) the 80 flagged deals
\(P(\text{flag}\mid\text{no deal})\,P(\text{no deal})\) \(0.18\) the 180 flagged non-deals
the denominator (total probability of a flag) \(0.26\) all 260 flagged pitches

The formula is a short way to write the tree. Drawing a tree and counting is a good way to solve a Bayes question. Use an imaginary total that is easy to calculate with, such as 1,000. The tree and the formula always give the same answer.

Bayes’ rule also works on the Shark Tank events. Starting from \(P(D\mid L)\), it gives \(P(L\mid D)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} P(L\mid D)&=\frac{P(D\mid L)\,P(L)}{P(D)}\\ &=\frac{(505/734)(734/1441)}{882/1441}=\frac{505}{882}\approx 0.573. \end{aligned} \]

The R check computes the same formula and compares it with the direct count from the table. The first line plugs \(P(D\mid L)\), \(P(L)\) and \(P(D)\) into Bayes’ rule. The second line takes the share of late-season pitches among the deals directly:

Both numbers are 0.573: Bayes’ rule turns the 68.8% of Sentence A into the 57.3% of Sentence B. With a counts table at hand you can always count directly. Bayes’ rule is needed when, as for the venture fund, you only know the rates in one direction and the base rate.

Independence of events

MIT explanation (review): 03.3 Independence of Two Events.

Shark Tank data Sometimes knowing that one event happened tells you nothing about another event. This case matters in practice. A signal that is unrelated to the outcome in this way is useless for screening, however often it appears. In finance, an investor who holds several startups hopes that their failures are unrelated, so that one failure does not make the others more likely. Conditional probability makes the idea precise.

Definition

Independent events. Events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent when learning that \(B\) happened does not change the probability of \(A\). For \(P(B)>0\),

\[P(A\mid B)=P(A).\]

The same condition in product form:

\[P(A\cap B)=P(A)\,P(B).\]

The product form follows when you multiply both sides of the first equation by \(P(B)\). It treats \(A\) and \(B\) in the same way, and it also works when an event has probability zero. Events that are not independent are called dependent.

Example 4.4 (Worked example: are D and L independent?) Check both forms with the numbers from the counts table.

With conditioning: \(P(D\mid L)=505/734\approx 0.688\), while \(P(D)=882/1441\approx 0.612\). Late-season pitches have a higher deal rate than all pitches together, so \(D\) and \(L\) are not independent.

With the product form: \(P(D\cap L)=505/1441\approx 0.350\), while \[P(D)\,P(L)=\frac{882}{1441}\cdot\frac{734}{1441}\approx 0.612\times0.509\approx 0.312.\] The joint probability is larger than the product. This says the same thing: deals and late seasons occur together more often than they would under independence.

The R output repeats both checks. The first pair compares \(P(D\mid L)=0.688\) with \(P(D)=0.612\): late-season pitches have a deal rate 7.6 percentage points higher, about an eighth of the overall rate. Disjoint is not the same as independent

The two words sound alike, but they describe very different situations.

Disjoint events Independent events
Meaning \(A\) and \(B\) never happen together Learning that \(B\) happened does not change the chance of \(A\)
Condition \(P(A\cap B)=0\) \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)\,P(B)\)
Example \(A\): the pitch is from an early season. \(B\): the same pitch is from a late season. \(A\): the first toss of a fair coin lands heads. \(B\): the second toss lands heads.

Take the early and late events \(L^c\) and \(L\). They are disjoint: no pitch is in both periods. Before you know anything, the probability of a late season is \(P(L)\approx 0.509\). Now you learn that the pitch is from an early season. Then it cannot be from a late season, so \(P(L\mid L^c)=0\). The new information has moved the probability from about 51% to 0%. That is the largest change possible, so the two events are as far from independent as events can be. The product form shows the same: \(P(L^c\cap L)=0\), while \(P(L^c)\,P(L)\approx 0.491\times 0.509\approx 0.25\).

The same reasoning holds for any two disjoint events with positive probability. Knowing that one happened makes the other impossible, so they can never be independent. For two coin tosses it is the other way round. Both can land heads, and the first toss tells you nothing about the second.

Independence closes the toolbox of this lecture. One more use of conditional probability is so common in business reports that it deserves its own section: comparing the success rates of groups. The next section shows that such a comparison is a set of conditional probabilities, and why shares and not counts must be compared.

Figure 4.3 shows three conditional proportions, each with its own denominator. The 63.6% bar means that 63.6% of the 385 pitches by all-women teams reached an on-air deal.

Why compare shares and not simply the numbers of deals? The counts table shows 449 deals for all-men teams, 245 for all-women teams and 183 for mixed teams. Mixed teams have the fewest deals, but only because they made the fewest pitches. Their share of deals is the highest. Dividing by the size of each group puts all three groups on the same scale: deals per 100 pitches of that group. On this scale, mixed teams reached a deal 8.2 percentage points more often than all-men teams, and all-women teams 5.6 percentage points more often. Relative to the all-men rate of 58.1%, these differences are about 14% and 10%.

What a conditional probability can and cannot say

  • Turning a condition around changes the number. Neither number shows a cause. 68.8% of late-season pitches got a deal. That does not mean that late seasons cause deals.
  • The team comparison uses the broad groups of presenters that the data source defined. It also uses only pitches that the producers selected.
  • The numbers for the AI tool of the venture fund are invented. If they were realistic for a real fund, the fund should conclude that about two out of three flagged pitches never lead to a deal. A real fund would need its own base rate and its own flag rates to draw this conclusion.

What you can do with it: before you act on a signal, look up the base rate, the share of all cases that have the outcome you are looking for. In the venture fund example only 10% of pitches reach a deal. The 900 non-deals then produce 180 false flags, more than the 80 true flags from the 100 deals. Whenever the base rate is low, many signals are false alarms. So treat a signal as a reason for a closer look, for example a short call with the founder, and not as a final decision.

Answering the driving question

The driving question asked which sentence tells you how likely a deal is when you pitch in a late season. The answer is Sentence A. It is \(P(D\mid L)=505/734\approx0.688\), with the late-season pitches as reference class: about 69% of them reached a deal. Sentence B is \(P(L\mid D)=505/882\approx0.573\), with the deals as reference class. It tells you how the deals are split over the seasons, not how likely a deal is for your pitch. Both sentences are correct and use the same 505 records, but they answer different questions. The typical error is to use the number of one sentence in the other.

As a founder who pitches in a late season, you might expect a chance of about 69% to leave with an on-air deal. That is 7.6 percentage points above the 61.2% for all seasons together. This expectation rests on two assumptions: that your pitch is like a typical late-season pitch in the file, and that the show keeps selecting and judging pitches as it did in Seasons 9 to 16. It does not say that pitching later causes a deal.

The venture fund example shows the same trap with a signal. A flag from the fund’s AI tool tells less than its 80% sensitivity suggests. With a 10% base rate and a 20% false-positive rate, only \(80/260\approx 31\%\) of flagged pitches reach a deal. Among flagged pitches the probability of a deal is 31% instead of 10%, and not more.

A founder’s decision (invented example)

Your venture sells boxes of prepared meals by subscription, and you give away free trial boxes. One in ten trial customers becomes a subscriber. You notice a signal: 80% of later subscribers open your follow-up email, and 20% of the others open it too. You could call trial customers to convince them. A phone call costs you 2 euros of your time, and a subscriber is worth 30 euros to you. Whom should you call: the customers who opened the email, the others, or everybody?

Take a student-run venture that sells boxes of prepared meals on campus. “I want \(P(\text{becomes a paying customer} \mid \text{clicked the ad})\), where the reference class is the students who clicked the ad this month. Before I trust a high number of clicks, I would need the base rate of buyers among all students who saw the ad. With a very low base rate, most students who click still never buy.”

Figure 4.6 shows why, with invented numbers. Suppose 2% of the students who see the ad become buyers. Half of the buyers click the ad, and 5% of the other students click it too. Of 1,000 students, 20 are buyers and 10 of them click. Of the 980 others, 49 click. So only \(10/59\approx 17\%\) of the students who click become buyers, and about 83% of the clicks come from students who never buy.
