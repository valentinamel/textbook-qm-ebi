Lecture 4: Conditioning, Bayes’ rule, and independence

By the end of Lecture 4, you can

  • calculate conditional probabilities and say which group each one refers to
  • rebuild an overall probability from the rates of separate groups
  • update a probability after new evidence with Bayes’ rule
  • check whether two events are independent and tell this apart from disjoint

Driving question

An investor who learns that a pitch is from a late season updates their expectations. A bank that sees a fraud warning on a payment does the same. Updating chances after new information is a core probability skill in business. It is also easy to turn the question around by mistake.

Late-season pitches reach deals more often. Two sentences about these pitches give the numbers 68.8% and 57.3%. What exactly does each sentence claim, and what does a positive flag from an AI tool that predicts deals really tell us?

Lecture material

This section starts from one counts table. On that table it builds four tools: conditional probability, the law of total probability, Bayes’ rule, and independence of events.

Start with a founder’s question

Shark Tank data A founder hears that most deals on the show come from the later seasons. The founder concludes that a pitch in a later season is likely to end in a deal. Is that the same statement? Here are two sentences about the 1,441 pitches in the Shark Tank file:

  • Sentence A. “68.8% of late-season pitches reached an on-air deal.”
  • Sentence B. “57.3% of the pitches that reached a deal came from the late seasons.”

Both sentences use the same 505 records: the late-season pitches that reached a deal. But they answer different questions and give different numbers. This lecture shows what each sentence claims. It also answers a harder question of the same kind: when an AI classifier flags a pitch as a likely deal, how much should that flag change what you believe?

Each of the five quantities below is a fraction of two counts. Before you read on, find in the table the count that goes on top and the count that goes underneath.

  1. \(P(D)\), the probability that a pitch reached a deal.
  2. \(P(L)\), the probability that a pitch is from the late seasons.
  3. \(P(D\cap L)\), the probability that a pitch is from the late seasons and reached a deal.
  4. Among late-season pitches, the share that reached a deal.
  5. Among pitches that reached a deal, the share that came from the late seasons.
Check your denominators
  1. \(P(D)\): 882 deal records out of all 1,441 records.
  2. \(P(L)\): 734 late-season records out of all 1,441 records.
  3. \(P(D\cap L)\): 505 late-season deal records out of all 1,441 records.
  4. Among late-season pitches, the share with a deal: 505 late-season deal records out of the 734 late-season records.
  5. Among deal pitches, the share from the late seasons: 505 late-season deal records out of the 882 deal records.

The first three all divide by 1,441, the number of all pitches. The last two divide by a part of the table. A share within a part of the table is called a conditional probability. The next section defines it.

Conditional probability

MIT explanation (review): 02.2 Conditional Probabilities.

Shark Tank data Items 4 and 5 above ask the same kind of question: what is the probability of one event if you already know that another event happens? You answer both in two steps. First, keep only the pitches in one group. This group is called the reference class. Second, take the share of them in which the event happens. For item 4 the reference class is the 734 late-season pitches, and 505 of them reached a deal. Looking only at a reference class is called conditioning on it.

The symbol for this is a vertical bar, read as “given”. Item 4 is written \(P(D\mid L)\), “the probability of \(D\) given \(L\)”. Item 5 is written \(P(L\mid D)\). The event after the bar is always the reference class.

Definition

Conditional probability. The probability that event \(A\) happens, given that event \(B\) happens. For events \(A\) and \(B\) with \(P(B)>0\),

\[P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}.\]

In words: keep only the outcomes in \(B\), then take the share of them that are also in \(A\).

The formula does the same two steps as the count. For item 4,

\[P(D\mid L)=\frac{P(D\cap L)}{P(L)} =\frac{505/1441}{734/1441}=\frac{505}{734}.\]

The reference class gives the denominator. Dividing by \(P(B)\) rescales the probabilities inside \(B\) so that they add up to 1 again.

For a fixed event \(B\), conditional probabilities follow all three rules from Lecture 2. So every rule that Lecture 2 derived from them also holds inside the reference class. The condition is that the event after the bar stays the same in the whole calculation. Two examples follow. The first is the complement rule, \(P(A^c\mid B)=1-P(A\mid B)\). Among late-season pitches, the share without a deal is

\[P(D^c\mid L)=1-P(D\mid L)=1-\frac{505}{734}=\frac{229}{734}\approx 0.312.\]

The second is the addition rule. For two events \(A\) and \(C\) and the same reference class \(B\),

\[P(A\cup C\mid B)=P(A\mid B)+P(C\mid B)-P(A\cap C\mid B).\]

Some calculations mix different reference classes, for example \(P(A\mid B)\) and \(P(A\mid B^c)\). For these you need the law of total probability, which a later section explains.

Example 4.1 (Worked example: what each of the two sentences means) Shark Tank data Sentence A asks about \(P(D\mid L)\). Conditioning on \(L\) means that you hide everything except the Late column:

Early Late (\(L\))
No deal · 229
Deal · 505
Total · 734

Within that one column, 505 of 734 records are deals:

\[P(D\mid L)=\frac{505}{734}\approx 0.688.\]

Sentence B asks about \(P(L\mid D)\). Conditioning on \(D\) means that you hide everything except the Deal row:

Early Late Total
No deal · · ·
Deal (\(D\)) 377 505 882

Within that one row, 505 of 882 records are late-season:

\[P(L\mid D)=\frac{505}{882}\approx 0.573.\]

The numerator is 505 in both calculations: the same cell of the table. The denominators differ, and so do the questions. Sentence A looks only at the 734 late-season pitches. Sentence B looks only at the 882 deal pitches.

Check: which sentence matches which number?

Sentence A (“of late-season pitches, … reached a deal”) conditions on the season, so it is \(P(D\mid L)=505/734\approx 68.8\%\). Sentence B (“of deal pitches, … came from late seasons”) conditions on the deal, so it is \(P(L\mid D)=505/882\approx 57.3\%\). A report that quotes “68.8% of deal pitches came from late seasons” has put the right number into the wrong sentence.

The same two numbers in R. D[L] keeps only the late-season pitches, and L[D] keeps only the deal pitches. This is the step that keeps only the reference class:

The multiplication rule

MIT explanation (review): 02.6 The Multiplication Rule.

Shark Tank data The probability of an intersection, \(P(A\cap B)\), is also called a joint probability: both events happen together. Multiply both sides of the definition of conditional probability by \(P(B)\). The result is the multiplication rule. It builds a joint probability from a conditional one:

\[P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)\,P(B).\]

The same holds with the roles of \(A\) and \(B\) exchanged, because \(A\cap B\) and \(B\cap A\) are the same event:

\[P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)\,P(B)=P(B\mid A)\,P(A).\]

This line explains why the two sentences share a numerator and have different denominators. Both products equal \(505/1441\), but each one uses a different reference class.

Try it: verify the joint probability two ways

Step 1 (given). The direct way: \(P(D\cap L)=505/1441\approx 0.350\).

Step 2 (given). The first product: \(P(D\mid L)\,P(L)=\dfrac{505}{734}\cdot\dfrac{734}{1441}\). The 734 cancels. Cancel it and confirm that \(505/1441\) is left.

Step 3 (you). Write the second product, \(P(L\mid D)\,P(D)\), as two fractions from the table. Show that it also equals \(505/1441\).

The law of total probability

MIT explanation (review): 02.7 Total Probability Theorem.

Shark Tank data Lecture 2 introduced a partition: events that do not overlap and together cover every outcome. The early seasons and the late seasons are a partition of the pitches. You can rebuild an overall probability from the parts of a partition in three steps. Take the conditional probability of \(A\) within each part. Multiply it by the probability of that part, which gives a joint probability. Then add the joint probabilities.

Definition

Law of total probability. For a partition \(B_1,\ldots,B_k\) with every \(P(B_i)>0\),

\[P(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P(A\mid B_i)\,P(B_i).\]

In words: the overall probability of \(A\) is a weighted average of its probabilities within the groups. The weight of each group is the probability of that group.

With only two groups, \(B\) and its complement \(B^c\), the sum has two terms:

\[P(A)=P(A\mid B)\,P(B)+P(A\mid B^c)\,P(B^c).\]

Example 4.2 (Worked example: rebuilding P(D) from the deal rates of the two periods) Shark Tank data The early and late events \(L^c\) and \(L\) form a partition, so

\[P(D)=P(D\mid L^c)\,P(L^c)+P(D\mid L)\,P(L) =\frac{377}{707}\cdot\frac{707}{1441} +\frac{505}{734}\cdot\frac{734}{1441} =\frac{377+505}{1441}=\frac{882}{1441}\approx 0.612.\]

Two kinds of number appear here. \(377/707\approx 0.533\) and \(505/734\approx 0.688\) are the deal rates within each period. \(707/1441\approx 0.491\) and \(734/1441\approx 0.509\) are the weights: the share of all pitches that falls in each period. The overall deal probability is a weighted average of the two rates. So it must lie between 0.533 and 0.688, and it is a little closer to the rate of the larger group.

D <- sharks$deal_on_show == 1
L <- sharks$season >= 9
c(
  direct       = mean(D),
  from_periods = mean(D[!L]) * mean(!L) + mean(D[L]) * mean(L)
)
      direct from_periods 
   0.6120749    0.6120749

Bayes’ rule: count first, formula second

MIT explanation (review): 02.8 Bayes’ Rule.

Shark Tank data Return to the prediction question from before class. An AI classifier reads the transcripts of pitches (the written text of what was said) and flags (marks) the pitches that it considers likely to reach a deal. Three numbers describe an invented classifier of this kind:

  • the base rate: 10% of the pitches that it checks reach a deal.
  • the sensitivity: among actual deals, 80% get flagged, \(P(\text{flag}\mid\text{deal})=0.80\).
  • the false-positive rate: among non-deals, 20% still get flagged, \(P(\text{flag}\mid\text{no deal})=0.20\).

A pitch has just been flagged. How likely is a deal now: near 80%, 50%, or 30%? Count first, and use the formula afterwards.

Example 4.3 (Worked example: 1,000 imaginary pitches) Imagine 1,000 pitches and apply the three numbers to them one after the other, as in Figure 4.2.

  1. Split by the base rate. Of 1,000 pitches, 100 are deals and 900 are not.
  2. Flag the deals. Sensitivity 80%: of the 100 deals, 80 are flagged.
  3. Flag some non-deals too. False-positive rate 20%: of the 900 non-deals, 180 are flagged.
  4. Collect the flagged pitches. In total \(80+180=260\) pitches have a flag, and only 80 of them are deals:

\[P(\text{deal}\mid\text{flag})=\frac{80}{260}\approx 0.31.\]

The classifier finds 80% of deals, but fewer than a third of the flagged pitches are deals. The tree shows the reason. The 900 non-deals are a large group, so even a 20% false-positive rate produces 180 false flags. That is more than double the 80 true flags. A flag moves the deal probability from 10% to 31%. This is real evidence, but it is far from certainty.

Now the formula. The question turns a condition around. We describe the quality of the classifier with \(P(\text{flag}\mid\text{deal})\), but the person who makes the decision needs \(P(\text{deal}\mid\text{flag})\). The multiplication rule gives the numerator, and the law of total probability gives the denominator.

Definition

Bayes’ rule. A formula that turns a conditional probability around: from \(P(A\mid B)\) to \(P(B\mid A)\). For events \(A\) and \(B\) with positive probability,

\[P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid B)\,P(B)}{P(A)}.\]

When \(B_1,\ldots,B_k\) form a partition, you can write out the denominator \(P(A)\) with the law of total probability:

\[P(B_i\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid B_i)\,P(B_i)} {\sum_{j=1}^{k} P(A\mid B_j)\,P(B_j)}.\]

For the classifier, the partition is deal and no deal:

\[P(\text{deal}\mid\text{flag}) =\frac{0.80\times 0.10}{0.80\times 0.10+0.20\times 0.90} =\frac{0.08}{0.08+0.18}=\frac{0.08}{0.26}\approx 0.308.\]

Every term is a branch of the tree, divided by 1,000:

Formula term Value Tree count
\(P(\text{flag}\mid\text{deal})\,P(\text{deal})\) \(0.08\) the 80 flagged deals
\(P(\text{flag}\mid\text{no deal})\,P(\text{no deal})\) \(0.18\) the 180 flagged non-deals
the denominator (total probability of a flag) \(0.26\) all 260 flagged pitches

The formula is a short way to write the tree. Drawing a tree and counting is a good way to solve a Bayes question. Use an imaginary total that is easy to calculate with, such as 1,000. The tree and the formula always give the same answer.

Bayes’ rule also works on the Shark Tank events. Starting from \(P(D\mid L)\), it gives \(P(L\mid D)\):

\[P(L\mid D)=\frac{P(D\mid L)\,P(L)}{P(D)} =\frac{(505/734)(734/1441)}{882/1441}=\frac{505}{882}\approx 0.573.\]

The R check computes the same formula and compares it with the direct count from the table:

D <- sharks$deal_on_show == 1
L <- sharks$season >= 9
bayes_late_given_deal <- mean(D[L]) * mean(L) / mean(D)
c(
  from_bayes = bayes_late_given_deal,
  from_table = mean(L[D])
)
from_bayes from_table 
 0.5725624  0.5725624

Independence of events

MIT explanation (review): 03.3 Independence of Two Events.

Shark Tank data Sometimes knowing that one event happened tells you nothing about another event. Lecture 2 mentioned this idea. Conditional probability makes it precise.

Definition

Independent events. Events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent when learning that \(B\) happened does not change the probability of \(A\). For \(P(B)>0\),

\[P(A\mid B)=P(A).\]

The same condition in product form:

\[P(A\cap B)=P(A)\,P(B).\]

The product form follows when you multiply both sides of the first equation by \(P(B)\). It treats \(A\) and \(B\) in the same way, and it also works when an event has probability zero. Events that are not independent are called dependent.

Example 4.4 (Worked example: are D and L independent?) Check both forms with the numbers from the counts table.

With conditioning: \(P(D\mid L)=505/734\approx 0.688\), while \(P(D)=882/1441\approx 0.612\). Learning that a pitch is late-season raises the deal probability, so \(D\) and \(L\) are not independent.

With the product form: \(P(D\cap L)=505/1441\approx 0.350\), while \[P(D)\,P(L)=\frac{882}{1441}\cdot\frac{734}{1441}\approx 0.612\times0.509\approx 0.312.\] The joint probability is larger than the product. This says the same thing: deals and late seasons occur together more often than they would under independence.

D <- sharks$deal_on_show == 1
L <- sharks$season >= 9
c(
  P_D_given_L  = mean(D[L]),
  P_D          = mean(D),
  P_D_and_L    = mean(D & L),
  P_D_times_P_L = mean(D) * mean(L)
)
  P_D_given_L           P_D     P_D_and_L P_D_times_P_L 
    0.6880109     0.6120749     0.3504511     0.3117717

Disjoint is not independent. Take the early and late events \(L^c\) and \(L\). They are disjoint: no pitch is in both periods, so \(P(L^c\cap L)=0\). But the product \(P(L^c)\,P(L)\approx 0.491\times 0.509\approx 0.25\) is not zero, so the product check fails. Disjoint events with positive probability are always dependent. If you observe one of them, you know that the other did not happen. So one event tells you a lot about the other.

Second application: deal rates by team composition

Shark Tank data The variable pitcher_gender_group in the Shark Tank file puts each team that pitches into one of four groups: all_men, all_women, mixed, or unknown. The deal rate within a category is a conditional probability, \(P(D\mid \text{category})\), with the category as reference class. The code computes the three rates for the pitches with a known category:

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
D <- sharks$deal_on_show == 1
known_gender <- sharks$pitcher_gender_group != "unknown"
gender_labels <- c("all_men", "all_women", "mixed")

gender_deal_rates <- tapply(
  sharks$deal_on_show[known_gender],
  factor(sharks$pitcher_gender_group[known_gender], levels = gender_labels),

Figure 4.3 shows three conditional proportions, each with its own denominator. The 63.6% bar means that 63.6% of the 385 pitches by all-women teams reached an on-air deal. The three bars use three different reference classes. They can still be compared, because each rate is a share within its own group.

What a conditional probability can and cannot say

  • Turning a condition around changes the number. Neither number shows a cause. 68.8% of late-season pitches got a deal. That does not mean that late seasons cause deals.
  • The team comparison uses the broad groups of presenters that the data source defined. It also uses only pitches that the producers selected.
  • The classifier numbers are invented for the example.

What you can do with it: before you act on a signal, ask how often the outcome that you look for happens. With a low base rate, most signals are false alarms, so a cheap second check is worth its cost.

Answering the driving question

The two sentences make different claims about the same 505 records. “68.8% of late-season pitches reached a deal” is \(P(D\mid L)=505/734\), with the late seasons as reference class. “57.3% of deal pitches came from late seasons” is \(P(L\mid D)=505/882\), with the deals as reference class. Both are correct. The typical error is to use the number of one sentence in the other. A positive flag from the AI classifier tells us less than its 80% sensitivity suggests. With a 10% base rate and a 20% false-positive rate, only \(80/260\approx 31\%\) of flagged pitches are deals. The flag raises the probability of a deal from 10% to 31%, and not more.

A founder’s decision (invented example)

Your venture sells boxes of prepared meals by subscription, and you give away free trial boxes. One in ten trial customers becomes a subscriber. You notice a signal: 80% of later subscribers open your follow-up email, and 20% of the others open it too. Count with 1,000 trial customers. 100 become subscribers, and 80 of them open the email. Of the other 900, 180 open it. So 80 of 260 openers become subscribers, about 31%. A phone call costs you 2 euros of time, and a subscriber is worth 30 euros. A call to an opener is worth 0.31 × 30 = 9.30 euros on average, so you call the openers. Among the 740 who did not open, only 20 become subscribers, under 3%. A call to one of them is worth about 0.80 euros on average. The decision: call the openers and nobody else.

Recall questions

  1. What is the denominator of \(P(A\mid B)\)?
  2. Why is \(P(A\mid B)\) generally different from \(P(B\mid A)\)?
  3. What makes a collection of events a partition?
  4. What does the denominator in Bayes’ rule calculate?
  5. State the two equivalent definitions of independence of events.
  6. Can two disjoint events, each with positive probability, be independent?
Check your answers
  1. \(P(B)\), the probability of the conditioning event.
  2. They condition on different reference classes, so they have different denominators.
  3. No two parts overlap, and together they make up \(\Omega\) (their union is \(\Omega\)).
  4. The total probability of the observed evidence, added across all parts of the partition.
  5. \(P(A\mid B)=P(A)\) (learning \(B\) does not change the probability of \(A\)), and the product form \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)\,P(B)\).
  6. No. Their intersection has probability 0 while the product of their probabilities is positive, so the product check fails. Observing one makes the other impossible.

Finish a half-worked example: the Bayes tree

Re-read the worked example with the tree of 1,000 pitches (Example 4.3). Then build the tree again for an AI classifier that flags pitches likely to get a deal, with new numbers. Of the checked pitches, 20% reach a deal (the base rate). The classifier flags 90% of the deals (the sensitivity) and 30% of the non-deals (the false-positive rate).

Step 1 (given). Start with 1,000 imaginary pitches: 200 deals, 800 non-deals.

Step 2 (you). Fill in the four boxes at the end of the tree: the flagged and the unflagged counts in each branch.

Step 3 (you). Collect the flagged pitches and compute \(P(\text{deal}\mid\text{flag})\).

Check your answer

Of the 200 deals, 90% are flagged: 180. Of the 800 non-deals, 30% are flagged: 240. Flagged total \(180+240=420\), of which 180 are deals: \(P(\text{deal}\mid\text{flag})=180/420\approx 0.429\). The formula agrees: \(\dfrac{0.90\times 0.20}{0.90\times 0.20+0.30\times 0.80} =\dfrac{0.18}{0.42}\approx 0.429\).

On your own. A more accurate classifier checks a group of pitches in which 5% reach a deal. Its sensitivity is 90% and its false-positive rate is 10%. What share of flagged pitches are deals? Use either the tree or the formula.

Check your answer

With 1,000 pitches: 50 deals, of which 45 are flagged, and 950 non-deals, of which 95 are flagged. \(P(\text{deal}\mid\text{flag})=45/(45+95)=45/140\approx 0.321\). Even with 90% sensitivity and a low false-positive rate, the 5% base rate keeps the share of true flags below one third.

Shark Tank data Select one of the 1,441 recorded pitches at random. \(D\) is the event of an on-air deal, and \(L\) is the event of a late season (Seasons 9–16). Use the counts table of this lecture. Without a deal: 330 early-season and 229 late-season pitches (559 in total). With a deal: 377 early-season and 505 late-season pitches (882 in total). The early seasons have 707 pitches, the late seasons 734, and all seasons 1,441.

  1. Use the early/late partition to verify \[P(D)=P(D\mid L^c)\,P(L^c)+P(D\mid L)\,P(L).\]
  2. Calculate and interpret \(P(L\mid D)\). Explain why it differs from \(P(D\mid L)\).
  3. Decide by calculation whether \(D\) and \(L\) are independent, using the product form.
  4. The events \(L^c\) and \(L\) are disjoint. Are they independent? Explain in one sentence.
  5. Consider the 1,434 pitches with a known classification of the team. Of the 661 pitches with women represented (all-women or mixed), 428 reached an on-air deal. Of the 773 all-men pitches, 449 reached an on-air deal. Calculate both conditional deal proportions and interpret the difference.
Check your answers
  1. \((377/707)(707/1441)+(505/734)(734/1441)=(377+505)/1441=882/1441 \approx 0.612\).
  2. \(P(L\mid D)=505/882\approx 0.573\): among deal records, 57.3% came from Seasons 9–16. It differs from \(P(D\mid L)=505/734\approx 0.688\) because the conditioning event, and so the denominator, is different.
  3. \(P(D\cap L)=505/1441\approx 0.350\), while \(P(D)\,P(L)=(882/1441)(734/1441)\approx 0.312\). The two are not equal, so \(D\) and \(L\) are not independent.
  4. No: \(P(L^c\cap L)=0\) but \(P(L^c)\,P(L)\approx 0.25>0\), so the product check fails. Disjoint events with positive probability are always dependent.
  5. \(428/661\approx 0.648\) and \(449/773\approx 0.581\), a descriptive difference of about 6.7 percentage points. The data source defined the groups, and the producers chose the pitches for television. The difference does not show that the kind of team causes the deal.

Review: one question from Lecture 3

A variable \(Y\) has PMF \(p_Y(0)=0.2\), \(p_Y(1)=0.5\), and \(p_Y(2)=0.3\). Calculate \(E[Y]\) and say in one sentence what the number means. Can \(Y\) ever equal it exactly?

Check your answer

\(E[Y]=0(0.2)+1(0.5)+2(0.3)=1.1\). It is the probability-weighted average of the possible values, and the long-run average over many repetitions. It does not have to be a value that the variable can take: here \(Y\) is never 1.1.

Worked exam interpretation

Shark Tank data Question. Select one recorded Shark Tank pitch at random. \(D\) is the event of an on-air deal, and \(L\) is the event of a late season (Seasons 9–16). A student sees \(P(D\mid L)=0.688\) and writes, “68.8% of deal pitches came from late seasons.” Explain what is wrong and give the correct interpretation.

Check a model answer

The calculation conditions on \(L\), so the denominator is the 734 pitches in Seasons 9–16. The correct interpretation is that 68.8% of late-season records reached an on-air deal. The sentence the student wrote describes the reversed conditional probability, \(P(L\mid D)=505/882\approx 57.3\%\). Neither quantity measures completed investment or a causal season effect.

Check the AI

Shark Tank data You asked an AI assistant: “If a pitch got a deal, how likely is it that it came from a late season?” It answered:

“68.8% of late-season pitches got a deal. So if a pitch got a deal, there is a 68.8% chance that it came from a late season.”

Find what is wrong and write the corrected answer. This is the Verify step of Attempt → Ask → Verify → Explain.

Check your answer

The AI turned the condition around. 68.8% is the share of deals among late-season pitches, 505 of 734. The question asks for the share of late-season pitches among deals: 505 of 882, about 57.3%. Always ask which group is in the denominator.
