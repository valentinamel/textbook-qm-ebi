Lecture 6: Continuous distributions and the normal model

By the end of Lecture 6, you can

  • distinguish a continuous random variable from a discrete one
  • explain why \(P(X=x)=0\) for a continuous random variable and interpret a probability density function as area rather than point probability
  • use a CDF to calculate interval probabilities
  • calculate uniform probabilities from interval lengths
  • standardise a normal value and compute normal probabilities by hand from supplied \(Z\) values and with pnorm()
  • convert a percentile back to a raw value with \(x=\mu+z\sigma\) and verify with qnorm()
  • find quantiles of model distributions (the uniform median, normal quartiles)
  • check a proposed normal model against a histogram of data and say when it fails.

Must know

Be able to do yourself:

  • calculate uniform probabilities from interval lengths
  • standardise a value and use the supplied \(Z\) values, with the complement rule and the symmetry rule where needed
  • go back from a percentile to a value with \(x=\mu+z\sigma\)
  • say from a histogram whether a normal model fits.

Enough to recognise:

  • the density notation \(f_X(x)\)
  • pnorm() and qnorm()
  • the central 95% of the standard normal distribution, between \(-1.96\) and \(1.96\).

Driving question

Not everything comes in whole numbers: equity shares, waiting times, and revenues can take any value in a range. Insurers, lenders, and operations planners price risk on such quantities every day, and that needs a new kind of probability model.

You plan to offer the sharks 30% of your company. How unusual is that, and can a model with two numbers answer the question without counting every pitch?

Lecture material

This section builds probability models for quantities that can take any value in a range. It uses them to answer one question about the equity offered in the Shark Tank file.

Start with a founder’s question

You plan to offer the sharks 30% of your company. Is that unusual? The Shark Tank file records the equity offered in each pitch (equity_offered_pct). The code below reads the file and counts the pitches that offered 30% or more.

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
nrow(sharks)
[1] 1441
sum(sharks$equity_offered_pct >= 30)
[1] 76
mean(sharks$equity_offered_pct >= 30)
[1] 0.05274115

In the file, 76 of the 1,441 pitches offered 30% or more, about 5%. Now suppose you did not have the file. You only knew two numbers about the offers: the mean and the standard deviation.

c(mean_equity = mean(sharks$equity_offered_pct),
  sd_equity   = sd(sharks$equity_offered_pct))
mean_equity   sd_equity 
  13.207613    8.444355

The mean is 13.2% and the standard deviation is 8.4 percentage points. Could you still say how unusual 30% is? An AI assistant that gets this question will often answer with a model called the normal distribution, and it reports about 2%. That is less than half of the share in the data.

This lecture shows how such models work for quantities that can take any value in a range, how to calculate with them by hand, and how to check whether a model fits the data at hand. The check comes near the end, and it explains the gap between 2% and 5%.

From counting values to measuring intervals

Equity offered is a continuous quantity: an offer \(X\) can be any percentage in a range, for example 29.87%. What is the probability that an offer is exactly 30%?

Take a window around 30. The offer lies between 29.5% and 30.5% with some probability \(q\). The window from 29.95% to 30.05% is ten times narrower. Over such a short range the probability is spread almost evenly, so this window holds about \(q/10\). Every further decimal divides the probability by about ten. The value “exactly 30%” lies inside every one of these windows, so its probability cannot be larger than any of theirs. Only zero is that small. For a continuous random variable,

\[P(X=x)=0 \quad\text{for every single value } x.\]

This answers the Wooclap question from the preparation: exactly zero, not very small. Probability has not disappeared. It belongs to intervals, not to single values.

From probability mass to probability density

MIT explanation (review): 08.2 Probability Density Functions.

For a discrete random variable, the PMF gives a probability to each single value. For a continuous variable that no longer works. Look at the two windows again. The probability fell from \(q\) to about \(q/10\), and the width fell from 1 to 0.1 percentage points. Their ratio hardly changed:

\[\frac{q}{1}\approx\frac{q/10}{0.1}.\]

For narrow windows, the probability divided by the width is approximately constant. This ratio is the probability per unit of width near 30%. It is called the density at 30%, written \(f_X(30)\). The density takes the place of the PMF.

Definition

Probability density function (PDF). A curve \(f_X(x)\) that is never negative and has a total area of 1 under it. The probability that \(X\) lies between \(a\) and \(b\) is the area under the curve between \(a\) and \(b\).

Three reading rules follow. The height of the curve is a rate, not a probability. The area over an interval is a probability. A rate can be high where the probability is packed into a narrow range, so a density can exceed 1, as long as the total area is 1. A metal rod gives the same picture: it has a weight per metre at each point, but no single point has weight.

The Shark Tank file records the equity offered in rounded percentages, so values repeat.

sum(sharks$equity_offered_pct == 30)
[1] 33

In the file, 33 of the 1,441 pitches show exactly 30. That comes from rounding. The model is continuous.

You do not need calculus in this course. You read probability as an area, and you calculate it with a CDF, with R, or with supplied values.

Optional: the formal definition of a density

A function \(f_X\) is a density of the continuous random variable \(X\) if it is never negative and has total area 1,

\[f_X(x)\ge 0 \ \text{ for every } x, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(x)\,dx=1,\]

and if, for all values \(a\le b\),

\[P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^b f_X(x)\,dx.\]

The integral is the area under the curve between \(a\) and \(b\). For \(a=b\) the area has zero width, which gives \(P(X=a)=0\) again.

The normal distribution, which comes later in this lecture, has the density

\[f_X(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).\]

Nobody needs to memorise this formula. R evaluates it with dnorm().

Check: is a rectangle of width 0.5 and height 2 a valid density?

Yes. The curve is never negative and its total area is \(0.5 \times 2 = 1\), so it is a valid PDF. The height 2 is not a probability. No probability here exceeds 1, because every probability is an area inside that rectangle. The height 2 means that the probability is packed into a narrow range.

The CDF works for every random variable

The cumulative distribution function (CDF) is still

\[F_X(x)=P(X\le x),\]

exactly as in Lecture 3. In a picture, \(F_X(x)\) is the area under the density to the left of \(x\). For a continuous random variable the CDF rises smoothly, without jumps, from 0 to 1. It never falls, because a move to the right can only add area. Figure 8.2 in the next section shows an example.

The probability of an interval is the difference of two CDF values:

\[P(a<X\le b)=F_X(b)-F_X(a).\]

Because a single point has zero probability, changing \(<\) to \(\le\) does not change a continuous probability: \(P(a<X<b)\), \(P(a\le X\le b)\), and the two mixed versions are all equal. This differs from a discrete variable, where a point may carry positive probability and the inequality type matters.

The uniform distribution

MIT explanation (review): 08.3 Uniform and Piecewise Constant PDFs.

The uniform distribution is the simplest continuous model. All possible values lie in an interval from \(a\) to \(b\), and all parts of this interval with the same length are equally likely. We write \(X\sim U(a,b)\). The numbers \(a\) and \(b\) are the parameters of the distribution. The interval \([a,b]\) is the set of possible values, also called the support. The density is constant on the support,

\[f_X(x)=\frac{1}{b-a},\qquad a\le x\le b,\]

and zero outside it. The height is not a free choice. The whole rectangle must have area 1:

\[\text{width}\times\text{height}=(b-a)\times\frac{1}{b-a}=1.\]

Because the height is constant, the probability of an interval depends only on its length. For \(a\le c<d\le b\),

\[P(c<X<d)=\frac{d-c}{b-a}.\]

The mean, the variance, and the standard deviation are stated here without derivation:

\[E[X]=\frac{a+b}{2},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{(b-a)^2}{12}, \qquad \operatorname{SD}(X)=\frac{b-a}{\sqrt{12}}.\]

Example 8.1 (Worked example: when does the pitch appear?) This is an invented example. A recording session lasts 90 minutes, and a pitch is equally likely to start at any moment of it. Model the start time as \(X\sim U(0,90)\), measured in minutes.

Step 1: the height. The density is \(1/(90-0)=1/90\approx 0.011\) per minute. The total area of 1 fixes this height.

Step 2: the event. “The pitch starts during the 18-minute window from minute 30 to minute 48” is the event \(30<X<48\).

Step 3: the area. The probability is the area of the shaded rectangle in Figure 8.1:

\[P(30<X<48)=\frac{48-30}{90-0}=\frac{18}{90}=0.20.\]

For a uniform model, only the length of the window matters: any 18-minute window inside the session has probability 0.20.

See the R code for this figure

Read the horizontal axis as possible start times and the vertical axis as density, not probability. The navy density line is at 0 before minute 0, jumps to \(1/90\) on the support, and drops back to 0 after minute 90. The green probability is an area: \(18\times(1/90)=0.20\). Compare this with the check above: here the height is far below 1, there it was 2. Both densities are valid, because only the area is a probability.

The CDF of the session model

For the session model, the area to the left of \(x\) is a rectangle of width \(x\) and height \(1/90\). So the CDF is \(F_X(x)=x/90\) for \(0\le x\le 90\). It is 0 before minute 0 and 1 after minute 90. Figure 8.2 shows it. The probability of the window is the difference of two CDF values:

\[P(30<X<48)=F_X(48)-F_X(30)=\frac{48}{90}-\frac{30}{90}=0.20.\]

This is the same 0.20 as the green area in Figure 8.1.

The normal distribution

MIT explanation (review): 08.8 Normal Random Variables.

Many quantities are the sum of many small influences: body height, measurement errors, the weight of a filled package. Such quantities often have a bell-shaped distribution, with most values near the middle and few values far away on either side. The normal distribution is the model for this shape. It is a model, not a law. Many business variables, such as revenues, are strongly skewed and do not follow it.

A normal model needs two numbers. The first is its mean \(\mu\). The second is its standard deviation \(\sigma\). Lecture 3 introduced both symbols.

Definition

Normal distribution. A continuous distribution with a symmetric, bell-shaped density. Its centre is the mean \(\mu\), and its spread is the standard deviation \(\sigma>0\). We write \(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\).

Read the notation with care: the second number in the brackets is the variance \(\sigma^2\), not the standard deviation. For example, \(N(50,100)\) has \(\sigma=\sqrt{100}=10\). In symbols,

\[E[X]=\mu,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=\sigma^2, \qquad \operatorname{SD}(X)=\sigma.\]

The two parameters do different jobs, as Figure 8.3 shows. A change in \(\mu\) shifts the whole curve to the left or to the right, and the shape stays the same. A change in \(\sigma\) makes the curve wider or narrower. A wider curve is also lower, because the total area stays 1.

The figure shows the answer of the model, not the answer of the data. The curve also gives some area to values below 0%, which no offer can have. The check of the model later in this lecture returns to both points. To turn the orange area into a number, you need one more tool: standardising.

Standardising: every normal question becomes a Z question

Definition

z-score. The z-score of a value \(x\) is \(z=\dfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\). It says how many standard deviations \(x\) lies above the mean (positive \(z\)) or below the mean (negative \(z\)). Calculating a z-score is called standardising.

If \(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), then the standardised variable is again normal, with mean 0 and standard deviation 1:

\[Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1).\]

The distribution \(N(0,1)\) is called the standard normal distribution. The mean 0 and the standard deviation 1 follow from the rules for shifting and rescaling in Lecture 3.

Standardising helps because one set of standard-normal values then serves every normal model. Whatever \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are, the question “what is \(P(X\le x)\)?” becomes the question “what is \(P(Z\le z)\)?”, with \(z=(x-\mu)/\sigma\).

Two general rules turn one value \(P(Z\le z)\) into further probabilities. Both hold for every \(z\).

  • Complement rule. The total area under the curve is 1, so \(P(Z>z)=1-P(Z\le z)\).
  • Symmetry rule. The curve is symmetric around 0, so \(P(Z\le -z)=P(Z>z)\).

Example 8.2 (Worked example: normal probabilities from supplied Z values) This is an invented example. Processing time \(Y\) (in minutes) is modelled as \(Y\sim N(50,100)\). The variance is 100, so \(\sigma=\sqrt{100}=10\). In the exam, the standard-normal values that you need are printed in the question, as here:

P(Z <=  1.5) = 0.9332
P(Z <=  1.0) = 0.8413
P(Z <= -1.0) = 0.1587

(a) \(P(Y\le 65)\). Standardise the boundary:

\[z=\frac{65-50}{10}=1.5,\]

so

\[P(Y\le 65)=P(Z\le 1.5)=0.9332.\]

(b) \(P(40<Y<60)\). Standardise both boundaries:

\[z_{\text{low}}=\frac{40-50}{10}=-1.0,\qquad z_{\text{high}}=\frac{60-50}{10}=1.0,\]

then take the difference of the two cumulative probabilities, exactly as with any CDF:

\[P(40<Y<60)=P(Z\le 1.0)-P(Z\le -1.0)=0.8413-0.1587=0.6826.\]

That is the 68% rule seen in Figure 8.4: the interval runs from one standard deviation below the mean to one above.

(c) \(P(Y>60)\) and \(P(Y\le 35)\). These two need the rules. The z-score of 60 is 1.0, and the complement rule gives

\[P(Y>60)=P(Z>1.0)=1-P(Z\le 1.0)=1-0.8413=0.1587.\]

The z-score of 35 is \((35-50)/10=-1.5\). No value for \(-1.5\) is supplied, so use the symmetry rule first and then the complement rule:

\[P(Y\le 35)=P(Z\le -1.5)=P(Z>1.5)=1-0.9332=0.0668.\]

The supplied values also show the symmetry rule at work: \(P(Z\le -1.0)=0.1587\) is the same number as \(P(Z>1.0)\).

pnorm() is the normal CDF in R. Give it the boundary, the mean, and the standard deviation (not the variance). The four lines below repeat the four results of the worked example:

pnorm(65, mean = 50, sd = 10)
[1] 0.9331928
pnorm(60, mean = 50, sd = 10) - pnorm(40, mean = 50, sd = 10)
[1] 0.6826895
1 - pnorm(60, mean = 50, sd = 10)
[1] 0.1586553
pnorm(35, mean = 50, sd = 10)
[1] 0.0668072

The hand calculation and pnorm() agree. Small differences in the fourth decimal come from the rounding of the supplied values.

The answer of the model to the opening question

Now the orange area in Figure 8.5 can be calculated. The model is \(X\sim N(13.2,\,8.4^2)\) and the boundary is 30:

\[z=\frac{30-13.2}{8.4}=2.0.\]

An offer of 30% lies two standard deviations above the mean. The supplied value is \(P(Z\le 2.0)=0.9772\), and the complement rule gives

\[P(X\ge 30)=P(Z\ge 2.0)=1-P(Z\le 2.0)=1-0.9772=0.0228.\]

For a continuous variable, \(\ge\) and \(>\) give the same probability. R confirms the result:

pnorm(2)
[1] 0.9772499
1 - pnorm(2)
[1] 0.02275013
1 - pnorm(30, mean = 13.2, sd = 8.4)
[1] 0.02275013

The normal model says that about 2% of pitches offer 30% or more. The count in the file gave about 5%. The section on checking the model explains the difference.

The reverse direction: from a probability to a value

pnorm() starts from a value and returns the probability at or below it. The reverse question starts from the probability: which value has 90% of the probability at or below it? Such a value is called a quantile.

Definition

Quantile and percentile. The 0.90 quantile of a distribution is the value with 90% of the probability at or below it. It is also called the 90th percentile. In the same way, the 0.25 quantile is the 25th percentile. For the standard normal we write \(z_{0.90}\), and R gives it with qnorm(0.90).

For the standard normal, \(z_{0.90}=1.2816\): 90% of the area lies at or below 1.2816. For any other normal model, solve \(z=(x-\mu)/\sigma\) for \(x\):

\[x=\mu+z\sigma,\]

where \(z\) is the standard-normal quantile for the chosen proportion.

Example 8.3 (Worked example: the 90th percentile of processing time) Take the invented processing-time model \(Y\sim N(50,100)\) again. Which processing time is exceeded by only 10% of cases? This is the 90th percentile of \(Y\). The supplied standard-normal quantile is \(z_{0.90}=1.2816\).

Step 1. The 90th percentile of \(Z\) is 1.2816. The value you look for therefore lies 1.2816 standard deviations above the mean.

Step 2. Translate back to the scale of \(Y\):

\[x=\mu+z\sigma=50+1.2816\times 10=62.8 \text{ minutes.}\]

Step 3. Verify with qnorm(), the reverse of pnorm():

qnorm(0.90, mean = 50, sd = 10)
[1] 62.81552

About 62.8 minutes: 90% of processing times fall at or below it, 10% above.

The central 95% of the standard normal

One pair of quantiles is used again and again in later lectures. Which two values enclose the central 95% of the standard normal? If 95% of the probability lies in the middle, 5% is left for the two tails. By symmetry, each tail holds 2.5%. The upper boundary therefore has 97.5% of the probability at or below it. It is the 0.975 quantile. The lower boundary is the 0.025 quantile.

qnorm(c(0.025, 0.975))
[1] -1.959964  1.959964

So \(z_{0.025}=-1.96\) and \(z_{0.975}=1.96\). In the standard normal, 95% of the probability lies between \(-1.96\) and \(1.96\), with 2.5% in each tail, as Figure 8.6 shows.

Sums of normal variables

A sum of independent normal variables is again normal. Its mean and its variance follow from the sum rules of Lecture 5. Lecture 8 uses this fact for the sample mean.

Checking the normal model against data

The normal distribution is a model, not a label that every quantitative variable automatically deserves. Skewness, bounds, extreme outliers, or a few heavily repeated values can make it a poor description. The check is visual: draw the histogram of the data, overlay the normal curve with the same mean and standard deviation, and see whether the curve reproduces the bars. Figure 8.7 does this for the equity offered (equity_offered_pct) in the Shark Tank file, with the mean of 13.2% and the standard deviation of 8.4 percentage points from the start of this lecture.

Read Figure 8.7 with the five-step sequence from Lecture 1. Unit and variables: one observation is one pitch. The horizontal axis is the equity offered, in percentage points. Scales: the vertical axis is density, so the areas of the bars, not their heights alone, represent proportions of pitches. One element: the green bars are the 1,441 observed offers. The orange curve is \(N(13.2,\,8.4^2)\), the normal model with the same mean and SD. Pattern: the bars pile up between 5% and 20% and stretch out a long right tail. The data are bounded between 0% and 100%, strongly right-skewed, and spiked at round values such as 10% and 20%. The symmetric curve misses all three features. Limits: the figure rejects the normal model for single equity offers. It says nothing yet about averages of offers. A model is a choice, and this check rejects the choice.

The opening question shows what happens without the check. The normal model gave \(P(X\ge 30)=0.0228\), about 2%. The data give 76 of 1,441, about 5%. The model misses the long right tail, and the offers of 30% or more lie in that tail.

The normal model stays useful all the same. From Lecture 8 onwards it describes the sampling distribution of an average, that is, how an average varies from sample to sample. Lectures 7 and 8 explain this idea. The normal model fits those distributions well, even when the underlying variable is as skewed as this one. That result is the central limit theorem. It is the main reason why the normal model is used so much in statistics.

Quantiles of a model distribution

Lecture 1 located quantiles of data: the median, the quartiles \(Q_1\) and \(Q_3\), and the interquartile range of the equity offered (see Lecture 1). A model distribution has the same quantiles. The median is the 0.50 quantile, and the quartiles are the 0.25 and the 0.75 quantiles.

  • Uniform: by symmetry, the median of \(U(a,b)\) is the midpoint \((a+b)/2\). For the session model \(U(0,90)\), the median start time is 45 minutes: half of the probability lies on each side.
  • Normal: by symmetry, the median equals the mean \(\mu\). The quartiles lie \(0.6745\) standard deviations on either side of the mean, \(\mu\pm 0.6745\sigma\). For the processing-time model \(Y\sim N(50,100)\):
qnorm(c(0.25, 0.75), mean = 50, sd = 10)
[1] 43.2551 56.7449

The middle half of this model distribution runs from about 43.3 to 56.7 minutes. The width of this range is the interquartile range of the model.

What a normal label can and cannot say

  • “Normally distributed” is a model, not a fact. It has to be checked against a histogram.
  • The 68% and 95% rules hold for the model. For skewed or bounded data they give wrong numbers, as with the 30% offers.
  • A curve with the right mean and standard deviation can still have the wrong shape.

What you can do with it: use the normal model for quick answers when the histogram looks roughly bell-shaped, and count in the data when it does not. From Lecture 8 on, the normal model describes averages, where it works well.

Answering the driving question

An offer of 30% or more is unusual: about 1 pitch in 20 makes one. In the Shark Tank file, 76 of the 1,441 pitches did, about 5%. The normal model with the same mean and standard deviation says about 2%, because it ignores the long right tail of the offers. So a model with two numbers can answer such a question without counting every pitch, but only when the model fits. The histogram check tells you whether it does.

The tools behind this answer are general. For a continuous variable, no single value has positive probability, \(P(X=x)=0\). Probabilities belong to intervals, and they are areas under a density. For a uniform model, only the length of the interval matters: the 18-minute window in the session model has probability \(18/90=0.20\). For a normal model, you standardise and use the supplied \(Z\) values. In the processing-time model \(N(50,100)\) this gave \(P(Y\le 65)=0.9332\) and \(P(40<Y<60)=0.6826\), and pnorm() confirmed both. A density is a modelling choice, so check the model before you trust its areas.

A founder’s decision (invented example)

Your delivery times look roughly normal, with a mean of 28 minutes and a standard deviation of 6 minutes. You want to promise a delivery time that 90% of orders meet. The 90th percentile is 28 + 1.2816 × 6 = 35.7 minutes. The decision: promise 36 minutes on the website. First check the histogram of your delivery times. If a few deliveries go very wrong, the normal model is too optimistic, and you promise the 90th percentile of the actual times instead.

Changing units (5 minutes)

The session-timing variable \(X\sim U(0,90)\) is recorded in minutes and then converted to seconds. Explain what changes about the PDF and what remains unchanged.

Check your answer

The support stretches from \([0,90]\) minutes to \([0,5400]\) seconds, and the density height drops from \(1/90\) per minute to \(1/5400\) per second: the numerical size of a density depends on the units. Probabilities of corresponding time intervals are unchanged, because they are areas: the 18-minute window becomes a 1,080-second window, and \(1080/5400=18/90=0.20\).
