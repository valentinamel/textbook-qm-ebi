Lecture 8: Sampling distributions and the central limit theorem

By the end of Lecture 8, you can

  • distinguish a population distribution, a sampling distribution, and a realised estimate;
  • derive the mean and variance of a sample mean using the linearity rules;
  • calculate and interpret a standard error;
  • use the square-root rule to relate sample size to precision;
  • state the central limit theorem for this course’s conditions and apply it to means and proportions;
  • explain what the law of large numbers and the CLT each claim — and what neither of them repairs.

Driving question

Any number computed from a sample would have come out differently with a different sample. Before acting on an estimate, a careful analyst asks how much such numbers wobble — and what tames the wobble.

Our sample of 100 gave mean equity 12.76. How wrong could that number be, and why does averaging make it trustworthy even though single pitches are wildly skewed?

In class

The session opens with the usual four-minute entry quiz on the prep videos, then turns to the driving question. Start from where Lecture 7 ended: the five dots. Five random samples of 100 pitches produced five different sample means, plotted on one axis, while the population mean sat still underneath them. During the break you guessed where a sixth mean would land. This lecture is about the pattern behind those dots — the hardest and most useful idea in the course.

The pattern behind the five dots

Imagine not five samples but every possible random sample of the same size, each producing its own sample mean. The distribution of those possible values of \(\bar X\) is its sampling distribution. It describes uncertainty in an estimator, not variation among individual pitches:

  • the population distribution describes how equity offers vary across the 1,441 pitches;
  • the sampling distribution describes how \(\bar X\) would vary across repeated samples;
  • a realised estimate, such as 12.76, is one draw from that sampling distribution.

The two population parameters this chapter keeps returning to are the frame mean and SD of equity offered, and the frame deal proportion. Construct them yourself — because the 1,441 records are the entire frame, the SD divides by \(N\), not \(n-1\):

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
mu_equity <- mean(sharks$equity_offered_pct)
sigma_equity <- sqrt(mean((sharks$equity_offered_pct - mu_equity)^2))
p_deal <- mean(sharks$deal_on_show)
c(mu_equity = mu_equity, sigma_equity = sigma_equity, p_deal = p_deal)
   mu_equity sigma_equity       p_deal 
  13.2076128    8.4414247    0.6120749

So \(\mu = 13.21\) percentage points, \(\sigma = 8.44\) percentage points, and \(p = 0.612\). These are fixed numbers. What varies is any statistic computed from a random sample of the frame.

Everything in the rest of this lecture is the mathematics of what you just manipulated: where the pile sits, how wide it is, and why it becomes bell-shaped.

The mean and variance of a sample mean

For independent, identically distributed variables \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) with population mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\),

\[ E[\bar X]=\mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \operatorname{SD}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}. \]

The first equality says that \(\bar X\) is unbiased for \(\mu\): the pile of sample means is centred on the parameter, exactly as in the lab. The third quantity — the SD of the estimator across repeated samples — is called the standard error (SE). An SD describes variation among observations; an SE describes variation of an estimator.

These are not new facts. They follow from the linearity rules you already have on the formula sheet (the Lecture 8 block, and the Refresher’s linearity rules):

  • \(E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]\) and \(E[aX]=aE[X]\), so \(E[\bar X]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i E[X_i]=\frac{1}{n}\,n\mu=\mu\);
  • \(\operatorname{Var}(aX)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)\) and, for independent variables, \(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)\), so

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\bar X) =\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n}{n}\right) =\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Var}(X_i) =\frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2} =\frac{\sigma^2}{n}. \]

Independent positive and negative deviations partly cancel in an average. That cancellation is why the right-hand histogram in the lab is so much narrower than the population histogram.

In practice \(\sigma\) is usually unknown and is estimated by the sample SD \(S\), giving the estimated standard error \(\operatorname{SE}(\bar X)=S/\sqrt n\). Substituting an estimate for a parameter adds a little extra uncertainty; Lecture 9 opens with the price of that substitution.

Example 10.1 (Worked example: E, Var, and SE for σ = 12, n = 36) A population has mean \(\mu\) and SD \(\sigma=12\). A random sample of \(n=36\) observations is drawn. Find the mean, variance, and SE of \(\bar X\), naming the rule used at each step.

  • Mean. By linearity of expectation (\(E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]\), \(E[aX]=aE[X]\)), \(E[\bar X]=\mu\) — whatever \(\mu\) is, the sample mean is unbiased for it.
  • Variance. By \(\operatorname{Var}(aX)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)\) and additivity of variance under independence, \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n=12^2/36=144/36=4\).
  • SE. Take the square root: \(\operatorname{SD}(\bar X)=\sqrt 4=2\), i.e. \(\sigma/\sqrt n=12/6=2\).

The typical distance between a sample mean and \(\mu\) is about 2 units — one sixth of the typical distance of a single observation from \(\mu\). All three rules are on the formula sheet; the derivations are in the Mathematics Refresher.

The square-root rule

Precision grows with \(\sqrt n\), not with \(n\). This resolves the prep prediction: to cut the SE in half you need \(\mathbf{4\times}\) the observations, because

\[ \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{4n}}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}. \]

Doubling the sample buys less than you might hope; each extra digit of precision gets more expensive. This is worth knowing before you budget for any data collection.

Try it: how large must n become to cut an SE from 4 to 1?

In class this is the pair task before the break. Complete the missing steps before opening the answer.

Step 1 (given). The SE must fall by a factor of \(4/1 = 4\).

Step 2 (you). The SE is proportional to \(1/\sqrt n\). By what factor must \(n\) change to divide the SE by 4?

Step 3 (you). If the original sample had \(n=50\), how many observations does the new design need?

Check your answer

Dividing the SE by 4 requires multiplying \(n\) by \(4^2=16\): the square-root rule in reverse. From \(n=50\) the design needs \(16\times 50=800\) observations. Precision is bought at a quadratic price.

The central limit theorem

MIT explanation (review): 19.2 The Central Limit Theorem.

The lab showed one more pattern that the mean-and-variance rules do not explain: the pile of sample means became bell-shaped, even though the population is strongly skewed. That is the central limit theorem (CLT). Under the independent-sampling conditions used in this course, the standardised sample mean

\[ Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n} \]

approaches a standard normal distribution as \(n\) grows. Equivalently,

\[ \bar X\ \dot\sim\ N\!\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right) \]

for a sufficiently large sample. The dot above \(\sim\) signals an approximation. The normal model from Lecture 6 returns here — not as a model for the data, where it failed for equity offers, but as a model for the sample mean.

There is no universal rule that \(n=30\) is always enough. Strong skewness, extreme outliers, or dependence between observations may require much more data. If the population itself is normal, the sample mean is exactly normal for every \(n\).

Figure 10.1 shows the simulation behind the claim: 5,000 sample means of \(n=5\) draws and 5,000 sample means of \(n=40\) draws from the equity variable.

See the R code for this figure 
library(ggplot2)

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
mu_equity <- mean(sharks$equity_offered_pct)
sigma_equity <- sqrt(mean((sharks$equity_offered_pct - mu_equity)^2))
set.seed(208)
B <- 5000
means_5 <- replicate(B, mean(sample(sharks$equity_offered_pct, 5,
                                    replace = TRUE)))
means_40 <- replicate(B, mean(sample(sharks$equity_offered_pct, 40,
                                     replace = TRUE)))

panel_levels <- c("n = 5", "n = 40")
sim_means <- data.frame(
  sample_mean = c(means_5, means_40),
  panel = factor(rep(panel_levels, each = B), levels = panel_levels)
)

all_means <- c(means_5, means_40)
shared_limits <- c(floor(min(all_means)), ceiling(max(all_means)))
shared_breaks <- seq(shared_limits[1], shared_limits[2], length.out = 36)

normal_grid <- seq(shared_limits[1], shared_limits[2], length.out = 400)
normal_curves <- rbind(
  data.frame(x = normal_grid, panel = panel_levels[1],
             density = dnorm(normal_grid, mu_equity,
                             sigma_equity / sqrt(5))),
  data.frame(x = normal_grid, panel = panel_levels[2],
             density = dnorm(normal_grid, mu_equity,
                             sigma_equity / sqrt(40)))
)
normal_curves$panel <- factor(normal_curves$panel, levels = panel_levels)

ggplot(sim_means, aes(sample_mean)) +
  geom_histogram(aes(y = after_stat(density)), breaks = shared_breaks,
                 fill = "#009E73", alpha = 0.85, colour = "white") +
  geom_line(data = normal_curves, aes(x, density),
            colour = "#D55E00", linewidth = 0.9) +
  facet_wrap(~ panel, scales = "fixed") +
  scale_y_continuous(expand = expansion(mult = c(0, 0.05))) +
  labs(x = "Sample mean equity offered (%)", y = "Density") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 13) +
  theme(
    panel.grid.minor = element_blank(),
    panel.grid.major.x = element_blank(),
    plot.caption = element_text(colour = "#5B6976"),
    axis.title = element_text(colour = "#17324D"),
    strip.text = element_text(colour = "#17324D", face = "bold")
  )

Read Figure 10.1 with the five-step sequence. (1) Each bar counts simulated sample means, not pitches. (2) The horizontal axis is the possible sample mean of equity offered, in percentage points — the same range in both panels, so widths can be compared directly. (3) The vertical axis is density, also on a shared scale. (4) The pattern: the \(n=40\) pile is far narrower and visibly closer to its orange normal curve than the \(n=5\) pile, which still leans right like the population. (5) Takeaway: averaging 40 independent draws from a wildly skewed population already produces a concentrated, nearly normal sampling distribution.

Sample proportions are sample means

For a Bernoulli variable \(X\) with \(P(X=1)=p\) (Lecture 3),

\[ E[X]=p, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=p(1-p). \]

A sample proportion is just the sample mean of an indicator, so every result above applies immediately: \(\widehat P=\bar X\) satisfies

\[ E[\widehat P]=p, \qquad \operatorname{SE}(\widehat P)=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}. \]

Its sampling distribution is approximately normal when the expected numbers of successes and failures are both sufficiently large. This course uses \(np\ge10\) and \(n(1-p)\ge10\) as the working rule (the success–failure condition).

Example 10.2 (Worked example: SE of the deal proportion at n = 100) Suppose we draw a random sample of \(n=100\) pitches from the frame, where the deal proportion is \(p=0.612\). How far is the sample deal proportion typically from 0.612?

  • SE. \(\operatorname{SE}(\widehat P)=\sqrt{0.612\,(1-0.612)/100} =\sqrt{0.2375/100}\approx 0.0487\), about 4.9 percentage points.
  • Success–failure check. \(np=100(0.612)=61.2\ge 10\) and \(n(1-p)=100(0.388)=38.8\ge 10\), so the normal approximation is reasonable.

In R, using p_deal as constructed above:

p_deal <- mean(sharks$deal_on_show)
c(SE_deal_proportion_n_100 = sqrt(p_deal * (1 - p_deal) / 100),
  successes = 100 * p_deal, failures = 100 * (1 - p_deal))
SE_deal_proportion_n_100                successes                 failures 
              0.04872773              61.20749480              38.79250520

The law of large numbers and the CLT answer different questions

MIT explanation (review): 18.4 The Weak Law of Large Numbers.

  • The law of large numbers (LLN) says where the pile goes: \(\bar X\) becomes close to \(\mu\) as \(n\) grows.
  • The CLT says what the pile looks like: the approximate shape and scale of the sampling distribution after standardisation.

One is about convergence of a value, the other about convergence of a distribution. Both assume the sampling is random; neither repairs a sample that was selected badly in the first place.

Check: match each statement to LLN, CLT, or neither
  1. “With enough random draws, the sample mean equity will be very close to 13.21.”
  2. “For \(n=100\) random draws, the sample mean equity is approximately \(N(13.21, 0.844^2)\).”
  3. “With enough respondents, our LinkedIn poll will represent Dutch founders.”

Statement 1 is the LLN, statement 2 is the CLT, and statement 3 is neither: no theorem turns a self-selected sample into a representative one — see the caveat box.

What a bigger sample can and cannot fix

Everything in this lecture concerns random sampling error: the unavoidable wobble of a statistic across random samples. More observations shrink that wobble at the square-root rate. They do nothing for selection bias: if pitches enter the data because producers chose good television, a sample of a million such pitches still describes televised pitches, not entrepreneurs in general. An SE of 0.84 quantifies precision within the recorded-pitch frame; it is silent about who got into the frame, and an on-air deal is still not a completed investment.

Answering the driving question

Our sample of 100 gave mean equity 12.76, while the frame mean is 13.21. How wrong could 12.76 have been? Its standard error:

sigma_equity <- sqrt(mean((sharks$equity_offered_pct -
                             mean(sharks$equity_offered_pct))^2))
c(SE_mean_n_25 = sigma_equity / sqrt(25),
  SE_mean_n_100 = sigma_equity / sqrt(100))
 SE_mean_n_25 SE_mean_n_100 
    1.6882849     0.8441425

At \(n=100\) the SE is about 0.84 percentage points: a random-sample mean typically lands within about 0.84 points of 13.21, and rarely more than about two SEs away. (At \(n=25\) the SE would be twice as large — the square-root rule again.) Averaging is trustworthy despite the wild skew of single pitches because deviations cancel (the variance rule) and the CLT gives the mean a predictable, nearly normal distribution.

Next: We can now say how much a sample mean typically wobbles: one standard error. Turning that into a sentence a report can carry — “the frame mean plausibly lies between \(a\) and \(b\)” — needs one more ingredient, because in a real sample the population \(\sigma\) is unknown and must be estimated too, adding uncertainty of its own. Lecture 9 introduces the t distribution that prices in this extra uncertainty, builds the confidence interval from it, and runs the course’s first formal test of a benchmark claim.

Check your answers
  1. The statistic varies across possible repeated samples; the parameter and the population stay fixed.
  2. Its long-run average across repeated samples equals the population parameter.
  3. An SD describes variation among observations; an SE describes variation of an estimator across samples.
  4. By a factor of 4: the SE is proportional to \(1/\sqrt n\), so halving it requires \(2^2=4\) times the observations.
  5. The standardised sample mean — and therefore, approximately, the sampling distribution of the mean. Not the original observations.
  6. Sample size shrinks random error only. It does not change how units entered the sampling frame or the sample.

Worked exam interpretation (5 minutes)

Question. Mean equity offered in the recorded-pitch frame is \(\mu=13.21\) with \(\sigma=8.44\). A random sample of \(n=100\) pitches is drawn. Using the CLT, find the probability that the sample mean exceeds 14.5 percentage points.

Check a model answer

By the CLT, \(\bar X\ \dot\sim\ N(13.21, 0.844^2)\) since \(\operatorname{SE}=8.44/\sqrt{100}=0.844\). Standardise: \(z=(14.5-13.21)/0.844\approx 1.53\), so \(P(\bar X>14.5)\approx P(Z>1.53)\approx 0.063\). About a 6% chance — and note the question is about the mean, not a single pitch: for one pitch the normal model was already rejected in Lecture 6.
