Lecture 8: Sampling distributions and the central limit theorem

By the end of Lecture 8, you can

  • distinguish a population distribution, a sampling distribution, and a realised estimate
  • derive the mean and variance of a sample mean using the rules for rescaling and for sums
  • calculate and interpret a standard error
  • use the square-root rule to relate sample size to precision, and find the sample size for a target margin of error
  • state the central limit theorem for this course’s conditions and apply it to means and proportions
  • explain what the law of large numbers and the CLT each claim, and what neither of them repairs.

Must know

Be able to do yourself:

  • calculate the standard error of a mean and of a proportion
  • use the square-root rule: four times the data halves the standard error
  • find the sample size needed for a target margin of error
  • check the success-failure condition
  • calculate a probability for a sample mean with the CLT and supplied \(Z\) values
  • tell a standard deviation from a standard error.

Enough to recognise:

  • the derivation of \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n\)
  • the formal statements of the law of large numbers and the CLT
  • the binomial distribution \(\text{Bin}(n,p)\) for a number of successes, with mean \(np\) and variance \(np(1-p)\).

Driving question

Any number computed from a sample would have come out differently with a different sample. Before acting on an estimate, a careful analyst asks how much such numbers vary from sample to sample, and what reduces that variation.

A random sample of 100 pitches from the Shark Tank file had a mean equity offered of 12.76%. How wrong could that number be, and why can we trust an average even though the equity offered in single pitches is strongly skewed?

Lecture material

This section describes the sampling distribution of the sample mean: where it is centred, how wide it is, and what shape it has. It then applies the same results to proportions.

Start with a founder’s question

You asked 100 customers and calculated an average. How far can that average be from the truth, and how many customers would it take to halve the distance? Lecture 7 showed that averages differ from sample to sample. This lecture measures by how much. Start from the five dots of Lecture 7. Five random samples of 100 pitches gave five different sample means, while the population mean stayed fixed. Where would the mean of a sixth sample land? The answer lies in the pattern behind those dots.

The pattern behind the five dots

Imagine not five samples but every possible random sample of the same size, each with its own sample mean. The distribution of those possible values of \(\bar X\) is the sampling distribution of the mean, which Lecture 7 named. It describes the uncertainty of an estimator, not the variation among single pitches. Three objects must be kept apart:

  • The population distribution describes how the equity offered varies across the 1,441 pitches.
  • The sampling distribution describes how \(\bar X\) would vary across repeated samples.
  • A realised estimate, such as 12.76, is one draw from that sampling distribution.

This lecture uses three parameters of the Shark Tank file: the mean and the SD of the equity offered (equity_offered_pct), and the proportion of pitches that ended with a deal (deal_on_show). The 1,441 records are the whole population, so the SD divides by \(N\) and not by \(n-1\):

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
mu_equity <- mean(sharks$equity_offered_pct)
sigma_equity <- sqrt(mean((sharks$equity_offered_pct - mu_equity)^2))
p_deal <- mean(sharks$deal_on_show)
c(mu_equity = mu_equity, sigma_equity = sigma_equity, p_deal = p_deal)
   mu_equity sigma_equity       p_deal 
  13.2076128    8.4414247    0.6120749

So \(\mu = 13.21\)%, \(\sigma = 8.44\) percentage points, and \(p = 0.612\). These are fixed numbers. What varies is any number computed from a random sample of the 1,441 records.

Back to the lab

In Lecture 7 you worked with the sampling-distribution lab: the histogram of sample means sat near 13.2, narrowed as \(n\) grew, and turned bell-shaped although the population is strongly right-skewed. That pile of sample means is the sampling distribution of the mean. The rest of this lecture explains what you saw there: where the pile sits, how wide it is, and why it becomes bell-shaped.

The mean and variance of a sample mean

Let \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) be a random sample of independent and identically distributed (iid) variables, as in Lecture 7, with population mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). Then

\[ E[\bar X]=\mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \operatorname{SD}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}. \]

The first equality says that \(\bar X\) is unbiased for \(\mu\) (Lecture 7): the pile of sample means is centred on the parameter, as in the lab. The third quantity has its own name.

Definition

Standard error (SE). The standard deviation of an estimator across repeated random samples. For the sample mean, \(\operatorname{SE}(\bar X)=\sigma/\sqrt n\).

An SD describes the variation among observations. An SE describes the variation of an estimator.

The three formulas above are not new facts. They follow from the rules for rescaling a variable in Lecture 3 and from the rules for sums of variables in Lecture 5:

  • \(E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]\) and \(E[aX]=aE[X]\), so \(E[\bar X]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i E[X_i]=\frac{1}{n}\,n\mu=\mu\).
  • \(\operatorname{Var}(aX)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)\) and, for independent variables, \(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)\), so

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\bar X) =\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n}{n}\right) =\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Var}(X_i) =\frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2} =\frac{\sigma^2}{n}. \]

Independent positive and negative deviations partly cancel in an average. That is why the right-hand histogram in the lab is so much narrower than the population histogram.

The variance \(\sigma^2/n\) shrinks to zero as \(n\) grows, so \(\bar X\) gets close to \(\mu\). Estimators with this property are called consistent.

The formulas for \(E[\bar X]\) and \(\operatorname{SD}(\bar X)\) give the centre and the spread of the sampling distribution, not its shape. In one case the shape is known exactly. If the population itself is normal, the sample mean is exactly normal for every \(n\): \(\bar X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)\). The reason is that a sum of independent normal variables is again normal (Lecture 6). The central limit theorem below deals with populations that are not normal.

In practice \(\sigma\) is usually unknown and is estimated by the sample SD \(S\). This gives the estimated standard error \(\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\bar X)=S/\sqrt n\). The hat marks an estimate: \(\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}\) is a standard error calculated from the sample, just as \(\hat p\) is a proportion calculated from the sample. Replacing a parameter by an estimate adds a little extra uncertainty. Lecture 9 shows how to allow for it.

Example 10.1 (Worked example: E, Var, and SE for σ = 12, n = 36) A population has mean \(\mu\) and SD \(\sigma=12\). A random sample of \(n=36\) observations is drawn. Find the mean, variance, and SE of \(\bar X\), naming the rule used at each step.

  • Mean. By the rules for expectations (\(E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]\), \(E[aX]=aE[X]\)), \(E[\bar X]=\mu\): whatever \(\mu\) is, the sample mean is unbiased for it.
  • Variance. By \(\operatorname{Var}(aX)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)\) and the sum rule for independent variables, \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n=12^2/36=144/36=4\).
  • SE. Take the square root: \(\operatorname{SD}(\bar X)=\sqrt 4=2\), which is \(\sigma/\sqrt n=12/6=2\).

The typical distance between a sample mean and \(\mu\) is about 2 units. That is one sixth of the typical distance of a single observation from \(\mu\). All three rules are on the formula sheet.

The square-root rule

Precision grows with \(\sqrt n\), not with \(n\). This answers the prediction question from before class: to cut the SE in half you need four times the observations, because

\[ \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{4n}}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}. \]

Doubling the sample size does not halve the SE. It only divides the SE by \(\sqrt 2\approx 1.41\). More precision becomes more and more expensive, which is worth knowing before you budget for data collection.

Try it: how large must n become to cut an SE from 4 to 1?

Try this with your neighbour first. Complete the missing steps before opening the answer.

Step 1 (given). The SE must fall by a factor of \(4/1 = 4\).

Step 2 (you). The SE is proportional to \(1/\sqrt n\). By what factor must \(n\) change to divide the SE by 4?

Step 3 (you). If the original sample had \(n=50\), how many observations does the new design need?

Check your answer

Dividing the SE by 4 requires multiplying \(n\) by \(4^2=16\): the square-root rule in reverse. From \(n=50\) the design needs \(16\times 50=800\) observations. Four times the precision costs sixteen times the data.

The central limit theorem

MIT explanation (review): 19.2 The Central Limit Theorem.

The lab showed one more pattern that the rules for the mean and the variance do not explain. The pile of sample means became bell-shaped, although the population is strongly skewed. The central limit theorem describes this pattern.

Definition

Central limit theorem (CLT). For a large random sample of iid observations with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), the sample mean is approximately normal, whatever the shape of the population: \(\bar X\ \dot\sim\ N\!\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)\).

The dot above \(\sim\) marks an approximation, which improves as \(n\) grows. The same statement can be written with the standardised sample mean

\[ Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}, \]

which is approximately standard normal for large \(n\). The normal model of Lecture 6 returns here. It is not a model for the data, where it failed for the equity offered. It is a model for the sample mean.

With the CLT you can calculate a probability for a sample mean. Take the worked example above (Example 10.1), with \(\sigma=12\) and \(n=36\), so that the SE is 2. Suppose that \(\mu=50\). By the CLT, \(\bar X\) is approximately \(N(50,2^2)\). The probability of a sample mean above 53 is

\[ P(\bar X>53)\approx P\!\left(Z>\frac{53-50}{2}\right)=P(Z>1.5) =1-0.9332=0.0668, \]

with the supplied value \(P(Z\le1.5)=0.9332\). A sample mean above 53 occurs in about 7% of random samples.

There is no universal rule that \(n=30\) is always enough. Thirty is a common starting point for roughly symmetric data. Strongly skewed data, such as revenues or deal amounts, need more. Extreme outliers and dependence between observations can also require much more data.

Figure 10.1 shows a simulation: 5,000 sample means of \(n=5\) draws and 5,000 sample means of \(n=40\) draws from the equity offered (equity_offered_pct). The simulation draws with replacement, so its draws are exactly independent.

How many customers must I ask?

The square-root rule compares two sample sizes. A founder often asks a more direct question: how large must the sample be to reach a given precision?

The CLT gives the answer. The central 95% of a normal distribution lies within 1.96 standard deviations of its mean, because \(z_{0.975}=1.96\) (Lecture 6). So in about 95% of random samples, \(\bar X\) lies within \(1.96\times\sigma/\sqrt n\) of \(\mu\). This distance is called the margin of error at the 95% level. Lecture 9 uses it to build confidence intervals. If the margin of error may be at most \(m\), solve for \(n\):

\[ 1.96\times\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\le m \qquad\text{gives}\qquad n\ge\left(\frac{1.96\times\sigma}{m}\right)^2. \]

Example 10.2 (Worked example: a sample size for a margin of 2 euros) An invented example. You want to know the average order value of your customers to within 2 euros, at the 95% level. How many customers must you survey?

  • Planning value. The formula needs \(\sigma\), which you do not know before the survey. A small pilot survey gave an SD of about 12 euros, so use \(\sigma=12\) as a planning value. The target margin of 2 euros is one sixth of that SD.
  • Sample size. \(n\ge(1.96\times 12/2)^2=11.76^2\approx 138.3\).
  • Round up. Survey \(n=139\) customers. Always round up: with 138 customers the margin would be slightly above 2 euros.

Halving the margin to 1 euro needs \((1.96\times 12/1)^2=23.52^2\approx 553.2\), so 554 customers. Half the margin costs about four times the sample, as the square-root rule says.

sigma_plan <- 12
n_needed <- c(margin_2_euros = (1.96 * sigma_plan / 2)^2,
              margin_1_euro  = (1.96 * sigma_plan / 1)^2)
n_needed
margin_2_euros  margin_1_euro 
      138.2976       553.1904 
ceiling(n_needed)   # ceiling() rounds up to the next whole number
margin_2_euros  margin_1_euro 
           139            554

Sample proportions are sample means

For a Bernoulli variable \(X\) with \(P(X=1)=p\) (Lecture 3),

\[ E[X]=p, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=p(1-p). \]

A sample proportion is the sample mean of a 0/1 variable (Lecture 7), so every result above applies: \(\widehat P=\bar X\) satisfies

\[ E[\widehat P]=p, \qquad \operatorname{SE}(\widehat P)=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}. \]

This formula needs the parameter \(p\), which is usually unknown. Replacing \(p\) by the estimate \(\hat p\) gives the estimated standard error

\[ \widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\widehat P)=\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}}. \]

Use the version with \(p\) when the population proportion is given or assumed. Use the estimated version when you only have the sample, as in Tutorial 3.

By the CLT, the sampling distribution of \(\widehat P\) is approximately normal for a large sample. How large is large enough for a proportion? The next section answers this with the distribution of a count.

Counting successes: the binomial distribution

Take \(n\) independent 0/1 trials, each with the same success probability \(p\). The number of successes is the sum \(Y=X_1+\cdots+X_n\). Its distribution is called the binomial distribution, written \(Y\sim\text{Bin}(n,p)\). The sum rules of Lecture 5 give

\[ E[Y]=np, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y)=np(1-p). \]

The sample proportion is this count divided by the number of trials: \(\widehat P=Y/n\).

An invented example: 200 people visit your web shop, and each of them buys with probability 0.05, independently of the others. The number of buyers is \(Y\sim\text{Bin}(200,\,0.05)\). You expect \(np=200\times 0.05=10\) buyers, with a standard deviation of \(\sqrt{200\times 0.05\times 0.95}=\sqrt{9.5}\approx 3.1\) buyers.

A count is a sum, so the CLT applies to it: for large \(n\) the binomial distribution is close to a normal distribution. Figure 10.2 shows when \(n\) is large enough. With 80 visitors you expect only \(np=4\) buyers. The distribution is skewed, with a longer tail to the right. The normal curve puts about 2% of its area below zero, where no count can lie. The fit is worse than in the right panel. With 400 visitors you expect \(np=20\) buyers, and the normal curve fits well.

This course therefore uses the normal approximation for a count or a proportion only if \(np\ge10\) and \(n(1-p)\ge10\). This rule is called the success-failure condition: the expected numbers of successes and of failures must both be at least 10. When \(p\) is unknown, check the rule with the counts in the sample: at least 10 successes and at least 10 failures. In a test, use the null value \(p_0\) (Lecture 10). Some textbooks use 5 in place of 10. Ten is the safer choice. You do not need to calculate binomial probabilities in this course.

Example 10.3 (Worked example: SE of the deal proportion at n = 100) Suppose we draw a random sample of \(n=100\) pitches from the frame, where the deal proportion is \(p=0.612\). How far is the sample deal proportion typically from 0.612?

  • SE. \(\operatorname{SE}(\widehat P)=\sqrt{0.612\,(1-0.612)/100} =\sqrt{0.2375/100}\approx 0.0487\), about 4.9 percentage points.
  • Success-failure check. \(np=100(0.612)=61.2\ge 10\) and \(n(1-p)=100(0.388)=38.8\ge 10\), so the normal approximation is reasonable.

In R, using p_deal as constructed above:

p_deal <- mean(sharks$deal_on_show)
c(SE_deal_proportion_n_100 = sqrt(p_deal * (1 - p_deal) / 100),
  successes = 100 * p_deal, failures = 100 * (1 - p_deal))

              0.04872773              61.20749480              38.79250520

The law of large numbers and the CLT answer different questions

MIT explanation (review): 18.4 The Weak Law of Large Numbers.

Definition

Law of large numbers (LLN). As the sample size \(n\) grows, the sample mean \(\bar X\) of a random sample gets close to the population mean \(\mu\).

This is the consistency of \(\bar X\) noted above. The LLN and the CLT answer different questions:

  • The LLN says where the pile of sample means goes: towards \(\mu\).
  • The CLT says what the pile looks like: its approximate shape and width.

The LLN is about a value. The CLT is about a distribution. Both assume that the sampling is random. Neither repairs a sample that was selected badly in the first place.

Check: match each statement to LLN, CLT, or neither
  1. “With enough random draws, the sample mean equity will be very close to 13.21.”
  2. “For \(n=100\) random draws, the sample mean equity is approximately \(N(13.21, 0.844^2)\).”
  3. “With enough respondents, our LinkedIn poll will represent Dutch founders.”

Statement 1 is the LLN, statement 2 is the CLT, and statement 3 is neither. No theorem turns a self-selected sample into a representative one (see the box below).

What a bigger sample can and cannot fix

  • More data shrink the random error, at the square-root rate.
  • More data do nothing for selection bias. A million televised pitches still describe televised pitches.
  • The standard error measures precision inside the list you sampled from. It is silent about who is on the list.

What you can do with it: first fix who you ask, then decide how many. Stop collecting when more data would no longer change your decision.

Answering the driving question

A random sample of 100 pitches from the Shark Tank file had a mean equity offered of 12.76%, while the mean of all 1,441 records is 13.21%. How wrong could 12.76 have been? Its standard error:

sigma_equity <- sqrt(mean((sharks$equity_offered_pct -
                             mean(sharks$equity_offered_pct))^2))
c(SE_mean_n_25 = sigma_equity / sqrt(25),
  SE_mean_n_100 = sigma_equity / sqrt(100))
 SE_mean_n_25 SE_mean_n_100 
    1.6882849     0.8441425

At \(n=100\) the SE is about 0.84 percentage points, which is about 6% of the population mean. The mean of a random sample typically lands within about 0.84 points of 13.21, and rarely more than about two SEs away. The sample with 12.76 missed by 0.45 points, about half an SE. At \(n=25\) the SE would be twice as large, by the square-root rule.

Why can we trust the average although the equity offered in single pitches is strongly skewed? Deviations cancel in an average (the variance rule), and the CLT gives the mean a predictable, nearly normal distribution.

One detail: our samples are drawn without replacement from 1,441 records. That makes the exact standard error slightly smaller than the formula says, 0.81 instead of 0.84. The 10% rule says that the difference can be ignored. The optional box in Lecture 7 gives the exact formula.

A founder’s decision (invented example)

You ask customers to rate your boxes from 1 to 10. Ratings have a standard deviation of about 2. With 25 customers the standard error of the mean is 2/5 = 0.4. With 100 customers it is 0.2. Is the larger survey worth four times the effort? Your rule is to change the menu only if the mean rating is below 7. The first 25 customers give a mean of 8.4, which is 3.5 standard errors above 7. The decision: stop surveying and keep the menu. More data would not change what you do.

Next: We can now say how much a sample mean typically varies: by about one standard error. A report needs a sentence such as “the population mean plausibly lies between \(a\) and \(b\)”. That takes one more step, because in a real sample \(\sigma\) is unknown and must be estimated too, which adds uncertainty. Lecture 9 introduces the t distribution, which allows for this extra uncertainty, and builds the confidence interval from it.

Recall questions (10 minutes)

  1. What varies in a sampling distribution?
  2. Why is \(E[\bar X]=\mu\) called unbiasedness?
  3. What is the difference between an SD and an SE?
  4. To cut an SE in half, by what factor must the sample size grow, and why?
  5. What does the CLT say becomes approximately normal?
  6. Why does a larger sample not remove selection bias?
Check your answers
  1. The statistic varies across possible repeated samples. The parameter and the population stay fixed.
  2. Its long-run average across repeated samples equals the population parameter.
  3. An SD describes variation among observations. An SE describes variation of an estimator across samples.
  4. By a factor of 4: the SE is proportional to \(1/\sqrt n\), so halving it requires \(2^2=4\) times the observations.
  5. The sample mean. For a large sample its sampling distribution is approximately normal. The single observations do not become normal.
  6. Sample size shrinks random error only. It does not change how units entered the sampling frame or the sample.

Worked exam interpretation (5 minutes)

Question. The mean equity offered (equity_offered_pct) in the Shark Tank file is \(\mu=13.21\)% with \(\sigma=8.44\) percentage points. A random sample of \(n=100\) pitches is drawn. Using the CLT, find the probability that the sample mean exceeds 14.5%. Supplied value: \(P(Z\le 1.53)=0.937\).

Check a model answer

By the CLT, \(\bar X\ \dot\sim\ N(13.21, 0.844^2)\) since \(\operatorname{SE}=8.44/\sqrt{100}=0.844\). Standardise: \(z=(14.5-13.21)/0.844\approx 1.53\), so \(P(\bar X>14.5)\approx P(Z>1.53)=1-0.937=0.063\). That is about a 6% chance. Note that the question is about the mean, not about a single pitch. For a single pitch the normal model was rejected in Lecture 6.
