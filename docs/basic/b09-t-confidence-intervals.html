Lecture 9: The t distribution, confidence intervals, and the two-sided test

By the end of Lecture 9, you can

  • explain why estimating the population SD leads to a t distribution with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom, and use its critical values;
  • construct a t confidence interval for a population mean;
  • express comparison with a benchmark as a parameter equal to zero;
  • state a two-sided null and alternative hypothesis;
  • calculate and interpret a t statistic and a two-sided p-value;
  • connect a 95% confidence interval to a 5% two-sided test;
  • state correctly what “95% confidence” is a property of.

Driving question

A common pitch-deck rule of thumb says founders offer investors about 15 percent equity. Our data can put such a benchmark to the test — if we account honestly for sampling noise.

Is the typical initial equity ask in the recorded-pitch frame really different from 15 percent?

Before class

Total preparation time: about 30 minutes.

Video preparation (20 minutes)
Watch Confidence Intervals, Confidence Intervals When the Variance Is Unknown, and Introduction to Hypothesis Testing. Focus on repeated-sampling coverage, the t reference distribution, and the role of the null value. All links are also listed in the External Video Guide.

Reactivate (5 minutes). Let \(X\) be initial equity offered and define the equity gap

\[ D = X - 15. \]

Write two-sided hypotheses for the question: “Does population mean initial equity differ from 15 percentage points?”

Check your preparation

Let \(\delta=E[D]=\mu_X-15\). Then

\[ H_0:\delta=0 \qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_1:\delta\ne0. \]

This is equivalent to testing \(H_0:\mu_X=15\) against \(H_1:\mu_X\ne15\). Writing the contrast as \(\delta\) makes the central question “is the difference zero?” — the same structure later used for regression coefficients.

Predict (5 minutes). Answer the one-click Mentimeter question posted on Canvas before you come to class: in a real sample, the unknown population SD \(\sigma\) must be replaced by the sample SD \(S\). Should that substitution make a 95% interval wider, narrower, or leave it unchanged? Commit to an answer; the lecture resolves it.

If intervals, standardisation, or degrees of freedom feel rusty, review Mathematics Refresher: intervals, standardisation, and degrees of freedom.

This lecture turns those two numbers into a defensible claim: a range of plausible values for the parameter, and a yes-or-no decision about a benchmark.

From a benchmark to a zero contrast

The driving question compares a population mean to the benchmark 15. The prep exercise already made the key move: subtract the benchmark and study the gap

\[ D = X - 15, \qquad \delta = E[D] = \mu_X - 15. \]

“Is mean equity different from 15?” becomes “is \(\delta\) different from zero?” This looks like a cosmetic rewrite, but it is the single most reusable idea of the next four weeks: every benchmark question can be turned into a question about a parameter being zero. Lecture 10 shows that group differences, proportion gaps, and regression slopes all wear the same zero-contrast uniform. Learn the pattern once here, and every later test is a rerun.

The price of estimating σ: the t distribution

Lecture 8 gave the standardised sample mean

\[ Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}, \]

which is approximately standard normal by the CLT. But this \(Z\) contains the population SD \(\sigma\) — a number nobody knows in a real study. In practice we substitute the sample SD \(S\), and \(S\) is itself a random quantity: it changes from sample to sample, and in a small sample it can miss \(\sigma\) badly. Standardising with an estimated spread therefore produces a statistic that is more variable than \(Z\). For independent observations from a normal population,

\[ T=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n} \sim t_{n-1}. \]

The t distribution is symmetric around zero like the standard normal, but it has heavier tails: extreme standardised values are more likely, precisely because \(S\) sometimes underestimates the true spread. Its degrees of freedom control how much heavier:

  • few degrees of freedom mean \(S\) is estimated imprecisely, so the tails are heavier;
  • more degrees of freedom mean more information about spread;
  • as the degrees of freedom increase, the t distribution approaches \(N(0,1)\).

For a one-sample mean, \(df=n-1\) — the same \(n-1\) you met in Lecture 7: once the sample mean is estimated, the \(n\) deviations must sum to zero, so only \(n-1\) of them vary freely when spread is estimated.

The difference in Figure 12.1 is most visible in the tails. This is why a 95% t critical value is larger than the normal 1.96 when \(df\) is small — the reference curve must reach further out to enclose 95% of its probability.

This resolves the prep prediction: replacing \(\sigma\) with \(S\) makes the interval wider, because the heavier-tailed t distribution demands a larger critical value. The extra width is the honest price of estimating one more quantity from the same data.

Check: which is larger, \(t_{0.975,11}\) or \(t_{0.975,99}\)?

\(t_{0.975,11}\approx 2.20\) is larger than \(t_{0.975,99}\approx 1.98\). With only 11 degrees of freedom, \(S\) is an imprecise estimate of \(\sigma\), so the t distribution has heavier tails and its 97.5% quantile sits further from zero. At \(df=99\) the critical value is already close to the normal 1.96 — with a large sample, the t and normal answers nearly coincide. Verify in R: qt(0.975, df = 11) and qt(0.975, df = 99).

The interval recipe

A confidence interval combines the estimate with its uncertainty. The general form is

\[ \text{estimate} \ \pm\ \text{critical value}\times\text{standard error}. \]

The product after the \(\pm\) is the margin of error. A confidence interval becomes:

  • wider when the confidence level increases (a larger critical value);
  • wider when observations are more variable (a larger SE);
  • narrower when the sample size increases, all else equal.

For an independent sample with unknown population SD,

\[ \bar X\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\frac{S}{\sqrt n} \]

is a \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(\mu\). For a large independent sample, the interval is also a reasonable approximation when the population is not normal — the CLT again. The same recipe applied to the gap variable \(D\) gives an interval for \(\delta\).

Example 12.1 (Worked example: a 95% interval by hand) A random sample of \(n=64\) ventures reports a mean score of \(\bar x = 80\) with sample SD \(s = 16\). The industry benchmark is 75. Construct a 95% confidence interval for \(\delta=\mu-75\), voicing every arithmetic step.

  • Estimate. The estimated gap is the sample mean minus the benchmark: \(\widehat\delta = 80 - 75 = 5\).
  • Standard error. \(\operatorname{SE}=s/\sqrt n = 16/\sqrt{64}\). The square root of 64 is 8, so \(\operatorname{SE}=16/8=2\).
  • Degrees of freedom and critical value. \(df=n-1=63\), and \(t_{0.975,63}\approx 2.00\) (the exam supplies this value; in R it is qt(0.975, df = 63), about 1.998).
  • Margin of error. Critical value times SE: \(2.00\times 2 = 4\).
  • Interval. Estimate plus and minus the margin: \(5 - 4 = 1\) and \(5 + 4 = 9\), so the 95% interval for \(\delta\) is \((1,\,9)\).

Every plausible value of the gap is positive: the data are consistent with a population mean between 1 and 9 points above the benchmark. Adding 75 back to both endpoints gives the equivalent interval \((76,\,84)\) for \(\mu\) itself — the gap form and the mean form are the same statement.

Check conditions before calculating

The formula is justified by the sampling design and distributional conditions, not by the presence of an R command. Before any interval or test, walk this checklist:

  1. Random selection. Were the rows selected randomly from a defined frame? (Ours will be: the seed-123 sample of 100 from the 1,441-record frame.)
  2. The 10% condition. Sampling without replacement creates weak dependence, but \(100/1441\approx 0.07 < 0.10\), so the usual independent-sample SE is a reasonable approximation.
  3. Shape and sample size. Pitch-level equity is right-skewed, but \(n=100\) makes the t approximation for the sample mean far more credible than it would be for a very small sample. Still check for data errors or observations that dominate the mean.

These conditions support inference to the recorded-pitch frame. They do not make televised pitches representative of all entrepreneurs — the caveat box returns to this.

The Shark Tank case, with nothing hidden

Now the real question. The recipe runs on the reproducible sample of 100 from Lecture 7, and every object is constructed on screen:

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
set.seed(123)
sample_rows <- sample(seq_len(nrow(sharks)), size = 100, replace = FALSE)
pitch_sample <- sharks[sample_rows, ]

benchmark <- 15
pitch_sample$equity_gap_pp <-
  pitch_sample$equity_offered_pct - benchmark

gap_bar <- mean(pitch_sample$equity_gap_pp)   # estimated gap
gap_sd  <- sd(pitch_sample$equity_gap_pp)     # sample SD of the gap
n       <- nrow(pitch_sample)
df      <- n - 1
gap_se  <- gap_sd / sqrt(n)                   # standard error

c(
  sample_mean_equity = mean(pitch_sample$equity_offered_pct),
  sample_mean_gap = gap_bar,
  sample_sd_gap = gap_sd,

A hypothesis test starts with a null model

The interval displays every plausible value. A test answers one question about one value. For a two-sided test,

\[ H_0:\delta=0 \qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_1:\delta\ne0. \]

The null says there is no population mean difference from the benchmark. The alternative allows a difference in either direction. Choose the two-sided alternative from the research question before inspecting the sample estimate. (Directional, one-sided alternatives exist; Lecture 10 treats them and explains when they are — and are not — legitimate.)

Standardise the estimated gap using its SE:

\[ T= \frac{\widehat\delta-0} {\operatorname{SE}(\widehat\delta)} = \frac{\bar D}{S_D/\sqrt n}. \]

By hand first, from the numbers already on screen: the estimate is \(-2.24\), its SE is \(0.77\), so

\[ t=\frac{-2.24}{0.77}\approx -2.91. \]

The sign records direction — below the benchmark; the absolute value records distance from zero in standard-error units — almost three SEs. Now the same calculation in R, with the p-value:

t_value <- gap_bar / gap_se
p_value <- 2 * pt(-abs(t_value), df = df)

c(
  estimate = gap_bar,
  standard_error = gap_se,
  t_statistic = t_value,
  degrees_of_freedom = df,
  two_sided_p_value = p_value
)
          estimate     standard_error        t_statistic degrees_of_freedom 
      -2.240000000        0.770788656       -2.906114385       99.000000000 
 two_sided_p_value 
       0.004515396

A two-sided p-value uses both tails

The p-value is the probability, assuming \(H_0\), of obtaining a test statistic at least as far from zero as the observed statistic in either direction:

\[ p=P\!\left(|T_{99}|\ge|t_{obs}|\mid H_0\right). \]

See the R code for this figure 
library(ggplot2)

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
set.seed(123)
sample_rows <- sample(seq_len(nrow(sharks)), size = 100, replace = FALSE)
pitch_sample <- sharks[sample_rows, ]
pitch_sample$equity_gap_pp <- pitch_sample$equity_offered_pct - 15
gap_bar <- mean(pitch_sample$equity_gap_pp)
gap_se <- sd(pitch_sample$equity_gap_pp) / sqrt(nrow(pitch_sample))
df <- nrow(pitch_sample) - 1
t_value <- gap_bar / gap_se

null_curve <- data.frame(x = seq(-4.5, 4.5, length.out = 1200))
null_curve$density <- dt(null_curve$x, df = df)
left_tail <- subset(null_curve, x <= -abs(t_value))
right_tail <- subset(null_curve, x >= abs(t_value))

ggplot(null_curve, aes(x, density)) +
  geom_area(data = left_tail, fill = "#D55E00", alpha = 0.85) +
  geom_area(data = right_tail, fill = "#D55E00", alpha = 0.85) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = c(-abs(t_value), abs(t_value)),
             linetype = "dashed", colour = "#D55E00", linewidth = 0.6) +

           colour = "#17324D", size = 3.4, hjust = -0.15) +
  scale_y_continuous(expand = expansion(mult = c(0, 0.05))) +
  labs(x = "t statistic under the null", y = "Density") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 13) +

    panel.grid.minor = element_blank(),

Read Figure 12.2 with the five-step sequence. (1) The curve is the distribution of the t statistic if the null were true — not data, a null model. (2) The horizontal axis is the possible t statistic; the vertical axis is density, so probability is area. (3) The dashed lines mark \(-2.91\) and \(+2.91\): statistics as far from zero as the one observed. (4) The pattern: the two orange tail areas beyond the dashed lines are tiny — together about 0.0045. (5) Takeaway: if the true mean gap were zero, a sample as extreme as ours would occur in roughly 5 of every 1,000 samples. That is not the probability that \(H_0\) is true, and not the size of the gap.

At \(\alpha=0.05\), reject \(H_0\) because \(p<0.05\). The random sample provides evidence that mean initial equity in the recorded-pitch frame differs from 15 percentage points; the negative estimate indicates that it is lower.

The confidence interval and test tell the same story

For the same two-sided t procedure:

  • reject \(H_0:\delta=0\) at level \(\alpha\) exactly when the \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval excludes zero;
  • do not reject when the interval includes zero.

Here the gap interval \((-3.77,\,-0.71)\) excludes zero, matching the rejection. The interval adds information the p-value does not: it displays the direction, plausible magnitude, and precision of the difference. Course policy, from here to the end of Intermediate: report the estimate and interval alongside any test decision.

Try it: read the decision straight from an interval

A colleague reports a 95% confidence interval for a mean gap: \((-1.4,\,3.8)\) percentage points. No t statistic, no p-value.

Step 1 (given). The matched two-sided test rejects \(H_0:\delta=0\) at 5% exactly when the 95% interval excludes zero.

Step 2 (you). Does this interval exclude zero? State the test decision without computing anything.

Step 3 (you). What may you conclude about the population mean gap — and what may you not conclude?

Check your answer

Zero lies inside \((-1.4,\,3.8)\), so the 5% two-sided test does not reject \(H_0:\delta=0\). The data are compatible with a zero gap — and also with gaps as large as 3.8 points in one direction and 1.4 in the other. You may not conclude that the gap is zero: “do not reject” is not “accept,” a distinction Lecture 10 builds on.

Interpret 95% confidence correctly

The parameter is fixed; interval endpoints vary from sample to sample. “95%” describes the procedure: if the same valid recipe were repeated on many random samples, about 95% of the resulting intervals would contain the parameter.

The simulation in Figure 12.3 repeats the random sample and interval calculation 100 times. The fixed target is the mean initial equity among all 1,441 recorded pitches.

See the R code for this figure 
library(ggplot2)

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
set.seed(209)
B_coverage <- 100
n_coverage <- 100
target_mean <- mean(sharks$equity_offered_pct)
ci_lower <- ci_upper <- ci_centre <- numeric(B_coverage)

for (i in seq_len(B_coverage)) {
  repeated_sample <- sample(
    sharks$equity_offered_pct,
    size = n_coverage,
    replace = FALSE
  )
  repeated_mean <- mean(repeated_sample)
  repeated_se <- sd(repeated_sample) / sqrt(n_coverage)
  repeated_critical <- qt(0.975, df = n_coverage - 1)
  ci_centre[i] <- repeated_mean
  ci_lower[i] <- repeated_mean - repeated_critical * repeated_se
  ci_upper[i] <- repeated_mean + repeated_critical * repeated_se
}

intervals <- data.frame(
  lower = ci_lower, centre = ci_centre, upper = ci_upper,
  covers = ifelse(ci_lower <= target_mean & ci_upper >= target_mean,
                  "contains the target mean", "misses the target mean")
)
intervals <- intervals[order(intervals$centre), ]
intervals$position <- seq_len(B_coverage)

ggplot(intervals, aes(y = position, colour = covers)) +
  geom_segment(aes(x = lower, xend = upper, yend = position),
               linewidth = 0.7) +
  geom_point(aes(x = centre), size = 0.9) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = target_mean, linetype = "dashed",
             colour = "#C98B2E", linewidth = 0.7) +
  annotate("text", x = target_mean, y = B_coverage + 3.5,
           label = "mu == 13.21", parse = TRUE,
           colour = "#17324D", size = 3.4, hjust = -0.1) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = c("contains the target mean" = "#009E73",
                                 "misses the target mean" = "#D55E00")) +
  scale_y_continuous(breaks = c(1, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100)) +
  labs(x = "Mean initial equity offered (%)",
       y = "Repeated sample (ordered by estimate)",
       colour = NULL) +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 13) +
  theme(
    panel.grid.minor = element_blank(),
    panel.grid.major.y = element_blank(),
    plot.caption = element_text(colour = "#5B6976"),
    axis.title = element_text(colour = "#17324D"),
    legend.position = "top"
  )

Each horizontal segment in Figure 12.3 is one 95% confidence interval; its dot is that sample’s mean. The gold dashed vertical line is the fixed target mean, 13.21. In this run (seed 209), 96 of 100 intervals cross the line. A new run need not give exactly 95, but across many repetitions the proportion approaches 95% when the procedure’s conditions hold.

One sentence is now forbidden for the rest of your studies:

“There is a 95% probability that the population mean is in my interval.”

The realised interval \((11.23, 14.29)\) either contains the fixed parameter or it does not — there is no probability left once the sample is drawn. The correct replacement:

“This interval was produced by a procedure that captures the fixed population mean in about 95% of repeated valid samples.”

What significance can and cannot tell you

Three boundaries around everything in this lecture. First, significance is not importance: \(p\approx 0.005\) says the gap is measured precisely enough to distinguish it from zero, not that a 2.24-point gap matters for any decision — magnitude and context decide that. Second, significance is not causation: the evidence concerns a mean in an observational frame; nothing here says why equity asks sit below 15 points. Third, a narrow interval repairs nothing about selection: the interval quantifies random sampling error within the recorded-pitch frame. If the frame itself is a producer-curated selection of telegenic pitches — and it is — then no interval width, however impressive, extends the conclusion to entrepreneurs in general. An on-air agreement is still not a completed investment.

Answering the driving question

Yes — within the frame. The seed-123 sample gives an estimated gap of \(-2.24\) percentage points with SE \(0.77\); the 95% interval for mean equity is \((11.23, 14.29)\), which excludes 15, and the two-sided test rejects \(H_0:\delta=0\) at 5% (\(t=-2.91\), \(p\approx 0.005\)). The evidence says the frame’s mean initial equity ask sits below 15 percent — plausibly by between 0.7 and 3.8 points. Whether that gap matters commercially, and what caused it, are separate questions the test cannot answer.

Next: We can now test a benchmark and report the answer as an interval and a p-value. But every test decision can be wrong in one of two ways — rejecting a true null, or missing a real effect — and the two mistakes rarely cost the same. Lecture 10 names the two error types, shows the four levers that make wrong calls rarer (power), settles when a one-sided alternative is legitimate, and then opens the door this whole course has been walking toward: the same estimate–SE–t–p–interval row, sitting in a regression table.

Check your answers
  1. \(S\) is itself estimated and varies from sample to sample; the t distribution’s heavier tails represent that extra uncertainty.
  2. Estimating the sample mean imposes one constraint (deviations sum to zero) before spread is estimated, leaving \(n-1\) freely varying deviations.

From worked to faded (10 minutes)

Re-read the hand-worked interval (Example 12.1). Then complete the same calculation with new numbers: \(n=25\) customers, mean satisfaction \(\bar x = 42\), sample SD \(s = 10\), benchmark 40, so \(\delta=\mu-40\).

Step 1 (given). The estimated gap is \(42-40=2\), and \(t_{0.975,24}\approx 2.06\).

Step 2 (you). Calculate the SE and the margin of error.

Step 3 (you). Write the 95% interval for \(\delta\) and state, without any further computation, the 5% two-sided test decision.

Check your answer

\(\operatorname{SE}=10/\sqrt{25}=2\); margin of error \(2.06\times 2=4.13\). The interval is \(2\pm 4.13=(-2.13,\,6.13)\). Zero lies inside, so the 5% two-sided test does not reject \(H_0:\delta=0\): the data are compatible with a zero gap — and with gaps up to about 6 points. (For completeness, \(t=2/2=1.00\), \(p\approx 0.33\).)

Practice (10 minutes)

  1. A sample has \(n=49\), \(\bar x=110\), and \(s=14\). Define \(\delta=\mu-100\). Construct a 95% interval for \(\delta\) (use \(t_{0.975,48}\approx 2.01\)).
  2. Test \(H_0:\delta=0\) against \(H_1:\delta\ne0\) at 5%. Report the statistic, degrees of freedom, approximate p-value, decision, and a contextual conclusion.
  3. A coefficient-like estimate is 0.42 percentage points per €100,000, with SE 0.18 and \(df=120\). Test a two-sided zero null and construct a 95% interval (use \(t_{0.975,120}\approx 1.98\)).
  4. Explain why a statistically significant mean gap need not be practically important or causal.
Check your answers
  1. The estimated gap is \(110-100=10\), its SE is \(14/\sqrt{49}=2\), and the interval is \(10\pm 2.01(2)=(5.98,\,14.02)\).
  2. \(t=10/2=5.00\), \(df=48\), two-sided p-value below 0.001. Reject \(H_0\): the data provide strong evidence that the population mean exceeds 100 — plausibly by 6 to 14 points.
  3. \(t=0.42/0.18\approx 2.33\), two-sided p-value about 0.021. The 95% interval is \(0.42\pm1.98(0.18)=(0.064,\,0.776)\) percentage points per €100,000. Reject the zero null at 5%.
  4. Statistical significance concerns precision relative to zero. Practical importance depends on the magnitude and the decision context, and causality requires an appropriate design and assumptions — neither is delivered by a small p-value.

Rewrite the forbidden sentence (5 minutes)

Rewrite this claim correctly: “There is a 95% chance that the population mean is in my interval \((11.23, 14.29)\).”

Check a model answer

“The interval \((11.23, 14.29)\) was produced by a procedure that captures the fixed population mean in about 95% of repeated valid samples.” The parameter is fixed; it is the interval endpoints that vary across samples, as in Figure 12.3.

Worked exam interpretation (10 minutes)

Question (Mock Exam Q4 pattern). From a frame of 1,000 accelerator applicants, a simple random sample of 64 was selected. prep_hours is hours spent preparing the application; \(\mu\) is the frame mean. The sample has \(\bar x=18.4\) and \(s=8.0\). R produced:

One Sample t-test

data: prep_hours
t = 2.400, df = 63, p-value = 0.0193
alternative hypothesis: true mean is not equal to 16
95 percent confidence interval:
 16.40 20.40
sample estimate:
mean of x = 18.40

Verify the SE and the t statistic, explain the degrees of freedom, interpret the p-value and the interval, and give the 5% decision.

Check a model answer

\(\operatorname{SE}=8/\sqrt{64}=1\) hour, so \(t=(18.4-16)/1=2.4\), matching the output. \(df=64-1=63\) because the deviations from the estimated sample mean must sum to zero, leaving 63 free. If the frame mean were 16 hours, a sample mean at least 2.4 SEs from 16 in either direction would occur in about 1.9% of random samples; since \(0.0193<0.05\), reject \(H_0:\mu=16\). The interval \((16.40, 20.40)\) gives the plausible range for \(\mu\) — it excludes 16, agreeing with the test — and was produced by a procedure with about 95% coverage across repeated samples. The claim concerns the 1,000-applicant frame, not applicants in general.
