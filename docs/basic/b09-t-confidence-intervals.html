Lecture 9: The t distribution and confidence intervals

By the end of Lecture 9, you can

  • explain why estimating the population SD leads to a t distribution with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom, and use its critical values
  • construct a t confidence interval for a population mean
  • express comparison with a benchmark as a parameter equal to zero
  • state a two-sided null and alternative hypothesis
  • calculate a t statistic and say what it measures
  • state correctly what “95% confidence” is a property of.

Must know

Be able to do yourself:

  • build a 95% interval by hand from the mean, the standard deviation, the sample size, and a supplied t value
  • write \(H_0\) and \(H_1\) for a gap from a benchmark
  • calculate a t statistic and say what it measures
  • say what “95%” refers to.

Enough to recognise:

  • the t curves for different degrees of freedom
  • qt().

Driving question

Suppose an advisor tells founders that pitches typically offer investors about 15 percent equity. Our data can check such a benchmark, if we take the variation between samples into account.

Is the mean equity offered (equity_offered_pct) across all recorded Shark Tank pitches really different from 15 percent?

Before class

Total preparation time: about 30 minutes.

Minimum path: the video preparation and the prediction. Full path: everything in this section.

Video preparation (20 minutes)
Watch Confidence Intervals, Confidence Intervals When the Variance Is Unknown, and Introduction to Hypothesis Testing. Focus on repeated-sampling coverage, the t reference distribution, and the role of the null value. All links are also listed in the External Video Guide.

Reactivate (5 minutes). Let \(X\) be the equity offered in a pitch (equity_offered_pct, in percentage points) and define the equity gap

\[ G = X - 15. \]

Write two-sided hypotheses for the question: “Does the population mean of the equity offered differ from 15 percentage points?”

Check your preparation

Let \(\delta=E[G]=\mu_X-15\). Then

\[ H_0:\delta=0 \qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_1:\delta\ne0. \]

This is equivalent to testing \(H_0:\mu_X=15\) against \(H_1:\mu_X\ne15\). Writing the gap as \(\delta\) turns the question into “is the difference zero?”. Regression coefficients are tested in the same way later.

Predict (5 minutes). Answer the one-click Wooclap question posted on Canvas before you come to class: in a real sample, the unknown population SD \(\sigma\) must be replaced by the sample SD \(S\). Should that substitution make a 95% interval wider, narrower, or leave it unchanged? Commit to an answer. You find the answer in the lecture material.

If intervals, standardisation, or degrees of freedom feel rusty, review Mathematics Refresher: intervals, standardisation, and degrees of freedom.

Lecture material

Lecture 8 gave a sample mean and its standard error. This section turns those two numbers into a range of plausible values for the parameter, the confidence interval. It ends with the t statistic, which measures how far an estimate lies from a benchmark. Lecture 10 uses it to reach a decision.

Start with a founder’s question

Suppose an advisor tells you that founders on the show offer about 15% of their company. You have looked at 100 pitches, chosen by chance, and their average equity offered (equity_offered_pct) is 12.76%. Is the advisor wrong? Or is 12.76 simply what 100 pitches can give by chance when the true average is 15? R answers with one command:

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
set.seed(123)

pitch_sample <- sharks[sample_rows, ]
t.test(pitch_sample$equity_offered_pct, mu = 15)

From a benchmark to a zero contrast

The driving question compares a population mean to the benchmark 15. As in the preparation exercise, subtract the benchmark and study the gap

\[ G = X - 15, \qquad \delta = E[G] = \mu_X - 15. \]

“Is the mean equity offered different from 15?” becomes “is \(\delta\) different from zero?” A difference of this kind is called a contrast. Every benchmark question can be rewritten as a question about a contrast being zero. Lecture 10 tests a gap between proportions and a regression slope in this way, and names the difference between two groups as a further case.

The price of estimating σ: the t distribution

Lecture 8 gave the standardised sample mean

\[ Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}, \]

which is approximately standard normal by the central limit theorem (CLT). But this \(Z\) contains the population SD \(\sigma\), and in a real study nobody knows \(\sigma\). In practice we replace it by the sample SD \(S\). But \(S\) is itself random. It changes from sample to sample, and in a small sample it can be far from \(\sigma\). A mean that is standardised with an estimated spread therefore varies more than \(Z\). For independent observations from a normal population,

\[ T=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n} \sim t_{n-1}. \]

Definition

t distribution and degrees of freedom. The t distribution describes a sample mean that is standardised with the sample SD \(S\) in place of \(\sigma\). It is symmetric around zero and has heavier tails than the standard normal. Its shape depends on one number, the degrees of freedom (\(df\)). For one sample mean, \(df=n-1\).

Heavier tails mean that values far from zero are more likely than under the standard normal. The reason is that \(S\) sometimes underestimates the true spread. The degrees of freedom say how much heavier the tails are:

  • few degrees of freedom mean that \(S\) is an imprecise estimate of \(\sigma\), so the tails are heavier
  • more degrees of freedom mean more information about spread
  • as the degrees of freedom increase, the t distribution approaches the standard normal \(N(0,1)\).

For one sample mean, \(df=n-1\). This is the same \(n-1\) as in Lecture 7. Once the sample mean is estimated, the \(n\) deviations must sum to zero, so only \(n-1\) of them vary freely when spread is estimated.

The difference in Figure 12.1 is most visible in the tails. The central 95% of the standard normal lies between \(-1.96\) and \(1.96\) (Lecture 6). A t curve must reach further out to enclose its central 95%. The point where the central 95% ends is the 0.975 quantile of the t distribution. It is written \(t_{0.975,\,df}\) and computed in R with qt(0.975, df). This number is called the 95% critical value, or the multiplier. For small \(df\) it is clearly larger than 1.96.

Check: which is larger, \(t_{0.975,11}\) or \(t_{0.975,99}\)?

\(t_{0.975,11}\approx 2.20\) is larger than \(t_{0.975,99}\approx 1.98\). With only 11 degrees of freedom, \(S\) is an imprecise estimate of \(\sigma\), so the t distribution has heavier tails and its 97.5% quantile sits further from zero. At \(df=99\) the critical value is already close to the normal 1.96. With a large sample, the t and normal answers are almost the same. Verify in R: qt(0.975, df = 11) and qt(0.975, df = 99).

The interval recipe

A sample can be used in three ways. A point estimate gives one number for the parameter, such as the sample mean 12.76. An interval estimate gives a range of plausible values. A test answers a yes-or-no question about one value of the parameter. Lectures 7 and 8 gave point estimates. This lecture builds the interval estimate and prepares the test.

A confidence interval combines the estimate with its uncertainty. The general form is

\[ \text{estimate} \ \pm\ \text{critical value}\times\text{standard error}. \]

Definition

Confidence interval. A range of plausible values for a parameter, calculated from the sample as estimate \(\pm\) critical value \(\times\) standard error. A 95% confidence interval is built by a method that captures the parameter in about 95% of random samples.

Definition

Margin of error. The part after the \(\pm\) sign: critical value \(\times\) standard error. It is half the width of the confidence interval.

A confidence interval becomes:

  • wider when the confidence level increases (a larger critical value)
  • wider when observations are more variable (a larger SE)
  • narrower when the sample size increases, all else equal.

For an independent sample from a normal population with unknown SD, or approximately for large \(n\),

\[ \bar X\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\frac{S}{\sqrt n} \]

is a \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(\mu\). Here \(\alpha\) is one minus the confidence level. A 95% interval has \(\alpha=0.05\), and the multiplier \(t_{0.975,\,n-1}\) leaves \(\alpha/2=0.025\) in each tail. The words “approximately for large \(n\)” refer to the CLT: for a large independent sample the interval is a reasonable approximation even when the population is not normal. The same recipe applied to the gap variable \(G\) gives an interval for \(\delta\).

The margin of error is the precision of the estimate at the stated confidence level. A smaller margin means a more precise estimate.

This also answers the prediction question from the preparation. Replacing \(\sigma\) with \(S\) makes the interval wider on average, because the t multiplier is larger than 1.96. The extra width is the price of estimating one more quantity from the same data.

In the exam, the multiplier you need is printed in the question or on the formula sheet. For a value of the degrees of freedom that is not on the sheet, use the closest one listed.

Example 12.1 (Worked example: a 95% interval by hand) This is an invented example. A random sample of \(n=64\) ventures reports a mean customer satisfaction score of \(\bar x = 80\) points, with sample SD \(s = 16\) points. The industry benchmark is 75 points. Construct a 95% confidence interval for \(\delta=\mu-75\), step by step.

  • Estimate. The estimated gap is the sample mean minus the benchmark: \(\widehat\delta = 80 - 75 = 5\).
  • Standard error. \(\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}=s/\sqrt n = 16/\sqrt{64}\). The square root of 64 is 8, so \(\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}=16/8=2\).
  • Degrees of freedom and critical value. \(df=n-1=63\), and \(t_{0.975,63}\approx 2.00\). In R it is qt(0.975, df = 63), about 1.998. The closest value on the formula sheet is the one for \(df=60\), which is 2.000.
  • Margin of error. Critical value times SE: \(2.00\times 2 = 4\).
  • Interval. Estimate plus and minus the margin: \(5 - 4 = 1\) and \(5 + 4 = 9\), so the 95% interval for \(\delta\) is \((1,\,9)\).

Every plausible value of the gap is positive. The data are consistent with a population mean between 1 and 9 points above the benchmark. Adding 75 back to both endpoints gives the equivalent interval \((76,\,84)\) for \(\mu\) itself. The gap form and the mean form are the same statement.

Check conditions before calculating

The formula is valid only when conditions on the sampling design and on the shape of the data hold. R does not check them for you. Before any interval or test, go through this checklist:

  1. Random selection. Were the rows selected randomly from a defined frame? In the Shark Tank case below they were: the sample of 100 is drawn at random (seed 123) from the 1,441 recorded pitches.
  2. The 10% condition. Sampling without replacement creates weak dependence, but \(100/1441\approx 0.07 < 0.10\), so the usual independent-sample SE is a reasonable approximation.
  3. Shape and sample size. The equity offered (equity_offered_pct) is right-skewed. With \(n=100\) the t approximation for the sample mean is still reasonable. For a very small sample it would not be. Also check for data errors and for single observations that dominate the mean.

These conditions support conclusions about the recorded-pitch frame. They do not make televised pitches representative of all entrepreneurs. The caveat box at the end of this section returns to this point.

Try it: is the average order above break-even?

Try this with your neighbour first. This is an invented example. A venture needs an average order of 20 euros to break even. A random sample of \(n=36\) orders has a mean of 21.50 euros and a standard deviation of 6 euros. Supplied value: \(t_{0.975,\,35}=2.030\).

Step 1 (given). The estimated standard error is \(\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}=6/\sqrt{36}=1\) euro.

Step 2 (you). Calculate the margin of error and the 95% interval.

Step 3 (you). Is 20 euros a plausible value for the mean order? What would you tell the founder?

Check your answer

The margin is \(2.030\times1=2.03\) euros, so the interval runs from 19.47 to 23.53 euros. The break-even value of 20 euros lies inside the interval. The sample mean is above break-even, but the data cannot rule out that the true mean is below it. Advice: collect more orders before any decision that depends on being above break-even. With four times as many orders the margin would be about half as wide.

The Shark Tank case, step by step

Now apply the recipe to the Shark Tank data. The code draws the same random sample of 100 pitches as in Lecture 7 and builds every object step by step:

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
set.seed(123)
sample_rows <- sample(seq_len(nrow(sharks)), size = 100, replace = FALSE)
pitch_sample <- sharks[sample_rows, ]

benchmark <- 15
pitch_sample$equity_gap_pp <-
  pitch_sample$equity_offered_pct - benchmark

gap_bar <- mean(pitch_sample$equity_gap_pp)   # estimated gap
gap_sd  <- sd(pitch_sample$equity_gap_pp)     # sample SD of the gap
n       <- nrow(pitch_sample)
df      <- n - 1
gap_se  <- gap_sd / sqrt(n)                   # standard error

c(
  sample_mean_equity = mean(pitch_sample$equity_offered_pct),
  sample_mean_gap = gap_bar,
  sample_sd_gap = gap_sd,
  standard_error_gap = gap_se,
  degrees_of_freedom = df
)

That is about one seventh of the benchmark. Subtracting the constant 15 changes the mean but not the spread, as the shift rule of Lecture 3 says. So gap_sd equals the sample SD of the equity offered itself, 7.71 points. The standard error is \(7.71/\sqrt{100}=0.77\) points.

Interpret 95% confidence correctly

The parameter is fixed. Interval endpoints vary from sample to sample. “95%” describes the procedure: if the same valid recipe were repeated on many random samples, about 95% of the resulting intervals would contain the parameter.

The simulation in Figure 12.2 repeats the random sample and the interval calculation 100 times. The fixed target is the mean equity offered (equity_offered_pct) among all 1,441 recorded pitches, 13.21 points.

See the R code for this figure 
library(ggplot2)

sharks <- read.csv("data/shark_tank_teaching.csv", stringsAsFactors = FALSE)
set.seed(209)
B_coverage <- 100
n_coverage <- 100
target_mean <- mean(sharks$equity_offered_pct)
ci_lower <- ci_upper <- ci_centre <- numeric(B_coverage)

for (i in seq_len(B_coverage)) {
  repeated_sample <- sample(
    sharks$equity_offered_pct,
    size = n_coverage,
    replace = FALSE
  )
  repeated_mean <- mean(repeated_sample)
  repeated_se <- sd(repeated_sample) / sqrt(n_coverage)
  repeated_critical <- qt(0.975, df = n_coverage - 1)
  ci_centre[i] <- repeated_mean
  ci_lower[i] <- repeated_mean - repeated_critical * repeated_se
  ci_upper[i] <- repeated_mean + repeated_critical * repeated_se
}

intervals <- data.frame(
  lower = ci_lower, centre = ci_centre, upper = ci_upper,
  covers = ifelse(ci_lower <= target_mean & ci_upper >= target_mean,
                  "contains the target mean", "misses the target mean")
)
intervals <- intervals[order(intervals$centre), ]
intervals$position <- seq_len(B_coverage)

ggplot(intervals, aes(y = position, colour = covers)) +
  geom_segment(aes(x = lower, xend = upper, yend = position),
               linewidth = 0.7) +
  geom_point(aes(x = centre), size = 0.9) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = target_mean, linetype = "dashed",
             colour = "#C98B2E", linewidth = 0.7) +
  annotate("text", x = target_mean, y = B_coverage + 3.5,
           label = "mu == 13.21", parse = TRUE,
           colour = "#17324D", size = 3.4, hjust = -0.1) +
  scale_colour_manual(values = c("contains the target mean" = "#009E73",
                                 "misses the target mean" = "#D55E00")) +
  scale_y_continuous(breaks = c(1, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100)) +
  labs(x = "Mean equity offered (%)",
       y = "Repeated sample (ordered by estimate)",
       colour = NULL) +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 13) +
  theme(
    panel.grid.minor = element_blank(),
    panel.grid.major.y = element_blank(),
    plot.caption = element_text(colour = "#5B6976"),
    axis.title = element_text(colour = "#17324D"),
    legend.position = "top"

Each horizontal segment in Figure 12.2 is one 95% confidence interval. Its dot is that sample’s mean. The gold dashed vertical line is the fixed target mean, 13.21. In this run (seed 209), 96 of 100 intervals cross the line. A new run need not give exactly 95, but across many repetitions the proportion approaches 95% when the procedure’s conditions hold.

The following sentence may look natural, but it is wrong:

“There is a 95% probability that the population mean is in my interval.”

Before the sample is drawn, the endpoints of the interval are random, and the 95% applies to them. After the sample is drawn, the interval \((11.23, 14.29)\) consists of two fixed numbers. It either contains the fixed parameter or it does not, and no probability is left. The correct sentence is:

“This interval was produced by a procedure that captures the fixed population mean in about 95% of repeated valid samples.”

From interval to test: the hypotheses and the t statistic

The interval shows every plausible value. A test answers one question about one value: is the gap zero? A test starts from two hypotheses:

\[ H_0:\delta=0 \qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_1:\delta\ne0. \]

Definition

Null hypothesis and alternative hypothesis. The null hypothesis \(H_0\) states that the parameter equals the benchmark value, here \(\delta=0\). The alternative hypothesis \(H_1\) states that it does not. A two-sided alternative, \(\delta\ne0\), allows a difference in either direction.

Here the null hypothesis says that the population mean of the equity offered does not differ from the benchmark. State both hypotheses from the research question before you look at the sample estimate. Alternatives that allow only one direction (one-sided alternatives) also exist. They are optional reading in Lecture 10 and are not examined.

The t statistic standardises the estimated gap with its SE. Its general form is the estimate minus the null value, divided by the standard error:

\[ T= \frac{\widehat\delta-0} {\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\widehat\delta)} = \frac{\bar G}{S_G/\sqrt n}. \]

First by hand, with the numbers calculated above. The estimate is \(-2.24\) and its SE is \(0.77\), so

\[ t=\frac{-2.24}{0.77}\approx -2.91. \]

The sign gives the direction: the estimate is below the benchmark. The absolute value gives the distance from zero in standard-error units, here almost three SEs. Now the same calculation in R:

t_value <- gap_bar / gap_se

c(
  estimate = gap_bar,
  standard_error = gap_se,
  t_statistic = t_value,
  degrees_of_freedom = df
)
          estimate     standard_error        t_statistic degrees_of_freedom 
        -2.2400000          0.7707887         -2.9061144         99.0000000

A t statistic of \(-2.91\) says that the estimate lies almost three standard errors below zero. Lecture 10 measures how surprising such a value would be if the null hypothesis were true. That measure is called the p-value.

What an interval can and cannot tell you

  • Precision is not importance. An interval that excludes 15 says the gap is measured well, not that 2.24 points matter for a decision.
  • An interval is not a claim about cause. Nothing here says why offers sit below 15 points.
  • A narrow interval repairs nothing about selection. It describes the recorded pitches, not founders in general.

What you can do with it: read both ends of the interval as possible truths, and ask whether your decision would be the same at each end.

Answering the driving question

For the recorded-pitch frame, the evidence points to yes. The random sample of 100 pitches gives an estimated gap of \(-2.24\) percentage points with SE \(0.77\). The 95% interval for the mean equity offered is \((11.23, 14.29)\), which excludes 15. The t statistic of \(-2.91\) puts the estimate almost three standard errors below the benchmark. Lecture 10 turns that distance into a p-value and a formal decision. Whether the gap matters commercially, and what caused it, are separate questions that no interval can answer.

A founder’s decision (invented example)

From 25 orders, the average order value is 9.60 euros with a standard deviation of 2 euros. You need 9 euros per order to break even on a kitchen lease. The 95% interval is 9.60 ± 2.064 × 0.40, from 8.77 to 10.43 euros. The break-even value of 9 euros lies inside the interval. The decision: do not sign yet. With 100 orders the standard error falls to 0.20. If the mean stays at 9.60, the interval then runs from 9.20 to 10.00 and excludes 9. You collect orders for three more weeks and decide then.

Next: We can now report an estimate with an interval, and we have measured how far the estimate lies from a benchmark in standard-error units. Lecture 10 turns that distance into a p-value and a decision. It names the two ways a decision can be wrong and shows what makes wrong decisions rarer. It ends with a first look at a regression table, which reports the same five items: estimate, SE, t, p-value, and interval.

Check your answers
  1. \(S\) is itself estimated and varies from sample to sample. The t distribution’s heavier tails represent that extra uncertainty.

  3. Estimate, critical value, and standard error.
  4. The null hypothesis states that the population gap \(\delta\) is zero. The alternative allows a non-zero gap in either direction.
  5. The estimate lies 2.91 standard errors below the null value of zero.
  6. The procedure: about 95% of intervals built this way from repeated valid samples contain the fixed parameter.

Check your answer

Practice (10 minutes)

The numbers in tasks 1 to 3 are invented.

  1. A random sample has \(n=49\), \(\bar x=110\), and \(s=14\). Define \(\delta=\mu-100\). Construct a 95% interval for \(\delta\) (use \(t_{0.975,48}\approx 2.01\)).
  2. For the sample of task 1, state \(H_0\) and \(H_1\) for a two-sided test of a zero gap. Calculate the t statistic and its degrees of freedom.
  3. An estimate is 0.42 percentage points per €100,000, with SE 0.18 and \(df=120\). Calculate the t statistic and construct a 95% interval (use \(t_{0.975,120}\approx 1.98\)).
  4. Explain why an interval that excludes zero does not show that a gap is important in practice, or that it has a particular cause.
Check your answers
  1. The estimated gap is \(110-100=10\), its SE is \(14/\sqrt{49}=2\), and the interval is \(10\pm 2.01(2)=(5.98,\,14.02)\).
  2. \(H_0:\delta=0\) against \(H_1:\delta\ne0\). The t statistic is \(t=10/2=5.00\) with \(df=48\), so the estimate lies five standard errors above zero. The interval from task 1 lies entirely above zero. Plausible gaps run from about 6 to 14 points.
  3. \(t=0.42/0.18\approx 2.33\). The 95% interval is \(0.42\pm1.98(0.18)=(0.064,\,0.776)\) percentage points per €100,000, which excludes zero.
  4. An interval that excludes zero says that the estimate is precise enough to tell the gap from zero. Whether the gap is important depends on its size and on the decision. A causal claim needs a suitable study design. A narrow interval gives neither.
