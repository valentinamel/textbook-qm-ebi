Tutorial 1: Evidence and probability foundations

By the end of Tutorial 1, you can

  • classify variables, and tell sampling variability and selection bias apart
  • distinguish data, events, random variables, and distributions
  • apply probability rules to the Shark Tank record-selection experiment
  • calculate and interpret a Bernoulli PMF, CDF, and expectation
  • calculate a variance and standard deviation from a small PMF
  • calculate the expected value and standard deviation of a shifted and rescaled variable \(aX+b\)
  • calculate a mean, a standard deviation, the quartiles, and the interquartile range from a frequency table
  • describe a random experiment by its sample space, and write events in set notation
  • calculate probabilities from a CDF table and recover the PMF from the CDF
  • compare two options by their expected values and standard deviations
  • write a short interpretation of supplied R output
  • find and correct the errors in an AI answer
  • read a histogram by hand and find the interval that contains the median.

Tutorials are exam preparation sessions. Every task below follows the format of the final exam. You first try each task in pairs. You may use AI for this, following Attempt, Ask, Verify, Explain. Then the class discusses the solution together.

Before class

Total preparation time: about 15 minutes.

Bring your after-class work from Lectures 1 to 3. Without looking at the chapters again, complete these definitions:

  1. An observational unit is …
  2. An event is …
  3. A random variable is …
  4. A PMF is …
  5. The variance of a random variable averages …
  6. The lower quartile \(Q_1\) and the upper quartile \(Q_3\) are …

Use the formula sheet up to and including Lecture 3.

Review Mathematics Refresher: sets and event notation and summation notation if the notation is difficult for you.

Check your preparation
  1. An observational unit is the unit that one row of the data describes.
  2. An event is a specified subset of the sample space.
  3. A random variable is a numerical measurement of the outcome: a fixed rule that turns each outcome into a number.
  4. A PMF assigns probability \(P(X=x)\) to each value in the support of a discrete random variable. Its masses are non-negative and sum to 1.
  5. Variance averages squared deviations from the variable’s mean: \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=E\!\left[(X-E[X])^2\right]\).
  6. At least one quarter of the observations are at or below \(Q_1\), and at least three quarters are at or above it. At least three quarters of the observations are at or below \(Q_3\), and at least one quarter are at or above it. The difference \(Q_3-Q_1\) is the interquartile range.

Task 1: Data and measurement (5 minutes)

Shark Tank data R reports:

                    rows                  columns                    deals 
            1441.0000000               19.0000000              882.0000000 
         deal_proportion median_description_words 
               0.6120749                5.0000000
  1. What is the observational unit?
  2. Verify the deal proportion from 882 deals and 559 non-deals.
  3. Explain why the mean of deal_on_show is a proportion.
  4. Evaluate: “The data contain 1,441 successful companies.”
  5. What exactly does description_words measure?
Check your answer

One row is one recorded televised pitch. There are \(882+559=1441\) records and \(882/1441\approx0.612\), so 61.2% reached an on-air deal. The mean of a 0/1 indicator equals the share coded 1 because its sum counts the 1s. “Successful companies” is incorrect: the outcome is a deal made on the show, not completed funding or later venture performance. Word count describes the written text in the source dataset, not the spoken pitch.

Task 2: Variables and samples (6 minutes)

An invented example. A coffee-bar start-up records, for each of 150 orders: order_id, drink (espresso, latte, or tea), cup_size (small, medium, or large), items (the number of items), and amount_eur (the amount paid, in euros).

  1. Classify each variable as categorical, ordinal, discrete, continuous, or an identifier.
  2. The founder asks 150 loyalty-card holders whether they would pay more for oat milk. Is the main problem sampling variability or selection bias? Explain in two sentences.
Check your answer
  1. order_id is an identifier, drink is categorical, cup_size is ordinal, items is discrete, and amount_eur is continuous.
  2. The main problem is selection bias. Loyalty-card holders are regular customers who like the bar, so they may be more willing to pay extra than other customers. Asking more card holders would not fix this, because the sample was not drawn at random from all customers.

Task 3: Events and probability rules (8 minutes)

Shark Tank data The experiment selects one of the 1,441 records at random, each with the same probability. The two events are defined exactly as in Lecture 2:

D <- sharks$deal_on_show == 1   # on-air deal
L <- sharks$season >= 9         # late season: Seasons 9-16

R reports:

      P_D       P_L P_D_and_L 
0.6120749 0.5093685 0.3504511
  1. Define \(\Omega\), and explain the difference between an outcome and the event \(D\).
  2. Translate \(D^c\), \(D\cap L\), and \(D\cup L\) into words.
  3. Calculate \(P(D^c)\).
  4. Calculate \(P(D\cup L)\) using the addition rule.
  5. Explain why these probabilities do not automatically apply to a future US entrepreneur.
Check your answer

The sample space is the set of 1,441 complete pitch records. One selected record is an outcome, while \(D\) is the subset of 882 deal records. The complement is no on-air deal, the intersection is a late-season deal, and the union is late-season, deal, or both. \(P(D^c)=1-882/1441=559/1441\approx0.388\). The union is \(P(D)+P(L)-P(D\cap L)\approx0.771\). To use these probabilities for a future entrepreneur, you need a defined target population and a comparable selection, time period, and outcome.

Task 4: PMF, CDF, and expectation (8 minutes)

Shark Tank data Let \(X=1\) for an on-air deal and \(X=0\) otherwise. Use \(p=882/1441\).

  1. Write the PMF and verify that its masses sum to 1.
  2. Write the full piecewise CDF \(F_X(a)\).
  3. Calculate \(E[X]\) from the PMF.
  4. Explain why \(E[X]\) equals the observed proportion of 1s.
  5. Explain the difference between the random variable \(X\) and a realised value \(x=1\).
Check your answer

\[ p_X(0)=1-p,\qquad p_X(1)=p, \]

and the masses sum to \(1-p+p=1\).

\[ F_X(a)= \begin{cases} 0,&a<0,\\ 1-p,&0\le a<1,\\ 1,&a\ge1. \end{cases} \]

The expectation is \(E[X]=0(1-p)+1p=p\approx0.612\). Because the indicator contains 882 ones among 1,441 observations, its mean is also \(882/1441\). \(X\) is the random variable: a numerical measurement of the outcome, a fixed rule that turns each outcome into a number (1 for a deal, 0 for no deal). \(x=1\) is one realised value, the number obtained when the selected pitch has a deal.

Task 5: Variance from a small PMF (10 minutes)

An invented example. A venture studio models \(N\), the number of investors who make an offer to one of its startups, with the PMF

\(n\) 0 1 2
\(p_N(n)\) 0.5 0.3 0.2
  1. Calculate \(E[N]\).
  2. Calculate \(\operatorname{Var}(N)\) from the definition \(E\!\left[(N-E[N])^2\right]\).
  3. Verify the result with the computational form \(E[N^2]-\left(E[N]\right)^2\).
  4. Report the standard deviation with its unit, and state in one sentence what it measures here.
  5. Each offer costs the studio 3 hours of meetings, and every startup needs 2 hours of preparation. The time spent on one startup is therefore \(T=3N+2\) hours. Calculate \(E[T]\) and \(\operatorname{SD}(T)\).
Check a model answer

\(E[N]=0(0.5)+1(0.3)+2(0.2)=0.7\) offers.

Definition form: \[ \operatorname{Var}(N)=(0-0.7)^2(0.5)+(1-0.7)^2(0.3)+(2-0.7)^2(0.2) =0.245+0.027+0.338=0.61. \]

Computational form: \(E[N^2]=0^2(0.5)+1^2(0.3)+2^2(0.2)=1.1\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(N)=1.1-0.7^2=1.1-0.49=0.61\). The two methods agree.

The standard deviation is \(\sqrt{0.61}\approx0.78\) offers. It measures the typical distance of the realised number of offers from the mean of 0.7, in the original unit (offers), whereas the variance is in squared offers.

Time per startup: use the rules for \(aX+b\) with \(a=3\) and \(b=2\). \(E[T]=3E[N]+2=3\times0.7+2=4.1\) hours. \(\operatorname{SD}(T)=|3|\operatorname{SD}(N)\approx3\times0.78=2.34\) hours, more than half of the expected time. The 2 hours of preparation raise the expected time and leave the spread unchanged.

Task 6: Check the AI (5 minutes)

You asked an AI assistant about the number of offers \(N\) in Task 5. It answered:

“E[N] = 0.7, so most startups get 0.7 offers. The variance is 0.61, so the number of offers typically differs from 0.7 by 0.61 offers.”

Check the answer sentence by sentence. Find what is wrong and write the corrected answer.

Check your answer

The first sentence is wrong. No startup gets 0.7 offers, because \(N\) is a count with the values 0, 1, and 2. The expected value is a probability-weighted average, not the most likely value. The most likely number is 0 offers, with probability 0.5. The second sentence is also wrong. The variance 0.61 is in squared offers. The typical distance from 0.7 is the standard deviation, \(\sqrt{0.61}\approx0.78\) offers. Corrected answer: on average a startup gets 0.7 offers, half of the startups get no offer, and the number of offers typically differs from 0.7 by about 0.78 offers.

Task 7: Read a histogram by hand (7 minutes)

An invented example. A venture records the delivery times of 120 orders. A histogram groups them into intervals of 10 minutes, and each bar includes its upper limit. The bar counts, from the first interval (0 to 10 minutes) to the fifth (above 40, up to 50 minutes), are 18, 46, 32, 16, and 8.

  1. What does one observation represent, and what is on each axis?
  2. Describe the tallest bar in one full sentence.
  3. Which interval contains the median? Show the cumulative counts.
  4. Describe the shape, and state one thing the histogram cannot tell you.
Check your answer

One observation is one order. The horizontal axis is the delivery time in minutes and the vertical axis is the number of orders. The tallest bar: 46 orders took more than 10 and at most 20 minutes. With 120 orders the median lies between the 60th and the 61st ordered value. The cumulative counts are 18 and then 18 + 46 = 64, so both values lie in the second interval: the median delivery time is above 10 and at most 20 minutes. The distribution is right-skewed: most orders arrive within 30 minutes and a few take much longer. The histogram cannot say which orders were late or why, and it hides the exact times inside each interval.

Task 8: Mean, spread, and quartiles from a frequency table (12 minutes)

An invented example. A founder runs an escape room. Groups of 2 to 6 persons book the room for one hour. The table shows the group size of the 40 bookings of last week.

Group size (persons) 2 3 4 5 6
Number of bookings 4 12 10 8 6

Give every booking the same weight, \(1/40\). This is the empirical distribution of Lecture 3, so the relative frequencies work like a PMF.

  1. State what one observation is. Calculate the relative frequency of each group size and check that the five values sum to 1.
  2. Calculate the mean group size.
  3. Calculate the variance and the standard deviation of this empirical distribution. Use the variance formula of Lecture 3 with the relative frequencies as weights. This version divides by \(n\). State the unit. Then calculate the sample standard deviation \(s\) of Lecture 3, which divides by \(n-1\).
  4. Write down the cumulative counts and find the median.
  5. Find the lower quartile \(Q_1\) and the upper quartile \(Q_3\) with cumulative counts, as for the median in Lecture 1. Add the counts from the left until the running total first reaches one quarter of the observations. The group size at which this happens is \(Q_1\). Do the same with three quarters for \(Q_3\). Then calculate the interquartile range and interpret it in one sentence.
  6. Compare the mean with the median. What does the comparison say about the shape of the distribution?

For these 40 values, R’s quantile() reports the same median and the same quartiles as the rules in parts 4 and 5.

Check your answer
  1. One observation is one booking. The relative frequencies are \(4/40=0.10\), \(12/40=0.30\), \(10/40=0.25\), \(8/40=0.20\), and \(6/40=0.15\). Their sum is 1.
  2. The mean is \(2(0.10)+3(0.30)+4(0.25)+5(0.20)+6(0.15)=0.20+0.90+1.00+1.00+0.90=4.0\) persons. The counts give the same result: \((2\times4+3\times12+4\times10+5\times8+6\times6)/40=160/40=4.0\).
  3. The variance is \[ (2-4)^2(0.10)+(3-4)^2(0.30)+(4-4)^2(0.25)+(5-4)^2(0.20)+(6-4)^2(0.15) \] \[ =0.40+0.30+0+0.20+0.60=1.50. \] The standard deviation is \(\sqrt{1.50}\approx1.22\) persons. A booking typically differs from the mean of 4 persons by about 1.2 persons, which is about 31% of the mean. This variance divides by \(n=40\). The sum of the squared deviations is \(40\times1.50=60\). The sample variance divides it by \(n-1=39\): \(s^2=60/39\approx1.54\) and \(s\approx1.24\) persons, the value of R’s sd().
  4. The cumulative counts are 4, 16, 26, 34, and 40. With 40 observations the median is the average of the 20th and the 21st ordered value. The ordered values 17 to 26 all have group size 4, so the median is 4 persons.
  5. One quarter of 40 is 10. The running total first reaches 10 at group size 3, because \(16\ge10\), so \(Q_1=3\). Three quarters of 40 is 30. The running total first reaches 30 at group size 5, because \(34\ge30\), so \(Q_3=5\). The interquartile range is \(5-3=2\) persons: the middle half of the bookings lies between 3 and 5 persons. Of the 40 bookings, 16 are at or below 3 persons. That is 40%, more than one quarter, which is why the definition of \(Q_1\) says “at least one quarter”.
  6. The mean and the median are both 4 persons, so the two agree. The distribution has no long tail on one side that pulls the mean away from the median.

Task 9: A random experiment, its sample space, and events (10 minutes, extra practice for home)

An invented example. A streetwear start-up sells one hoodie in five sizes: XS, S, M, L, and XL. The random experiment is this: the next order arrives and its size is recorded. The founders use the shares of last year’s orders as probabilities:

Size XS S M L XL
Probability 0.05 0.20 0.35 0.30 0.10

Two events matter for the founders. \(A\) is the event that the order is for a size up to M, that is XS, S, or M. \(B\) is the event that the order is for a standard size, that is S, M, or L. The start-up keeps the standard sizes in stock and makes XS and XL only after a customer orders them.

  1. Write down the sample space \(\Omega\). Is L an outcome or an event? What is \(\{\text{L}\}\)?
  2. Check that the table is a valid probability model.
  3. Write \(A\) and \(B\) in set notation, and calculate \(P(A)\) and \(P(B)\).
  4. Describe \(A^c\) and \(B^c\) in words and write them in set notation. Calculate their probabilities with the complement rule.
  5. Write \(A\cap B\) and \(A\cup B\) in set notation and in words. Calculate \(P(A\cup B)\) with the addition rule. Explain why \(P(A)+P(B)\) cannot be the answer.
  6. Which size is neither in \(A\) nor in \(B\)? Calculate its probability from part 5 with the complement rule, and check the result in the table.
  7. State one assumption that the founders make when they use these probabilities for the next order.
Check your answer
  1. \(\Omega=\{\text{XS},\text{S},\text{M},\text{L},\text{XL}\}\). L is an outcome: one complete possible result of the experiment. \(\{\text{L}\}\) is an event: the set that contains only this outcome.
  2. Every probability is at least 0, and \(0.05+0.20+0.35+0.30+0.10=1\), so \(P(\Omega)=1\). The five sizes are disjoint, so their probabilities may be added.
  3. \(A=\{\text{XS},\text{S},\text{M}\}\) and \(P(A)=0.05+0.20+0.35=0.60\). \(B=\{\text{S},\text{M},\text{L}\}\) and \(P(B)=0.20+0.35+0.30=0.85\).
  4. \(A^c=\{\text{L},\text{XL}\}\): the order is for a size above M. \(P(A^c)=1-0.60=0.40\). \(B^c=\{\text{XS},\text{XL}\}\): the order is for a size that is made only after an order. \(P(B^c)=1-0.85=0.15\). The table gives the same values: \(0.30+0.10=0.40\) and \(0.05+0.10=0.15\).
  5. \(A\cap B=\{\text{S},\text{M}\}\): the size is up to M and it is a standard size. Its probability is \(0.20+0.35=0.55\). \(A\cup B=\{\text{XS},\text{S},\text{M},\text{L}\}\): the size is up to M, or it is a standard size, or both. The addition rule gives \(P(A\cup B)=0.60+0.85-0.55=0.90\). The sum \(P(A)+P(B)=1.45\) is above 1, so it cannot be a probability. It counts the sizes S and M twice.
  6. Only XL. In symbols, \((A\cup B)^c=\{\text{XL}\}\), and the complement rule gives \(1-0.90=0.10\). The table shows the same value for XL.
  7. The probabilities are shares of last year’s orders. They describe the next order only if customers choose sizes in the same way as last year. A new group of customers or a new design of the hoodie could change the shares.

Verification in R:

size_prob <- c(XS = 0.05, S = 0.20, M = 0.35, L = 0.30, XL = 0.10)

Task 10: From a CDF to probabilities and to the PMF (10 minutes, extra practice for home)

An invented example. A founder rents out four cargo bikes to students who move house. Let \(X\) be the number of bikes that are rented out on a Saturday. Her model for \(X\) is given as a CDF:

Threshold \(a\) \(F_X(a)=P(X\le a)\)
\(a<0\) 0
\(0\le a<1\) 0.05
\(1\le a<2\) 0.20
\(2\le a<3\) 0.50
\(3\le a<4\) 0.80
\(a\ge4\) 1
  1. State the possible values of \(X\). How does the table show them?
  2. Read \(F_X(2)\), \(F_X(2.5)\), and \(F_X(6)\) from the table. Say in words what \(F_X(2)\) means.
  3. Calculate \(P(X>2)\), \(P(1<X\le3)\), and \(P(1\le X\le3)\).
  4. Recover the PMF of \(X\) from the CDF, and check that it is a valid PMF.
  5. Check your PMF in two ways. Calculate \(F_X(3)\) again as a running total, and calculate \(P(1\le X\le3)\) again by adding probability masses.
  6. Interpret \(p_X(4)\) in one sentence. State one thing that this number cannot tell the founder.
Check your answer

  1. \(X\) can take the values 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4. These are the thresholds at which the CDF jumps. Between two of these values the CDF stays flat, because no probability lies there.

  2. \(F_X(2)=0.50\): with probability 0.50, at most two bikes are rented out on a Saturday. \(F_X(2.5)=0.50\) as well, because \(X\) takes no value between 2 and 2.5. \(F_X(6)=1\), because \(X\) is never above 4.

  3. \(P(X>2)=1-F_X(2)=1-0.50=0.50\). \(P(1<X\le3)=F_X(3)-F_X(1)=0.80-0.20=0.60\). The event \(1\le X\le3\) includes the value 1. \(X\) takes whole numbers only, so this event is the same as \(0<X\le3\), and \(P(1\le X\le3)=F_X(3)-F_X(0)=0.80-0.05=0.75\).

  4. Each probability mass is the size of the jump of the CDF at that value: \(p_X(0)=0.05\), \(p_X(1)=0.20-0.05=0.15\), \(p_X(2)=0.50-0.20=0.30\), \(p_X(3)=0.80-0.50=0.30\), and \(p_X(4)=1-0.80=0.20\).

    \(x\) 0 1 2 3 4
    \(p_X(x)\) 0.05 0.15 0.30 0.30 0.20

    No mass is negative, and \(0.05+0.15+0.30+0.30+0.20=1\).

  5. \(F_X(3)=0.05+0.15+0.30+0.30=0.80\), as in the table. \(P(1\le X\le3)=0.15+0.30+0.30=0.75\), as in part 3.

  6. \(p_X(4)=0.20\): with probability 0.20, on about one Saturday in five, all four bikes are rented out. The number counts rented bikes. It cannot tell how many students asked for a bike on such a day, so it does not show how often a fifth bike would be rented.

Task 11: Two options, compared by expected value and spread (15 minutes)

An invented example. Two founders sell second-hand designer clothes online. For December they want to rent a pop-up store, and they can choose between two locations. Option A is a shop in a side street of the city centre. Option B is a booth at the Christmas market, where the rent is high and the number of visitors depends on the weather. \(X\) is the profit of the month with option A and \(Y\) is the profit of the month with option B, both in thousands of euros. A negative value is a loss. The founders use these two PMFs:

\(x\) 0 1 2 3 4
\(p_X(x)\) 0.05 0.25 0.45 0.15 0.10
\(y\) \(-2\) 0 2 4 8
\(p_Y(y)\) 0.10 0.10 0.20 0.50 0.10
  1. Check that both tables are valid PMFs.
  2. Calculate \(E[X]\) and \(E[Y]\).
  3. Calculate \(\operatorname{Var}(X)\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Y)\) from the definition \(E\!\left[(X-E[X])^2\right]\).
  4. Verify both variances with the computational form \(E[X^2]-\left(E[X]\right)^2\).
  5. Calculate both standard deviations and state them in euros. Compare each standard deviation with the expected profit of its option.
  6. Calculate the probability of a loss for each option.
  7. Decide which option you would choose. Give the numbers that support your choice.
Check your answer

Check your answers
  1. An event is a specified subset of the sample space.
  2. A PMF is non-negative and sums to 1 over the support.
