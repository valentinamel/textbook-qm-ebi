Lecture 2: Probability foundations and random variables

By the end of Lecture 2, you can

  • define a random experiment, outcome, sample space, and event
  • use complements, intersections, unions, and disjointness
  • state the probability rules and compute probabilities from a counts table
  • recognise a partition of the sample space
  • distinguish empirical proportions from model probabilities and from forecasts
  • say what a random variable is and state its support.

Must know

Be able to do yourself:

  • read a probability off a counts table
  • work out “not”, “and”, and “or” probabilities, with the addition rule for “or”
  • say whether two events are disjoint
  • say what a random variable is and list its possible values.

Enough to recognise:

  • the symbols \(\Omega\), \(\omega\), \(\varnothing\), \(A^c\), \(A\cap B\), \(A\cup B\), \(A\subseteq B\)
  • the three probability rules by name.

Driving question

Investors think in chances: how likely is a deal, how likely is a pitch from the show’s later, bigger years, how likely are both at once. Before we can compute any such chance, we need a precise language for “how likely”. This lecture introduces that language.

Pick one recorded pitch at random. What is the chance it reached a deal, came from a late season, or both, and what do those numbers mean beyond this file?

Start with a founder’s question

You are preparing a pitch. Before you walk in, you would like to know three things. How likely is a deal at all? Was it easier in the later seasons, when the show was better known? Answer by counting

  • A deal at all. 882 of the 1,441 pitches, 61.2%.
  • A late season. 734 of the 1,441 pitches, 50.9%.
  • Both. The bottom-right cell: 505 of 1,441, 35.0%.
  • A deal, or a late season, or both. Do not add 882 and 734. That would count the 505 pitches in the bottom-right cell twice. Count them once: 882 + 734 - 505 = 1,111 of 1,441, 77.1%. This answers the prediction from your preparation: the share is smaller than the sum of the two shares.
  • No deal. 1,441 - 882 = 559 pitches, 38.8%.

One more comparison a founder would want: among the late-season pitches, 505 of 734 got a deal (68.8%). Among the early ones, 377 of 707 did (53.3%). Lecture 4 is about comparisons of this kind.

You have just used every idea of this lecture. The rest of the lecture gives these ideas names and symbols, so that you can use them on any table and recognise them in any textbook: outcomes and events, “and”, “or”, and “not”, and the rules that say when shares may be added.

Why data analysis needs probability

The shares above describe one fixed file. Most business questions are about a larger group, a population, of which we see only a sample. A sample is one draw from the population, and another draw would give other numbers. Probability is the language for this uncertainty.

Lecture 7 shows this with the equity initially offered (equity_offered_pct) in the Shark Tank file. One random sample of 100 records gives a mean of 12.76%. There the 1,441 records play the role of the population. Their mean is 13.21%. Lectures 2 to 6 build the language of probability. Lectures 7 to 10 use it to go from a sample to a population.

A probability model begins with possible outcomes

MIT explanation (review): 01.2 Sample Space.

Probability starts by specifying exactly what may happen. A probability model needs a process, a list of complete possible results, and events that describe which results matter for the question.

A random experiment is a process whose outcome is not known in advance. The word random does not mean “unstructured.” It means the model does not specify in advance which possible outcome will occur.

Definition

Outcome, sample space, and event. An outcome, written \(\omega\), is one complete possible result of a random experiment. The sample space, written \(\Omega\), is the set of all possible outcomes. An event is a set of outcomes, so it is a part of the sample space. An event occurs when the outcome that happens belongs to it.

The table shows how these terms fit together:

Object Question it answers Shark Tank example
experiment What is done? Select one of the 1,441 records at random, each with the same chance
outcome \(\omega\) Which complete result occurred? One complete selected pitch record
sample space \(\Omega\) Which complete results were possible? The set of all 1,441 records
event \(A\) Which outcomes count as “yes” for this question? The subset of records with an on-air deal

For a single roll of a fair six-sided die,

\[\Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}.\]

The event \(E=\{2,4,6\}\) means “an even result.” The outcome \(\omega=4\) belongs to \(E\), so \(E\) occurs when 4 is rolled. The number 4 is an outcome. The set \(\{4\}\), which contains only this one outcome, is an event.

Check: is “the record with pitch_id 7” an outcome or an event?

The record itself is one complete possible result of the selection, so it is an outcome. The description “the selected pitch is the record with pitch_id 7” picks out the set that contains only that record, and a set of outcomes is an event. This is the same distinction as 4 versus \(\{4\}\) on the die.

An empirical selection experiment

Define the experiment as select one of the 1,441 recorded pitches uniformly at random. The sample space is the set of 1,441 pitch records. Define two events:

  • \(D\): the selected pitch reached an on-air deal
  • \(L\): the selected pitch appeared in a late season (Seasons 9–16).

These are the events behind the R objects D and L from the start of this lecture. Making the selection uniform means that every record has the same probability, \(1/1441\). The probability of an event then equals its share of the records in this fixed dataset, so \(D\) has probability \(882/1441\). This does not imply the same probabilities for future entrepreneurs. The box “What these numbers can and cannot say” below returns to this point.

Combining events

Events are sets of outcomes. Set notation therefore gives us a precise way to describe how events are combined.

Notation Name Meaning
\(A^c\) complement \(A\) does not occur
\(A\cap B\) intersection both \(A\) and \(B\) occur
\(A\cup B\) union \(A\), \(B\), or both occur
\(A\subseteq B\) subset every outcome in \(A\) is also in \(B\)
\(\varnothing\) empty event the event that contains no outcome, so it never occurs
\(A\cap B=\varnothing\) disjoint \(A\) and \(B\) cannot occur together

To make the notation concrete, suppose one number is selected at random from

\[\Omega=\{1,2,\ldots,12\}.\]

Define \(A=\{1,2,3,5,6,9\}\) and \(B=\{1,2,3,4\}\). Each panel of Figure 2.1 shows the same sample space. A green circle means that the outcome belongs to the event named above the panel.

Thus \(A^c=\{4,7,8,10,11,12\}\), \(A\cap B=\{1,2,3\}\), and \(A\cup B=\{1,2,3,4,5,6,9\}\). Notice that or includes outcomes that are in both events: \(1,2,3\) belong to the union as well as the intersection.

The same grammar works for business events. In the selection experiment, let \(F\) be “the selected pitch is from the Food and Beverage industry” and \(T\) “from the Technology/Software industry.” Each record lists exactly one industry, so \(F\) and \(T\) are disjoint: \(F\cap T=\varnothing\). The union \(F\cup T\) is “a food or technology pitch,” and \(F^c\) collects every pitch from any other industry. Nothing new is needed, only the sets have changed.

Disjoint does not mean independent. If two disjoint events both have positive probability, observing one makes the other impossible. Independence, introduced in Lecture 4, means that observing one event does not change the probability of the other.

Example 2.1 (Worked example: three events in words and symbols) From the table, describe each event in words and give its count.

  • \(D^c\), “no on-air deal”: the top row, \(330 + 229 = 559\) records.
  • \(D\cap L\), “deal and late season”: the single bottom-right cell, 505 records.

Optional: more rules for combining events

Four rules help to rewrite a combination of events. \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\) are any three events.

  • First distributive law: \(A\cap(B\cup C)=(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap C)\).
  • Second distributive law: \(A\cup(B\cap C)=(A\cup B)\cap(A\cup C)\).
  • First De Morgan rule: \((A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c\). “Not (\(A\) or \(B\))” is the same as “not \(A\) and not \(B\)”.
  • Second De Morgan rule: \((A\cap B)^c=A^c\cup B^c\). “Not (\(A\) and \(B\))” is the same as “not \(A\) or not \(B\)”.

In the counts table, \((D\cup L)^c=D^c\cap L^c\) is the top-left cell: the 330 early-season pitches without a deal.

Probability rules

MIT explanation (review): 01.4 Probability Axioms.

Definition

Probability. The probability of an event \(A\), written \(P(A)\), is a number between 0 and 1 that says how likely the event is. The closer the number is to 1, the more likely the event. An impossible event has probability 0, and a certain event has probability 1.

A model assigns a probability \(P(A)\) to each event \(A\). These numbers must satisfy three rules:

  1. Non-negativity: \(P(A)\ge 0\) for every event \(A\).
  2. Normalisation: \(P(\Omega)=1\).
  3. Additivity: if \(A\) and \(B\) are disjoint, then \[P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B),\] and the same holds for any finite number of events of which no two can occur together.

The first two rules set the probability scale: probability cannot be negative, and something in the sample space must occur. The third says that the probabilities of disjoint events can be added, because no outcome is counted twice.

Optional: countable additivity

In the formal theory the third rule is stated for infinite sequences of events of which no two can occur together, \(P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\right) =\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i)\). This matters for sample spaces with infinitely many outcomes. Every sample space in Lectures 2 to 5 is finite, so the finite version above is all you need there.

Write \(|A|\) for the number of outcomes in the event \(A\). For a finite sample space with equally likely outcomes,

\[P(A)=\frac{|A|}{|\Omega|}.\]

For example, \(|D|=882\) and \(|\Omega|=1441\), so \(P(D)=882/1441\approx 0.612\). That ratio is a special case, not a universal definition. It applies because equal likelihood has been built into the model: here, because the selection was made uniform.

Rules derived from the three above

MIT explanation (review): 01.5 Simple Properties of Probabilities.

Because \(A\) and \(A^c\) are disjoint and together form \(\Omega\),

\[P(A^c)=1-P(A).\]

When \(A\) and \(B\) may overlap,

\[P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B).\]

This is the addition rule. The intersection is subtracted once because it was counted in both \(P(A)\) and \(P(B)\).

Example 2.2 (Worked example: P(deal or late season), by hand) Every number comes from the counts table above. Find each one in the table as you read.

\[ P(D\cup L) =\frac{882}{1441}+\frac{734}{1441}-\frac{505}{1441} =\frac{882+734-505}{1441} =\frac{1111}{1441}\approx 0.771. \]

The 882 is the deal row total, the 734 is the late column total, and the 505 is the deal-and-late cell that both totals contain. It is subtracted once so that no record is counted twice.

This is the count from the start of the lecture, written as a rule: \(P(D\cup L)\) is smaller than \(P(D)+P(L)\) whenever the two events overlap, because the overlap \(P(D\cap L) > 0\) is subtracted.

Three more properties follow from the three rules. Each one can be read off the counts table.

  • The empty event has probability zero: \(P(\varnothing)=0\). No record is both a deal and not a deal, so \(D\cap D^c=\varnothing\) contains 0 of the 1,441 records.
  • A subset is never more likely: if \(A\subseteq B\), then \(P(A)\le P(B)\). The 505 late-season deals \(D\cap L\) are part of the 882 deals \(D\), and \(505/1441\approx 0.350\) is below \(882/1441\approx 0.612\).
  • An event can be split into two parts: \(P(A)=P(A\cap B)+P(A\cap B^c)\). The 882 deals are the 505 late-season deals plus the 377 early-season deals, so \(P(D)=P(D\cap L)+P(D\cap L^c)=505/1441+377/1441=882/1441\). Lecture 4 uses this property again.

Partitions

A partition divides \(\Omega\) into events that do not overlap and together include every possible outcome. Each outcome belongs to exactly one part. For example, Seasons 1–8 and Seasons 9–16 partition the recorded pitches into an early-period event \(L^c\) and a late-period event \(L\) (Figure 2.2).

From complete outcomes to numerical measurements

MIT explanation (review): 05.2 Definition of Random Variables.

An outcome is a complete result, but an analysis usually focuses on one number that describes that result.

Definition

Random variable. A number whose value depends on the outcome of a random experiment. A fixed rule turns each outcome into a number.

Random variables are written with capital letters, such as \(X\). Their values are written with lower-case letters, such as \(x\).

Three random variables on our selection experiment:

  • the deal indicator \(X\): equal to 1 when the selected pitch reached an on-air deal and 0 otherwise
  • the season \(S\): the season number of the selected pitch
  • the word count \(W\): the number of words in its written description.

The rule is fixed before an outcome is selected. The value is unknown until then. That is the only sense in which the variable is “random.” After selection, the number produced, written in lower case, \(x\), \(s\), or \(w\), is its realised value.

A random variable is discrete when its possible values can be listed. A random variable is continuous when it can take every value in an interval, for example a waiting time. Lecture 6 treats continuous random variables.

The support of a discrete random variable is the set of values it can take with positive probability. The support of the deal indicator is \(\{0,1\}\). The support of season is \(\{1,2,\ldots,16\}\). Lecture 3 attaches a probability to every value in the support.

Optional: the formal definition of a random variable

Formally, a random variable is a function from the sample space to the real numbers, \(X:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\). Read left to right: \(X\) takes an outcome \(\omega\) from \(\Omega\) and returns the number \(X(\omega)\). For the deal indicator, \(X(\omega)=1\) when \(\omega\in D\) and \(X(\omega)=0\) otherwise, so the event \(D\) and the variable \(X\) are linked by \(D=\{\omega : X(\omega)=1\}\). The table lists the symbols:

Symbol Meaning
\(\omega\) one complete outcome, such as one selected pitch record
\(X\) the fixed measurement rule
\(X(\omega)\) the number assigned by \(X\) to outcome \(\omega\)
\(x\) a possible or realised numerical value of \(X\)

What these numbers can and cannot say

  • \(P(D)=882/1441\approx 0.612\) is exact for one question only: the chance of a deal when one record is drawn at random from this file. It is an empirical proportion.
  • It becomes a forecast for a future pitch under four assumptions: the future pitch belongs to the same kind of pitches, the producers select in the same way, the process stays stable over time, and “deal” keeps its meaning.
  • It says nothing about completed investments, which the file does not record.

What you can do with it: use 61% as a first guess for a pitch on the show, and adjust it when you know more about the pitch. Lecture 4 shows how.

Answering the driving question

For one uniformly selected record: the chance it reached a deal is \(P(D)\approx 0.612\), the chance it came from a late season is \(P(L)\approx 0.509\), the chance of both is \(P(D\cap L)\approx 0.350\), and the chance of at least one is \(P(D\cup L)\approx 0.771\). Beyond this file, those numbers describe future pitches only under the four assumptions in the box “What these numbers can and cannot say”.

A founder’s decision (invented example)

Your order records show three shares among your customers: 12% had a late delivery, 8% gave a low rating, and 3% had both. You want to send a personal message to every customer with at least one of these problems. The addition rule gives the share: 12% + 8% - 3% = 17%. With 400 customers that is 68 messages, not the 80 you would budget by adding the two shares. The decision: 68 messages fit into one evening, so you send them this week.

Next: We can now attach a probability to a single event, deal or no deal, late or early. Most business quantities have many possible values: which season, how many words, how many units sold. Lecture 3 moves from single events to the whole distribution of a random variable, which is a probability for every possible value. It also introduces two numbers that summarise a distribution: the expected value and the variance.

  1. \(P(D^c)=1-882/1441=559/1441\approx0.388\): 38.8% of the records did not reach an on-air agreement.
  2. The event leaves out only the records that are both deals and late season, so \(P(D^c\cup L^c)=1-P(D\cap L)=1-505/1441=936/1441\approx0.650\): 65.0% of the records did not reach an on-air deal, came from Seasons 1–8, or both.   3. They are disjoint because a record cannot simultaneously have and not have an on-air deal.
  4. \(S\) records the season number of the selected pitch record. Its support is \(\{1,2,\ldots,16\}\).
  5. \(X\) equals 1 when the selected pitch is in \(D\) and 0 otherwise. \(D\) is a set of outcomes. \(X\) is a measurement rule that assigns a number to every outcome. \(D\) is the set of outcomes for which \(X\) equals 1.

Review: one question from Lecture 1 (5 minutes)

A student concludes: “Our class survey had 180 responses, so its average risk score describes Dutch first-year students well. With that many responses, chance variation is small.” Which problem does the size of the sample not fix?

Check your answer

Selection bias. A larger sample shrinks sampling variability, but the class is a convenience sample of one programme, not a random sample of Dutch first-year students. More responses from the same selected group do not make it more representative.

Worked exam interpretation (10 minutes)

Question. R reports mean(sharks$deal_on_show) = 0.612. Is this an empirical proportion or a universal probability?

Check a model answer

It is first an empirical proportion: 882 of the 1,441 recorded pitches reached an on-air agreement. It is also the event probability in a model that selects one of those 1,441 records uniformly. Applying it to a future entrepreneur requires additional population, selection, stability, and outcome assumptions.
