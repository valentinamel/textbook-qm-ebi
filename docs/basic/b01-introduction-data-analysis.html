Lecture 1: Introduction to Data Analysis

By the end of Lecture 1, you can

  • organise a reproducible R analysis and verify AI-assisted work;
  • distinguish categorical, discrete, continuous, and identifier variables;
  • identify a sample, a target population, sampling variability, and selection bias;
  • inspect, validate, and qualify a real dataset;
  • describe one variable with frequency tables, bar charts, and histograms;
  • read a histogram by hand, including locating the median interval from cumulative bar counts.

Driving question

Shark Tank turns a business decision — fund this venture or not — into recorded data: 1,441 pitches, each with an ask, an equity offer, and an outcome. Learning from records like these is how investors, founders, and analysts figure out what works.

1,441 televised pitches are on our screen. What can this dataset tell us about entrepreneurship — and what can it never tell us?

Data analysis with R and AI

Reproducible scripts

A reproducible script recreates the analysis in a new R session, without depending on objects created earlier at the Console.

Use these habits from the start:

  • open the project rather than changing the working directory with setwd();
  • keep downloaded data unchanged;
  • use relative paths such as data/shark_tank_teaching.csv;
  • record importing, checking, transforming, plotting, and modelling in scripts;
  • give objects meaningful names;
  • keep code and written interpretation together; and
  • restart R and run the complete script before sharing the analysis.

The analysis cycle

Data analysis is iterative:

  1. Ask a focused question.
  2. Define the unit and decide how each idea will be measured.
  3. Obtain or create the data and document their provenance.
  4. Inspect and validate before calculating.
  5. Summarise and visualise patterns.
  6. Interpret the result in context and state its limits.

Use AI as a fallible assistant

AI systems generate plausible responses; they do not certify that a calculation, source, or interpretation is correct. Use:

Attempt → Ask → Verify → Explain

  1. Attempt the question and identify where you are stuck.
  2. Ask for a hint, diagnostic question, counterexample, or code review.
  3. Verify every claim against the data, R output, course notation, and reliable sources.
  4. Explain the result in your own words and ask for feedback on that explanation.

Do not share confidential, personal, or assessment-restricted material. You remain responsible for every submitted claim.

Collecting our own data

A choice with money at risk

Imagine receiving EUR 100 and making one choice:

Choice Amount placed at risk Possible final amounts
Keep it EUR 0 EUR 100 for certain
Split it EUR 50 EUR 50 or EUR 150, each with 50% chance
Go all-in EUR 100 EUR 0 or EUR 200, each with 50% chance

All three choices have the same average monetary payoff across many repetitions: EUR 100. The options differ in how uncertain the one-time result is.

Next, answer:

How do you see yourself in general? Use 0 for “not at all willing to take risks” and 10 for “very willing to take risks.”

Finally, give your current belief:

What is the probability that you will start or co-found a business at least once during the next ten years?

Before the class map appears on screen, predict its shape: will students who place more money at risk also report a higher founder probability? The map places general risk willingness on the horizontal axis and the subjective founder probability on the vertical axis. One anonymous point represents one responding student.

From ideas to variables

Variable What is recorded Type
respondent_id anonymous row label identifier
amount_at_risk 0, 50, or 100 euros discrete quantitative
risk_willingness one selected point from 0 to 10 ordered discrete rating
founder_probability belief from 0% to 100% continuous concept recorded on a finite-precision slider

Risk attitude is a theoretical construct. The monetary choice and the 0–10 response are two imperfect measurements of it. They may disagree because risk taking depends on context, hypothetical choices differ from real stakes, and self-perceptions are imperfect. A single response is not a diagnosis of a permanent personality type.

The first two responses offer separate allowable points. Founder probability is conceptualised on the continuum from 0% to 100%, although the software stores a rounded value.

Sample and target population

The sample consists of students who are present and respond. Plausible target populations include:

  • the responding students;
  • everyone attending this lecture;
  • all students enrolled in QM Basic;
  • all first-year business students in the Netherlands; or
  • all young adults in the Netherlands.
Check: which of the five candidate populations can our responses describe directly?

Only the first: the responding students. Everyone else — absent classmates, other QM Basic students, Dutch first-years, young adults — enters the picture only through assumptions, because attendance and participation were not random. The data describe the respondents; they do not automatically represent wider groups.

Sampling variability versus selection bias

Two uncertainties must not be confused:

  • Sampling variability: even a genuinely random sample can contain an unusual number of highly risk-tolerant students by chance. Larger random samples usually reduce this chance variation.
  • Selection bias: a convenience sample can systematically exclude certain people. Collecting more responses from the same selected group does not necessarily repair that bias.

Example 1.1 (Worked example: two scenarios, classified aloud) Scenario 1. A researcher draws 40 students at random from the full programme enrolment list. By chance, the sample contains an unusually large number of students who chose “go all-in.”

Scenario 2. A survey about founding a business is shared in the group chat of the entrepreneurship club, and 400 members respond.

Scenario 1 is sampling variability: the selection mechanism was random, so the oddity is chance variation, and a larger random sample would usually tame it. Scenario 2 is selection bias: club members are systematically more interested in founding than the average student, and collecting 4,000 responses from the same chat would not repair the distortion — it would make a biased estimate more precise.

A probability sample of Dutch students would require a defined population, a suitable sampling frame, and random selection. Non-response and measurement error could still remain.

Meet the Shark Tank data

The first block used data the class created; this block turns to the data the whole course runs on.

Context and unit of observation

Shark Tank US is a television programme in which entrepreneurs present a venture or product to a panel of investors known as sharks. Entrepreneurs may ask for money in exchange for an ownership share. The investors question them, negotiate, and may announce a deal during the episode.

The recorded outcome is an on-air agreement. It does not establish that an investment was completed or that the venture later succeeded.

The teaching file contains 1,441 televised pitches from completed Seasons 1–16 and 19 variables. The table below introduces the variables used most often in this course.

Variable Meaning Type
pitch_id row identifier identifier
startup_name venture or product name categorical label
deal_on_show 1 for an on-air agreement; 0 otherwise binary categorical, stored as 0/1
season television season, 1–16 ordered discrete/time index
episode episode number within season discrete identifier within season
industry source industry category categorical
description_words words in the written dataset description discrete quantitative count
pitcher_gender_group all_women, all_men, mixed, or unknown, based on the source classification categorical
women_represented 1 for all_women or mixed; 0 for all_men; missing for unknown binary categorical, stored as 0/1
multiple_entrepreneurs source indicator for more than one entrepreneur binary categorical, with missing values
ask_amount_usd amount requested quantitative, USD
equity_offered_pct ownership percentage initially offered quantitative, percentage points
requested_valuation_usd valuation implied by the initial ask quantitative, USD
deal_amount_usd, deal_equity_pct, deal_valuation_usd recorded deal terms quantitative; structurally missing without a deal

Gender describes the people presenting the pitch, not necessarily every founder or employee. women_represented is not the share of women: the source does not report the presenter counts needed to calculate that share for mixed teams. Names were not used to infer gender.

Note carefully what description_words measures: the length of the written description in the source dataset, not anything about the pitch itself. It counts words typed by whoever compiled the file, not words spoken in the tank. The file also does not measure the spoken pitch, completed investment, or later venture performance.

Reading a histogram by hand

The exam gives you a printed histogram and no computer, so this skill is practised by hand. Every graph in this course is read with the same five-step sequence:

  1. Unit and variables. What does one observation represent, and what is on each axis, in which units?
  2. Scales. Read the axis scales, including where they start and how wide each interval is.
  3. One element. Put one bar (or point) into a full sentence.
  4. Pattern. Describe the overall shape: where values concentrate, whether the distribution is symmetric or skewed, whether there are outliers.
  5. Limits. State one thing the graph cannot tell you.

Example 1.2 (Worked example: the five steps and the median interval) Apply the sequence to Figure 1.3.

  1. One observation is one recorded pitch; the horizontal axis is equity initially offered in percentage points, the vertical axis is the number of pitches.
  2. The horizontal axis runs from 0 to 100 in intervals of width 5; the vertical axis counts pitches.
  3. The tallest bar covers 5–10%: 560 pitches offered more than 5% and at most 10% equity.
  4. The distribution is strongly right-skewed: most offers are at or below 20%, with a thin tail stretching towards 100%.
  5. The histogram cannot say why offers cluster there, and it hides the exact values inside each interval.

Locating the median interval. With 1,441 ordered observations, the median is the 721st value. Add bar counts from the left until the running total reaches 721:

Interval (%) Count Cumulative count
0–5 293 293
5–10 560 853
10–15 194 1,047
15–20 241 1,288

After the first bar only 293 observations are covered — not yet 721. After the second bar the running total is \(293+560=853\ge721\), so the 721st observation lies in the 5–10% interval: the median interval. (R confirms: the exact median is 10%, which this histogram counts in the 5–10 bar because each bar includes its right endpoint.) The same cumulative-count reading is tested in Mock Exam Question 1.

Try it: locate the upper-quartile interval

In class this is the pair task. The upper quartile is the value with three-quarters of the observations at or below it.

Step 1 (given). Three-quarters of 1,441 observations is about 1,081, so you are looking for the interval containing the 1,081st ordered value.

Step 2 (you). Extend the cumulative counts in the table above until the running total first reaches 1,081.

Step 3 (you). Name the upper-quartile interval.

Check your answer

Through 10–15% the cumulative count is \(293+560+194=1{,}047\), still below 1,081. Adding the 15–20% bar gives \(1{,}047+241=1{,}288\ge1{,}081\), so the upper quartile lies in the 15–20% interval. (R confirms: quantile(sharks$equity_offered_pct, 0.75) returns 20, on the upper edge of that bar.)

What these records can and cannot say

The Shark Tank file describes 1,441 recorded televised pitches, nothing more, for three structural reasons:

  • Selection into television. Producers choose which pitches are filmed and aired. The file is not a random sample of startups, entrepreneurs, or even Shark Tank applicants.
  • On-air deal is not investment. deal_on_show records an on-air agreement. It does not establish that money changed hands or that the venture later succeeded.
  • Measured is not the same as meant. description_words counts words in the source’s written description, not the spoken pitch; women_represented is a category, not a share; the file does not measure later performance at all.

The same discipline applies to the class dataset: it describes the students who responded, and a bigger convenience sample would not make it describe Dutch students in general.

Answering the driving question

The dataset can tell us, precisely and reproducibly, what happened in these 1,441 recorded pitches: 882 of them (61.2%) reached an on-air agreement, most initial offers gave up at most 20% equity with a median in the 5–10% interval, and mean offers drifted downward across seasons. It can never tell us, by itself, what would happen to a new entrepreneur, whether an on-air agreement became a completed investment, or why any of these patterns arose — those questions need assumptions about selection, measurement, and causality that the file cannot supply.

Next: We can now describe what happened in these 1,441 pitches: counts, shares, and shapes. But description alone cannot answer the chance questions an investor actually asks — how likely is a deal if I pick a pitch at random, how likely is a late-season pitch, how likely are both at once. Lecture 2 builds the tool for exactly such questions, a probability model: select one record at random so that every pitch is equally likely, define events such as “reached a deal” and “late season”, and compute how likely each is — with rules that say when chances may simply be added.

Worked exam interpretation (5 minutes)

Question (mirrors Mock Exam Q1). A startup accelerator records, for each of 240 applications: funding_requested_k (euros, in thousands), team_size, sector (four categories), and accepted (0/1). Classify each variable. A histogram of funding_requested_k has equal-width intervals of EUR 20,000 with counts 58, 88, 54, 24, 10, 6. Which interval contains the median?

Check a model answer

funding_requested_k is quantitative continuous; team_size is quantitative discrete; sector is categorical; accepted is categorical binary, even though it is coded 0/1. With 240 observations, the median lies between the 120th and 121st ordered values. Cumulative counts: 58 below EUR 20,000, then \(58+88=146\) below EUR 40,000 — so observations 120 and 121, and hence the median, lie in the EUR 20,000–40,000 interval.

From the reading ladder (10 minutes)

This course trains you to read real research, one bounded piece at a time — the reading ladder explains the plan and the six-question protocol. First rung: open Ajzen (1991), the Theory of Planned Behavior via the university library and read only the title and abstract. List two theoretical constructs the theory uses (for example attitude toward the behaviour, subjective norm, perceived behavioural control, intention), and for each propose one concrete measurable variable you could actually record from a survey of E&BI students — then classify each proposed variable as categorical, discrete quantitative, or continuous quantitative.

Check an example answer

The construct entrepreneurial intention could be measured as the answer to “do you plan to start a business within five years?” — a categorical (binary) variable; the construct attitude toward entrepreneurship could be measured as agreement with “running my own business appeals to me” on a 1–7 scale — a discrete quantitative measurement of a construct no single question fully captures. The gap between the construct and the variable is exactly the imperfect-measurement point from today’s lecture.
