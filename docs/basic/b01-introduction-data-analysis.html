Lecture 1: Introduction to data analysis

By the end of Lecture 1, you can

  • organise a reproducible R analysis and check work done with AI help,
  • tell types of variables apart and spot selection bias in a sample,
  • inspect a real dataset and describe one variable with tables and charts,
  • read a histogram and locate the interval holding the median.

Driving question

Every decision and every event leaves a trace in data. Such data can help a founder learn what works and what does not. Before you trust any lesson from data, you need to get to know the data: what they record, who is in them, and who is missing.

The Shark Tank file records 1,441 televised pitches. What can this dataset tell us about entrepreneurship, and what can it never tell us?

Read before class: working reproducibly

Reproducible scripts

A reproducible script recreates the analysis in a new R session. It does not need objects that you created earlier at the Console.

Use these habits from the start:

  • open the project rather than changing the working directory with setwd(),
  • keep downloaded data unchanged,
  • use relative paths such as data/shark_tank_teaching.csv,
  • write every step (importing, checking, transforming, plotting, and modelling) in scripts,
  • give objects meaningful names,
  • write what a result means as a comment (a line that starts with #) directly below the code that produced it,
  • restart R and run the complete script before you share the analysis.

The analysis cycle

Data analysis moves through six steps, and you often go back to an earlier step:

  1. Ask a focused question.
  2. Define the unit (what one row of the data stands for) and decide how to measure each idea.
  3. Obtain or create the data and write down where they come from.
  4. Inspect and check the data before calculating.
  5. Summarise and visualise patterns.
  6. Interpret the result in context and state its limits.

Going back is normal. A check in step 4 can show that a variable does not measure what the question needs, and you return to step 2.

Read before class: AI as a fallible assistant

You will often use an AI assistant for the code and interpretations in this course, so one habit belongs to the set-up: check what it tells you. AI systems write answers that sound right. They do not guarantee that a calculation, source, or interpretation is correct. The reason lies in how they work. An AI assistant is a language model that works with probabilities. It writes, word by word, what is likely to come next, given the texts it learned from. A likely answer is not always a correct answer. So where there is probability, there is uncertainty and room for error. To check an answer, use four steps, Attempt, Ask, Verify, Explain:

  1. Attempt the question and identify where you are stuck.
  2. Ask for a hint, a question that helps you find the problem, a counterexample, or a review of your code.
  3. Verify every claim against the data, R output, course notation, and reliable sources.
  4. Explain the result in your own words and ask for feedback on that explanation.

Do not share confidential or personal material, or exam material that you may not share. You stay responsible for every claim that you hand in.

Lecture material

This section shows how a question becomes data, how to check a dataset, and how to describe one variable. It uses two datasets: a short class survey that we collect ourselves and the ready-to-use Shark Tank file.

Start with a founder’s question

How many students in your year want to start a business in the next five years? And which attitudes towards risk do they have? A short survey can collect the answers. Founders ask the same kind of question about their market every week: how many people want this product, who they are, and how do I know? This lecture shows how such a question becomes data, and what the data can and cannot tell you.

Collecting our own data

Before we work with a ready-made dataset, we create one ourselves. During the lecture, the class answers a short anonymous survey. Our answers then form a small dataset: a projection of a few characteristics of the people in the room onto a table or a graph. Seeing how our own answers turn into rows and columns shows what any dataset is, and also what it leaves out. The survey has three questions.

The first question is:

You receive €100. Which option do you choose?

Option Amount placed at risk Possible final amounts
Keep €0 €100 for certain
Split €50 €50 or €150, with a 50% chance each
All-in €100 €0 or €200, with a 50% chance each

All three options have the same average payoff if the choice is repeated many times: €100. The options differ only in how uncertain the result of a single choice is. In other words, they differ in the spread of the possible outcomes around that average. Lecture 3 measures this spread with the variance, which is a central tool for judging the risk of an investment or a business plan.

The second question is:

How willing are you to take risks in general? (1 = not at all, 5 = very willing)

You answer it by choosing one of five separate values: 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5. No answer can lie between two values, so the answer is a discrete variable.

The third question asks for your current belief:

What is the probability you will start or co-found a business in the next five years?

You answer it with a percentage from 0% to 100%. Any value in this range is possible, such as 37.5%. The answer is a continuous variable. The second and third questions show the difference between a discrete variable, with separate values, and a continuous variable, whose values can lie anywhere in a range. The section below defines both types.

The answers of the class are analysed together during the lecture.

From ideas to variables

The survey turns three ideas into data. Five terms describe how this works.

Definition

Characteristic. The feature we want to know about. Example: a student’s risk attitude.

Variable. A well-defined rule for measuring a characteristic. Example: the amount of €0, €50, or €100 that a student puts at risk (amount_at_risk).

Unit (observational unit). One member of the group we study. Example: one student.

Value. One possible result of the measurement. Example: the variable amount_at_risk has the three values 0, 50, and 100.

Observation. The value recorded for one unit. Example: the observation for one student may be 50.

In a data table, every row is one unit and every column is one variable. In formulas, from Lecture 2 on, a variable is written with a capital letter, such as \(X\), and its values with the matching lower-case letter, \(x\). Here \(X\) stands for the rule of measurement, which is the same for every unit, and \(x\) for a number that the rule produced for one unit.

Variables come in different types. The type determines which tables, graphs, and calculations make sense.

Definition

Types of variables. The examples are from the Shark Tank file and the class survey.

Categorical. The values are groups. There are two kinds.

  • Nominal. The groups have no order. Example: industry. With only two groups the variable is called binary and is often stored as 0 and 1, as in deal_on_show.
  • Ordinal. The groups have an order, but there are no numerical distances between them. Examples: the stage of a venture, with the worded groups idea, prototype, and first sales, or an answer scale from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”. The Shark Tank file has no ordinal variable.

Numerical. The values are numbers that count or measure something. There are two kinds.

  • Discrete. The values are separate numbers, often counts. Examples: number_sharks_in_deal, and the answer from 1 to 5 to the second survey question (risk_willingness).
  • Continuous. The values can lie anywhere in a range. Examples: equity_offered_pct, and the probability of founding a business from the third survey question (founder_probability), which can take any value from 0% to 100%.

Identifier. The value only labels a unit. Calculating with it has no meaning. Example: pitch_id.

Rating scales such as the answer from 1 to 5 are sometimes treated as ordinal, because the step from 1 to 2 need not mean the same as the step from 4 to 5. In this course we treat such answers as discrete numerical variables. The class survey has the following variables:

Variable What is recorded Type
respondent_id anonymous row label identifier
amount_at_risk €0, €50, or €100 numerical, discrete
risk_willingness one selected value from 1 to 5 numerical, discrete
founder_probability belief from 0% to 100% numerical, continuous

Risk attitude is an idea that cannot be observed directly. The money choice and the answer from 1 to 5 are two imperfect measurements of it. The two measurements may disagree for three reasons. Risk taking depends on the situation. An imagined choice differs from a choice with real money. Also, people do not judge themselves perfectly. One answer does not show a fixed personality type.

Sample and target population

Data almost never cover everyone we want to know about. Three terms separate the units that we observed from the units that the question is about.

Definition

Population. The complete set of units about which a question is asked.

Sample. The part of the population that is observed.

Random sample. A sample drawn so that every unit of the population has the same chance of being drawn.

A population is usually too large to measure every unit, so we work with a sample. Inferential statistics asks what a sample can tell us about the population. Lectures 7 to 10 cover it.

In the class survey, the sample consists of the students who are present and respond. The population we want to learn about is called the target population. It could be any of these five groups:

  • the responding students,
  • everyone attending the lecture,
  • all students enrolled in QM Basic,
  • all first-year business students in the Netherlands,
  • all young adults in the Netherlands.
Check: which of the five possible populations can our responses describe directly?

Only the first: the responding students. For the other groups (absent classmates, other QM Basic students, Dutch first-year students, young adults) we would need assumptions, because attendance and participation were not random. The data describe the respondents. They do not automatically represent larger groups.

Sampling variability versus selection bias

A sample can differ from its population for two reasons. Do not mix them up:

  • Sampling variability: even a random sample can contain an unusual number of students who are very willing to take risks, purely by chance. Larger random samples usually reduce this chance variation.
  • Selection bias: a sample of people who were easy to reach can systematically leave out certain people. Collecting more responses from the same selected group does not remove that bias.

Example 1.1 (Worked example: two scenarios) Scenario 1. A researcher draws 40 students at random from the list of all students enrolled in the programme. By chance, the sample contains an unusually large number of students who chose “All-in”.

Scenario 2. A student shares a survey about founding a business in the group chat of the entrepreneurship club, and 400 members respond.

Scenario 1 is sampling variability. The selection was random, so the unusual result is chance variation, and a larger random sample would usually reduce it. Scenario 2 is selection bias. Club members are systematically more interested in founding than the average student. Collecting 4,000 responses from the same chat would not remove this bias. It would only give a more precise answer to the wrong question.

A random sample of Dutch students would require a clear definition of the population, a list of all its members, and random selection from that list. Even then, some students would not answer, and answers can be imprecise.

Meet the Shark Tank data

Shark Tank data The class survey showed how a small dataset is created. Most data that founders and analysts use were collected by somebody else, and they need the same questions: what is one unit, what was measured, and who was selected. The rest of the course uses one larger dataset of this kind: the Shark Tank file. Examples and tasks that use the Shark Tank file carry this tag: Shark Tank data.

Context and unit of observation

Shark Tank US is a television programme in which entrepreneurs present a venture or product to a panel of investors known as sharks. Entrepreneurs may ask for money in exchange for an ownership share. The investors question them, negotiate, and may announce a deal during the episode.

The recorded outcome is an on-air deal: the sharks and the entrepreneurs agree on a deal in front of the camera. It does not show that an investment was completed or that the venture later succeeded.

For a founder, the most interesting numbers in the file are valuations. A valuation is the value of the whole company that a request or a deal implies. Asking $100,000 for 10% of a company values it at $1 million. The file stores the valuation implied by the founder’s request in requested_valuation_usd. For pitches that ended in a deal, it stores the valuation implied by the agreed deal in deal_valuation_usd. The section Claimed and agreed valuations below compares the two.

The teaching file contains 1,441 televised pitches from completed Seasons 1 to 16 and 19 variables. The Before class section lists all 19. The table below repeats the five that this lecture uses most.

Variable Meaning Type
deal_on_show 1 for an on-air deal, 0 otherwise categorical, nominal with two groups (binary), stored as 0/1
season television season, 1 to 16 numerical, discrete, ordered in time
description_words number of words in the written description numerical, discrete (a count)
ask_amount_usd amount of money requested numerical, continuous, in USD
equity_offered_pct ownership share initially offered numerical, continuous, in percent

The gender variables describe the people who present the pitch, not necessarily every founder or employee. women_represented is not the share of women. For mixed teams, the source does not report how many women and how many men presented, so nobody can calculate that share.

Note carefully what description_words measures: the length of the written description in the source dataset, not anything about the pitch itself. It counts words typed by the person who created the source dataset, not words spoken on the show. The file also does not measure the spoken pitch, whether the investment was completed, or how the venture performed later.

Summarise in context

The first summary answers a simple question: how often did a pitch end in a deal? Two terms are needed.

Definition

Proportion. A count divided by the total. Multiplied by 100, it is a percentage.

Mean (average). The sum of all values divided by the number of values. For a variable stored as 0 and 1, the mean equals the proportion of 1s. Example: the four values 1, 0, 1, 1 have mean \(3/4=0.75\), and three of the four values are 1s.

The code counts the deals, counts the pitches, and computes the mean of deal_on_show.

deal_count <- sum(sharks$deal_on_show)
pitch_count <- nrow(sharks)
deal_rate <- mean(sharks$deal_on_show)

c(
  on_air_deals = deal_count,
  recorded_pitches = pitch_count,
  recorded_deal_rate = deal_rate
)
      on_air_deals   recorded_pitches recorded_deal_rate 
       882.0000000       1441.0000000          0.6120749

Here 882 of the 1,441 pitches reached a deal, and 882/1441 = 0.612, or 61.2%. Because deal_on_show is stored as 0 and 1, its mean returns the same 0.612. So roughly three in every five recorded pitches ended with a deal on air. A careful sentence is:

In this dataset, 882 of 1,441 recorded pitches (61.2%) reached an on-air deal.

This sentence names the dataset and outcome. It does not claim that a randomly selected entrepreneur has the same chance of a completed investment.

Describing one variable

Shark Tank data Before comparing groups or fitting models, learn to describe one variable well. The right table or graph depends on the variable’s type.

Definition

Empirical distribution. How often each value, or each range of values, occurs in a dataset.

Frequency table. A table with the count of every value of a categorical or discrete variable.

Relative-frequency table. The same table with proportions instead of counts. The proportions add up to 1.

Bar chart. One bar per value of a categorical or discrete variable. The height of a bar is its count or proportion.

Histogram. The graph for a numerical variable. It groups nearby values into intervals of equal width and draws one bar per interval. The height of a bar is the number of observations in that interval.

A shape like this, with most values small and a long tail to the right, is called right-skewed. Changing the interval width can show or hide patterns, so the analyst must choose the width with care.

The dashed line marks the median of the offers. The median is a typical value that is not pulled up by the few very large offers in the right tail.

Definition

Median. The middle value of the ordered data: at least half of the observations are at or below it and at least half are at or above it.

To find the median, put the values in order. With an odd number of observations the median is the single middle value. With an even number it is the average of the two middle values. Two small examples show both cases:

  • Five founders offer 10%, 5%, 25%, 8%, and 10%. In order: 5, 8, 10, 10, 25. The third value is in the middle, so the median is 10%.
  • Four founders offer 20%, 5%, 15%, and 8%. In order: 5, 8, 15, 20. The two middle values are 8 and 15, so the median is \((8+15)/2=11.5\%\).

R does the same for the 1,441 offers with median(sharks$equity_offered_pct). It orders the values and takes the 721st, because 720 values lie on each side of it. The result is 10%. So half of the founders offered 10% of their company or less. Many founders chose exactly this round number: 474 of the 1,441 pitches offered exactly 10%. For this reason, more than half of the offers lie at or below the median: 59% (853 of 1,441). This is why the definition says “at least half”.

How to read a graph

The bar chart and the histogram above are the first of many graphs in this course. Read every graph, in this lecture and later, in the same five steps:

  1. Unit and variables. What does one observation represent, and what is on each axis, in which units?
  2. Scales. Read the axis scales: where they start, how wide each interval is, and whether the vertical axis shows a count or a proportion.
  3. One element. Describe one bar, point, or line in a full sentence.
  4. Pattern. Describe the overall shape: where most values lie, whether the distribution is symmetric or skewed, whether there are outliers (single values far away from the rest).
  5. Limits. State one thing the graph cannot tell you.

Reading a histogram

Shark Tank data A printed histogram also shows where the middle of the data lies and how widely the data are spread, even without a computer. Besides the median, three more terms describe this.

Definition

Lower quartile \(Q_1\) and upper quartile \(Q_3\). At least one quarter of the observations are at or below \(Q_1\), and at least three quarters are at or above it. At least three quarters of the observations are at or below \(Q_3\), and at least one quarter are at or above it.

Interquartile range. \(\operatorname{IQR}=Q_3-Q_1\), the width of the middle half of the data.

The median lies between the two quartiles: it splits the data in half, and the quartiles split each half again. quantile() computes the values at or below which a given share of the data lies, here a quarter, a half, and three quarters. IQR() computes the interquartile range for the equity initially offered (equity_offered_pct):

The lower quartile is 8%, the median is 10%, and the upper quartile is 20%. The interquartile range is \(20-8=12\) percentage points: the middle half of the offers lies between 8% and 20% equity.

Many pitches offer exactly 10% or exactly 20%. For this reason 59% of the offers (853 of 1,441) are at or below the median, and 89% (1,288 of 1,441) are at or below \(Q_3\). The table in the worked example below shows these counts.

A simpler measure of spread is the range: the largest value minus the smallest value.

What these records can and cannot say

  • The file describes 1,441 pitches that producers chose to film and air. It is not a sample of all start-ups, or even of all applicants.
  • An on-air deal is made in front of the camera. It is not a completed investment, and it says nothing about later success.
  • A variable measures only what it records. description_words counts words in a written description, not anything about the pitch itself.

This answers the prediction from your preparation. What we learn from the file applies to these televised pitches. It does not automatically apply to any pitch or any start-up, because the producers selected the pitches, just as a survey in a club chat selects its respondents.

A causal claim says that changing one thing would change another, for example that offering less equity would get a founder a deal. Records like these show what went together. They do not show what would happen after a change.

What you can do with it: describe what happened in these pitches and form expectations about what happens on the show. For claims about founders in general, look for data that were collected for that purpose.

Answering the driving question

The driving question asked what the Shark Tank file can tell us about entrepreneurship, and what it can never tell us.

The file can tell us precisely what happened in 1,441 recorded pitches, and anyone can repeat the calculation. Of these pitches, 882 (61.2%) reached an on-air deal. Half of the founders offered 10% of their company or less, and the middle half offered between 8% and 20%. In the deals with an ownership share, the median agreed valuation ($1.0 million) was half of the median claimed valuation ($2.0 million), and 81% of these founders had to accept a lower valuation than they asked for.

Such numbers help a decision. As a founder preparing a pitch for the show, you might expect about three chances in five of a deal on air, and you should prepare for a valuation well below your own claim. These expectations hold under the assumption that your pitch resembles the pitches that producers selected for Seasons 1 to 16, and that the show still works in the same way.

The file can never tell us three things. It cannot describe start-ups in general, because producers selected the pitches. It cannot say whether a deal on air was completed or whether the venture succeeded later, because these outcomes were not measured. And it cannot say why the patterns exist, for example whether a lower offer causes a deal. The class survey has the same kind of limit: it describes the students who answered, not the whole year. The founder in the box below meets this problem with 40 friends.

A founder’s decision (invented example)

You plan a venture that sells boxes of prepared meals on campus. You ask 40 friends whether they would buy such a box, and 30 of them say yes, which is 75%. You are about to order packaging for a market in which 75% of students buy. Should you plan with 75%?

Try it yourself first.

  1. Who is in your sample, and which group do you want to know about?
  2. Who chose to answer, and in which direction could this push the 75%?
  3. Propose a better sample that you can collect in one lunch break.
See the solution
  1. The sample is 40 of your friends. The group you want to know about is all students on campus, your market. Friends are not a typical part of it, because they self-selected into this network.
  2. Your friends chose to answer, and they like you. They are more likely to say yes than other students. The share among all students is probably lower than 75%.
  3. Ask 50 students you do not know, for example every tenth person who leaves the canteen. This sample is still not random, because it misses students who do not eat there. It is much closer to your market than your friends, though.

The decision: do not order packaging for 75%. Spend one lunch break on the better sample and plan with that share.

Next: We can now describe what happened in these 1,441 pitches: counts, shares, and shapes. Description alone cannot answer the questions about chance that an investor asks. How likely is a deal if I pick a pitch at random? How likely is a late-season pitch? How likely are both at once? Lecture 2 starts with probability theory, the language to describe such uncertainty, whether it comes from a pitch drawn at random or from an answer written by an AI assistant. It builds a probability model that selects one pitch at random, so that every pitch has the same chance. The model defines events such as “reached a deal” and “late season”. Its rules say how likely each event is and when you may add chances.
